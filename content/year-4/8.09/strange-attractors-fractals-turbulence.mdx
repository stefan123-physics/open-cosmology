Maps showed chaos in its simplest form in the [previous section](/courses/8-09/chaos-logistic-map). The last section of Stewart's notes returns to differential equations, now with the three variables that chaos needs. It covers the Lorenz equations and their strange attractor, the Lyapunov exponents of a flow, fractals and how to give them a dimension, and finally chaos and turbulence in fluids. (Citations give the printed page numbers 189 to 195 of the Chapter 7 notes. On this page $\lambda_j$ are Lyapunov exponents, $\lambda$ in the turbulence part is a length scale, and $r$ is the Lorenz parameter, not a radius.)

## The Lorenz equations

The prototype of chaos in a flow is the system Lorenz studied (p. 189):

<Formula label="Lorenz equations" tex="\dot x = \sigma(y - x), \qquad \dot y = rx - y - xz, \qquad \dot z = -bz + xy" cite="reading1" at="p. 189, eq. (7.86)" />

with three positive parameters $\sigma$, $r$ and $b$. The equations are unchanged by $x \to -x$, $y \to -y$. Lorenz met chaotic behaviour in a model of the atmosphere and showed that it survives in these three equations, a stripped-down model of fluid turning in a convection roll. In it, $x$ measures how fast the fluid circulates, $y$ how much warmer the rising side is than the falling side, and $z$ how much the temperature inside the roll differs from that outside. The full Navier–Stokes and heat-transfer equations reduce to these three under drastic approximations (p. 189).

**Fixed points** (p. 189). The origin $x = y = z = 0$ is stable for $r < 1$ and a saddle for $r > 1$. For $r > 1$ there are also

<Formula label="The convecting fixed points" tex="x_* = y_* = \pm\sqrt{b(r - 1)}, \qquad z_* = r - 1" cite="reading1" at="p. 189" />

two steady rolls turning in opposite directions, born at $r = 1$ in a supercritical pitchfork. They stay stable only up to a value $r = r_H$; there each loses stability in a subcritical Hopf bifurcation, as an unstable limit cycle shrinks onto it (p. 189). (The notes do not give $r_H$; a linear analysis gives $r_H = \sigma(\sigma + b + 3)/(\sigma - b - 1)$, which is $24.74$ for Lorenz's values $\sigma = 10$, $b = \frac83$.)

**Dissipation and boundedness** (p. 190). Volumes in phase space contract everywhere at the same rate,

<Formula label="Divergence of the Lorenz flow" tex="\nabla\cdot\vec f = \frac{\partial\dot x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\dot y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial\dot z}{\partial z} = -(\sigma + b + 1) < 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 190" />

so the system is dissipative. Moreover, trajectories eventually stay inside a sphere centred at $(0, 0, r + \sigma)$, $x^2 + y^2 + (z - r - \sigma)^2 = \text{const}$ (p. 190).

**The strange attractor** (p. 190). Below $r_H$ trajectories settle on a stable fixed point. Above it they are bounded, volumes shrink, and yet there is no stable fixed point and no stable limit cycle to attract them. The result is chaos on a **strange attractor**. Trajectories on it still never cross (in three dimensions), they separate exponentially, and the attractor has zero volume, as $\dot V = \int\nabla\cdot\vec f\,dV < 0$ demands. Yet it has infinite surface area: the orbits trace out infinitely many sheets, each pass from $x > 0$ to $x < 0$ and back producing new ones, so the attractor is a **fractal**, with a dimension between 2 and 3, about $2.05$ for the Lorenz system (p. 190).

## Lyapunov exponents of a flow

How can nearby trajectories diverge exponentially while phase-space volume shrinks? In three dimensions there are three directions, along which separations can grow or shrink at different rates; in general these are principal axes that change along the trajectory, not fixed Cartesian axes. So there are three exponents (p. 190):

<Formula label="Three Lyapunov exponents" tex="\delta_j = \delta_{j0}\,e^{\lambda_j t} \ \ (j = 1, 2, 3) \quad\Rightarrow\quad V(t) \approx V_0\,e^{(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \lambda_3)t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 190, eq. (7.87)" />

When $\nabla\cdot\vec f$ is constant, as for Lorenz, comparing with (7.11) gives (p. 191)

<Formula label="Sum of the exponents" tex="\dot V = (\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \lambda_3)V(t) = \int\nabla\cdot\vec f\,dV = \nabla\cdot\vec f\,V(t) < 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 191, eq. (7.88)" />

Dissipation means only that the *sum* of the exponents is negative. Sensitivity to initial conditions needs just one positive exponent, and the **Lyapunov exponent** of the flow is defined as the largest, $\lambda \equiv \max(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3)$ (p. 191).

For the damped driven pendulum of Section 7.1, $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \lambda_3 = -1/q$. The drive phase $\varphi = \omega_D t$ just advances, so $\lambda_3 = 0$ and $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = -1/q$. Without damping ($q \to \infty$), chaos is still possible with $\lambda_1 = -\lambda_2 > 0$: chaos occurs in conservative as well as dissipative systems (p. 191). Geometrically, a small patch of phase space is stretched by $e^{\lambda_1 t}$, squeezed by $e^{\lambda_2 t}$ and turned; if trajectories are bounded, it must also be folded (p. 191), the stretching and folding of the logistic map again.

<WorkedExample title="Lorenz's attractor in numbers">
<Problem>
For Lorenz's values $\sigma = 10$, $b = \frac83$, $r = 28$: (a) locate the convecting fixed points; (b) by what factor does a phase-space volume shrink per unit time; (c) numerical studies in the literature give Lyapunov exponents of about $0.906$, $0$ and $-14.572$ for these values (not in the notes). Check them against the divergence, and estimate the attractor's dimension.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Fixed points">
$x_* = y_* = \pm\sqrt{\frac83 \times 27} = \pm\sqrt{72} = \pm 8.485$, $z_* = 27$. Since $28 > r_H = 24.74$ both are unstable.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Contraction">
$\nabla\cdot\vec f = -(10 + \frac83 + 1) = -13.667$, so volumes shrink by $e^{-13.667} \approx 1.2\times10^{-6}$ per unit time.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Exponents and dimension">
$0.906 + 0 - 14.572 = -13.666$, matching the divergence. The zero exponent belongs to the direction along the trajectory. In a section across the flow, the stretching exponent $0.906$ and the contracting one $-14.572$ play the roles of $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ in the Kaplan–Yorke relation derived below, giving $1 + 0.906/14.572 = 1.062$; adding one dimension for the direction along the flow, $d_F = 2.062$, close to the notes' value of about 2.05. (The notes' 2.05 may come from a different way of measuring dimension; the two need not agree exactly.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Fractals and their dimension

**Fractals** have nontrivial structure on arbitrarily small scales; typically they are **self-similar**, containing smaller copies of themselves at every scale (p. 191).

**The Cantor set** (pp. 191–192). Start from the segment $S_0 = [0, 1]$; remove its middle third to get $S_1$; remove the middle third of each remaining piece to get $S_2$; and so on. The Cantor set is what remains as $n \to \infty$. The number of pieces grows without bound (in the limit the set is even uncountable) while their total length $(2/3)^n$ goes to zero, the one-dimensional analogue of the Lorenz attractor's infinite area and zero volume (pp. 191–192).

**Defining the dimension** (p. 192). To cover a segment of length $a_0$ with pieces of length $a$ takes $N(a) = a_0/a$ pieces; to cover a square of side $a_0$ with squares of side $a$ takes $(a_0/a)^2$; a $D$-dimensional cube takes $(a_0/a)^D$. Turning this around defines a dimension for any set (p. 192):

<Formula label="Fractal (capacity) dimension" tex="d_F = \lim_{a\to0}\frac{\ln N(a)}{\ln(a_0/a)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 192, eq. (7.89)" />

the Hausdorff, capacity or fractal dimension. (Strictly, the box-counting definition here is the capacity dimension; the Hausdorff dimension is defined more carefully but agrees for these examples.) For the Cantor set, after $n$ steps $N = 2^n$ pieces of length $a_n = a_0/3^n$ (p. 192), so

<Formula label="Dimension of the Cantor set" tex="N(a) = 2^n, \qquad a_n = \frac{a_0}{3^n}, \qquad d_F = \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\ln 2^n}{\ln 3^n} = \frac{\ln 2}{\ln 3} \approx 0.6309" cite="reading1" at="p. 192, eqs. (7.90)–(7.92)" />

between a point ($d_F = 0$) and a line ($d_F = 1$). Fractal dimensions are usually not integers, and usually irrational (p. 192).

**The Koch curve** (pp. 192–193). Instead of deleting each middle third, replace it by the other two sides of an equilateral triangle built on it, so segments are added rather than removed; one side of the Koch snowflake is a Koch curve. Each step makes four pieces out of one, a third as long (p. 192):

<Formula label="Dimension of the Koch curve" tex="N(a) = 4^n, \qquad a_n = 3^{-n}a_0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad d_F = \frac{\ln 4}{\ln 3} \approx 1.262" cite="reading1" at="p. 192, eq. (7.93)" />

Between 1 and 2: the curve has infinite length, $(4/3)^n \to \infty$, but zero area (p. 193).

  caption="Total length after n construction steps, computed from N(a)·aₙ: (2/3)ⁿ for the Cantor set (2ⁿ pieces of length 3⁻ⁿ) and (4/3)ⁿ for the Koch curve (4ⁿ pieces of length 3⁻ⁿ). One vanishes and the other diverges; after 10 steps they are 0.017 and 17.8. Neither set is measured properly by length, which is why each has a non-integer dimension, 0.631 and 1.262." />

**Dimension from the dynamics** (p. 193). Fractal dimension can be tied to the Lyapunov exponents. Take, for simplicity, $\lambda_1 > 0$, $\lambda_2 < 0$ and $\lambda_3 = 0$, and follow a square patch of phase space of side $a_0$ (p. 193):

<Formula label="Area of a stretched patch" tex="A(t = 0) = a_0^2 \;\to\; A_0(t) = a_0^2\,e^{(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 193, eq. (7.94)" />

The patch becomes a long thin strip whose width shrinks as $a_0e^{\lambda_2 t}$, so covering it takes squares of area $A(t) = a_0^2e^{2\lambda_2 t}$, and their number is (p. 193)

<Formula label="Number of covering squares" tex="N(t) = \frac{A_0(t)}{A(t)} = e^{(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 193, eq. (7.95)" />

With $a = a_0e^{\lambda_2 t}$ in (7.89), $\ln N/\ln(a_0/a) = (\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)/|\lambda_2|$ (our intermediate step), which is the **Kaplan–Yorke relation** (p. 193):

<Formula label="Kaplan–Yorke relation" tex="d_F = 1 + \frac{\lambda_1}{|\lambda_2|}" cite="reading1" at="p. 193, eq. (7.96)" />

A fixed-point attractor has $d_F = 0$ and a limit cycle $d_F = 1$; a strange attractor generally has a non-integer dimension, set by the balance of stretching ($\lambda_1$) and contraction ($\lambda_2$) (p. 193).

## Chaos in fluids and turbulence

Fluids can be chaotic too (p. 194). For an incompressible fluid, $\nabla\cdot\vec v = 0$, of constant, uniform density $\rho$, the Navier–Stokes equation (with the heat-transfer equation alongside) reads (p. 194)

<Formula label="Navier–Stokes equation" tex="\frac{\partial\vec v}{\partial t} = -\vec v\cdot\nabla\vec v - \frac{\nabla P}{\rho} + \nu\nabla^2\vec v" cite="reading1" at="p. 194, eq. (7.97)" />

In the language of this chapter, the velocity field $\vec v(\vec x, t)$ is a continuum of variables, one for each point $\vec x$, and the derivatives couple neighbouring variables, much as a finite difference does (p. 194):

<Formula label="Derivative as coupling of neighbours" tex="\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} \approx \frac{v_x(x + h) - v_x(x - h)}{2h}" cite="reading1" at="p. 194, eq. (7.98)" />

with a small step $h$ (the notes write $\epsilon$, a letter used below for the energy flux).

Sometimes, as in the convection rolls of the Lorenz model, the time dependence is aperiodic while the spatial pattern stays regular, and the ideas of this chapter, such as the period-doubling route, apply. In other cases the spatial structure becomes irregular too, and which happens can depend on the initial conditions, as in wide convection rolls in a shallow layer of fluid: several attractors coexist. When the structure is irregular in space, the attractor's dimension grows in proportion to the size of the system, and it is more useful to speak of a dimension per unit volume, a "dimension density" (p. 194).

**Strong turbulence** is irregular in space, with features of every size and no characteristic one. There is still no complete theory of it, but dimensional analysis goes a long way (p. 194). In three dimensions eddies of all sizes $\lambda$ carry energy down to smaller scales. Let $L$ be the size of the container, $\lambda_0$ the scale where viscosity becomes important (Reynolds number about 1), $\epsilon$ the mean energy transferred per unit time and unit mass, with units m²/s³, and $v_\lambda$ the velocity variation across a length $\lambda$; the kinematic viscosity $\nu$ has units m²/s (p. 195). Three ranges follow (p. 195):

1. At $\lambda \approx L$, viscosity plays no part, so $\epsilon \propto v_L^3/L$. This scale holds most of the kinetic energy.
2. In the **inertial range** $\lambda_0 \ll \lambda \ll L$ viscosity still plays no part, so $\epsilon \propto v_\lambda^3/\lambda$, independent of $\rho$, $\nu$ and the container size $L$.
3. At $\lambda \approx \lambda_0$, the Reynolds number $v_0\lambda_0/\nu \approx 1$ gives $v_0 \approx \nu/\lambda_0$; energy is dissipated here, and with only $\nu$ and $\lambda_0$ left, $\epsilon \propto \nu^3/\lambda_0^4$.

In terms of the wavenumber $k \propto 1/\lambda$ and the kinetic energy per unit mass per unit wavenumber, $E(k)$ (so that $E(k)\,dk$ is the energy per unit mass in a range $dk$, with units m³/s²), the inertial range depends only on $\epsilon$ and $k$. Matching units, $\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2 = (\text{m}^2/\text{s}^3)^{2/3}(1/\text{m})^{-5/3}$, gives (p. 195)

<Formula label="Kolmogorov's law" tex="E(k) \sim \epsilon^{2/3}k^{-5/3}" cite="reading1" at="p. 195, eq. (7.99)" />

the famous Kolmogorov scaling law of strong turbulence, a universal prediction that can be tested in many systems (p. 195).

</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The **Lorenz equations** $\dot x = \sigma(y - x)$, $\dot y = rx - y - xz$, $\dot z = -bz + xy$ model a convection roll. The origin is stable for $r < 1$; a pitchfork at $r = 1$ creates the fixed points $(\pm\sqrt{b(r - 1)}, \pm\sqrt{b(r - 1)}, r - 1)$, which lose stability at $r_H$ in subcritical Hopf bifurcations.
- The flow is dissipative, $\nabla\cdot\vec f = -(\sigma + b + 1)$, and bounded; beyond $r_H$ it settles on a **strange attractor** of zero volume, infinite area and fractal dimension about 2.05.
- **Lyapunov exponents**: volumes change as $e^{(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \lambda_3)t}$; chaos needs one positive exponent, dissipation only a negative sum. Chaos occurs in conservative and dissipative systems.
- **Fractal dimension** $d_F = \lim\ln N(a)/\ln(a_0/a)$: Cantor set $\ln 2/\ln 3 \approx 0.631$, Koch curve $\ln 4/\ln 3 \approx 1.262$. The **Kaplan–Yorke relation** $d_F = 1 + \lambda_1/|\lambda_2|$ links it to the dynamics.
- Fluids are systems with a continuum of variables. Strong turbulence lacks a full theory, but dimensional analysis gives **Kolmogorov's law** $E(k) \sim \epsilon^{2/3}k^{-5/3}$ in the inertial range, and a dissipation scale $\lambda_0 \sim (\nu^3/\epsilon)^{1/4}$.
