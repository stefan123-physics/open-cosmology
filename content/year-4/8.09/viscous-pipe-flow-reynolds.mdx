With the [Navier-Stokes equation](/courses/8-09/viscous-fluid-equations) in hand, the notes solve their first viscous flows: steady flows through pipes, usually driven by a pressure difference (pp. 130–134). Such flows are called **Poiseuille flow**, after Jean Poiseuille, who first studied them in experiments (p. 130). The notes' figures are not reproduced, and the worked examples are our own.

The flow is taken incompressible, $\nabla\cdot\vec v = 0$, so as for ideal fluids the density is constant (p. 130). With a steady flow and no external force the Navier-Stokes equation is

<Formula label="Steady incompressible Navier-Stokes equation" tex="\vec v\cdot\nabla\vec v = -\frac{\nabla P}{\rho} + \nu\nabla^2\vec v" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7, eq. (6.120), p. 130" />

## Flow between parallel plates

Take a long, thin rectangular duct along $x$, of height $h$ and width $w \gg h$, with a pressure gradient along it (p. 130). Letting $w \to \infty$ with $h$ fixed turns it into two infinite parallel plates at $y = 0$ and $y = h$ (p. 131). The plates look the same at every $x$ and $z$, so nothing depends on $x$ or $z$, and nothing can make the fluid move in $y$ or $z$ (p. 131):

<Formula label="The velocity field between the plates" tex="\vec v = v_x(y)\,\hat x" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7, eq. (6.121), p. 131" />

The advective term then vanishes, $\vec v\cdot\nabla\vec v = v_x\,\partial\vec v/\partial x = 0$. The $y$ and $z$ components of (6.120) contain no velocity at all, and say (p. 131)

<Formula label="No pressure gradient across the flow" tex="\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial z} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7, eq. (6.122), p. 131" />

so $P = P(x)$. The $x$ component gives (p. 131)

<Formula label="Pressure gradient balances friction" tex="\frac{\partial P}{\partial x} = \rho\nu\nabla^2v_x = \eta\,\frac{\partial^2v_x}{\partial y^2} = k" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7, eq. (6.123), p. 131" />

The left side depends only on $x$ and the middle only on $y$, so both equal a constant $k$. Take $k < 0$: the pressure falls along $+x$, and in the Navier-Stokes equation this pressure drop balances the viscous stress (p. 131). Integrating twice (p. 131),

<Formula label="General solution" tex="v_x(y) = \frac{k}{2\eta}\,y^2 + ay + b" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7, eq. (6.124), p. 131" />

and the no-slip conditions $v_x(0) = v_x(h) = 0$ fix $a$ and $b$ (p. 131):

<Formula label="Poiseuille profile between plates" tex="v_x(y) = \frac{(-k)}{2\eta}\,y\left(h - y\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7, eq. (6.125), p. 131" />

With $k < 0$ the velocity is positive, down the pipe (reversing $k$ reverses the flow). The profile is a **parabola**, zero at the walls and fastest in the middle (p. 131).

The fluid also pulls on the walls (p. 132). The bottom plate has $\hat n = \hat y$, so by (6.117) the force per unit area along $x$ is

<Formula label="Drag on the wall" tex="F_x = \sigma_{yx} = \eta\,\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial y}\ \text{at}\ y = 0, \ = -\frac{hk}{2} > 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7, eq. (6.126), p. 132" />

positive, as one expects: the moving fluid pulls the wall in its own direction (p. 132). By symmetry the top plate feels the same.

**A moving plate.** Now let there be no pressure gradient, $P = P_0$ uniform and $k = 0$, but move the top plate at $\vec u = u\hat x$ (p. 132). Then $\nabla^2v_x = 0$, the profile is at most linear, and the conditions $v_x(0) = 0$, $v_x(h) = u$ give $v_x(y) = uy/h$: a straight-line profile.

Both vanish at the fixed bottom plate, as the no-slip condition requires." />

## Flow in a round pipe

Next a long pipe of radius $L$ along $x$, again with a pressure gradient (p. 132). Taking it infinitely long, nothing depends on $x$, so $\vec v = \vec v(y, z)$; in cylindrical coordinates $(x, r, \theta)$ the symmetry about the axis gives $\vec v = \vec v(r)$ (p. 132). The velocity must depend on $r$, since it vanishes at the wall $r = L$ but not everywhere (pp. 132–133). Continuity and symmetry also make it point along the pipe (p. 133):

<Formula label="The velocity field in the pipe" tex="\vec v = v_x(r)\,\hat x" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7, eq. (6.127), p. 133" />

To see why there is no radial flow, take a closed surface between two cylinders coaxial with the pipe, capped at two values of $x$ (p. 133). The flows through the two end caps cancel, because $\vec v$ does not depend on $x$; the flows through the inner and outer curved walls could only cancel too if there were none, since a radial flow would depend on $r$.

Again $(\vec v\cdot\nabla)\vec v = v_x\,\partial(v_x\hat x)/\partial x = 0$, so $\nabla P = \eta\nabla^2\vec v$ (p. 133). Its $y$ and $z$ components give $P = P(x)$, and its $x$ component (p. 133)

<Formula label="Pressure gradient in the pipe" tex="\frac{\partial P(x)}{\partial x} = \eta\nabla^2v_x(r) = k" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7, eq. (6.128), p. 133" />

is a constant $k$, chosen negative to push the fluid along $+x$. With the Laplacian in cylindrical coordinates (p. 133):

<Formula label="Radial equation" tex="\frac{1}{r}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r\,\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial r}\right) = \frac{k}{\eta}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7, eq. (6.129), p. 133" />

Integrating (p. 133),

<Formula label="General radial solution" tex="v_x(r) = \frac{k}{4\eta}\,r^2 + a\ln\left(\frac{r}{r_0}\right) + b" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7, eq. (6.130), p. 133" />

where $r_0$ only makes the logarithm's argument dimensionless; changing it just shifts $b$ (p. 133). The velocity must be finite on the axis, so $a = 0$ (a pipe with a solid core, which excludes the axis, would keep it) [the notes' bracket here, "if the geometry excluded the region at the middle", is incomplete; as written it seems to give the condition for keeping the logarithm rather than for dropping it], and $v_x(L) = 0$ fixes $b$ (p. 133):

<Formula label="Poiseuille profile in a round pipe" tex="v_x(r) = \frac{(-k)}{4\eta}\left(L^2 - r^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7, eq. (6.131), p. 133" />

The **discharge rate**, the mass passing per unit time, adds up the flow through rings of radius $r$ and width $dr$ (p. 133):

<Formula label="Discharge rate of a pipe" tex="\text{discharge rate} = \rho\int_0^L 2\pi r\,v_x(r)\,dr = -\frac{\pi kL^4}{8\nu} > 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7, eq. (6.132), p. 133" />

It goes as the **fourth power** of the radius, which is why narrowed blood vessels are so dangerous (p. 133).

<WorkedExample title="Water in a 2 cm pipe">
<Problem>
Water ($\rho = 1000$ kg/m³, $\eta = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ Pa s) flows steadily through a long pipe of radius 1 cm, driven by a pressure gradient $k = -5$ Pa/m. Find the speed on the axis, the mass discharged per second, and what happens to the discharge if the radius is halved at the same gradient.
</Problem>
<Step title="Speed on the axis">
At $r = 0$, $v_x = (-k)L^2/4\eta = 5 \times 10^{-4}/(4 \times 10^{-3}) = 0.125$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Discharge">
With $\nu = \eta/\rho = 10^{-6}$ m²/s: $-\pi kL^4/8\nu = \pi \times 5 \times 10^{-8}/(8 \times 10^{-6}) \approx 0.0196$ kg/s, about 20 grams of water per second.
</Step>
<Step title="Half the radius">
The discharge falls by $2^4 = 16$, to about 1.2 g/s. To keep the flow, the pressure gradient would have to rise sixteenfold.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The Reynolds number and similarity

How important is viscosity in a given flow? Pick a typical length $L$ of the problem (the radius of a pipe, the size of a sphere in the flow) and a typical speed $u$ (the speed on the pipe's axis, or the speed of the flow far upstream) (p. 134). The dimensionless **Reynolds number** is (p. 134)

<Formula label="Reynolds number" tex="R = \frac{uL}{\nu} = \frac{\rho uL}{\eta}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7, eq. (6.133), p. 134" />

Large $R$ means viscosity matters little for flows of that size and speed; small $R$ means it matters a great deal. Near a surface, however, viscosity always matters, because it changes the boundary condition (p. 134). The Poiseuille flows above are found, and stay laminar, for $R \lesssim 2000$ (p. 134). (Compared with the advective term, the viscous term of (6.113) is of order $1/R$; our remark, made precise in Section 6.8.)

The Reynolds number opens the way to **dimensional analysis**, best shown by examples (p. 134). For a steady incompressible flow fixed by one length $L$ and one upstream speed $u$, the only dimensionless combinations available are $\vec r/L$ and $R$, so the velocity and pressure must take the form (p. 134)

<Formula label="Form of a flow with one length and one speed" tex="\frac{\vec v}{u} = \vec h\left(\frac{\vec r}{L}, R\right), \qquad \frac{P}{\rho u^2} = g\left(\frac{\vec r}{L}, R\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7, eq. (6.134), p. 134" />

with $\vec h$ a dimensionless vector function and $g$ a scalar one: $u$ carries the units of the velocity and $\rho u^2$ those of the pressure. Flows with the same $R$ are therefore copies of one another, differing only in the scales of $\vec v$, $\vec r$ and $P$; they are called **similar** (p. 134).

For example, take viscous flow past a sphere of radius $a$, with velocity $u\hat x$ far upstream, so $R = ua/\nu$ (p. 134). [The notes print $R = (u/a)\nu$; the definition (6.133) with $L = a$ gives $ua/\nu$, which is also what the argument that follows needs.] Doubling both $\nu$ and $u$ leaves $R$ unchanged, so by (6.134) the new flow has exactly the same pattern, with velocities twice and pressures four times as large (p. 134).

Other dimensionless ratios may enter too, such as the ratio of two lengths or the Mach number $M = u/c_S$; for $M \ll 1$ the fluid is treated as incompressible and $M$ is left out (p. 134). How many independent ratios does a problem have? Count its variables and subtract the number of independent kinds of units among them; in most fluid problems that is three, for kilograms, metres and seconds (p. 134).

<WorkedExample title="A model in a wind tunnel">
<Problem>
A car 4 m long is to be tested at 20 m/s using a 1:10 scale model in a wind tunnel with air at the same temperature and pressure. How fast must the tunnel air blow for the flows to be similar, and is the incompressible treatment still fine there?
</Problem>
<Step title="Matching R">
The same air means the same $\nu$, so $R = uL/\nu$ is unchanged only if $uL$ is: with $L$ ten times smaller, $u = 10 \times 20 = 200$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="The catch">
At 200 m/s the Mach number is $200/340 \approx 0.59$, no longer small: compressibility would now matter, and the two flows would not be similar after all. This is why real tests match $R$ in other ways, for instance with a denser fluid or a larger model.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- **Poiseuille flow** between plates: $\partial P/\partial x = \eta\,\partial^2v_x/\partial y^2 = k$ gives the parabola $v_x = \frac{-k}{2\eta}y(h - y)$ and a drag $-hk/2$ per unit area on each wall. With no pressure gradient and a moving plate, $v_x = uy/h$.
- In a **round pipe** of radius $L$: $v_x = \frac{-k}{4\eta}(L^2 - r^2)$, and the discharge $-\pi kL^4/8\nu$ grows as $L^4$.
- The **Reynolds number** $R = uL/\nu = \rho uL/\eta$ measures how little viscosity matters; it always matters near surfaces.
- Flows with one length and one speed have $\vec v/u = \vec h(\vec r/L, R)$ and $P/\rho u^2 = g(\vec r/L, R)$: equal $R$ means **similar** flows. Count variables minus independent units to find the number of dimensionless ratios.
