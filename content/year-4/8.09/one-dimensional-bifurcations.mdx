The [previous section](/courses/8-09/chaos-phase-space-poincare-sections) ended with the driven pendulum's bifurcation diagram, where a single sampled value of $\omega$ split into two as the drive grew: a **period doubling**. Stewart now studies such qualitative changes systematically, starting with the simplest setting where they occur, a single variable. (Citations give the printed page numbers 153 to 163 of the Chapter 7 notes. On this page $x_*$ is a fixed point, $r$ the parameter being varied, and $a$, $b$ are constants unless stated otherwise.)

## Fixed points of a single variable

A **bifurcation** is an abrupt change in the existence or the type of a system's fixed points, limit cycles or attractors as one of its parameters is varied (notes p. 153). Since one variable is already enough to produce them, and their higher-dimensional versions mostly look the same, the notes begin with

<Formula label="A one-dimensional flow" tex="\dot x = f(x)" cite="reading1" at="p. 153, eq. (7.17)" />

The motion is simple: a trajectory either flows to a fixed point or runs off to $x \to \pm\infty$ (p. 153). Think of a particle on the $x$-axis whose velocity is $f(x)$. Where $f > 0$ it moves right, where $f < 0$ left, so the arrows on the axis between the zeros of $f$ show at once which fixed points attract and which repel. The notes' example is $\dot x = x^2 - 1$: positive outside $[-1, 1]$ and negative inside, so the particle flows towards $x = -1$ from both sides (stable) and away from $x = +1$ (unstable) (pp. 153–154). The same method is the subject of the 18.03 page on [autonomous equations](/courses/18-03/autonomous-equations).

**Linearizing.** Stability can also be found by writing $x = x_* + \eta$ and keeping only the first order in $\eta$. For $x^2 - 1$ this gives $\dot\eta \approx 2x_*\eta$: at $x_* = -1$, $\eta \propto e^{-2t}$ decays (stable); at $x_* = 1$, $\eta \propto e^{2t}$ grows (unstable) (p. 154). That is overkill for one variable but is the method that generalizes. With $n$ variables the same expansion gives a linear system (p. 154)

<Formula label="Linearization about a fixed point" tex="\dot{\vec\eta} = M\vec\eta" cite="reading1" at="p. 154, eq. (7.18)" />

with $M$ an $n \times n$ matrix whose eigenvalues $\lambda$ and eigenvectors give solutions $\vec\eta = \vec\eta_0 e^{\lambda t}$ near the fixed point; Section 7.3 works this out in two dimensions.

To study bifurcations, let $f$ depend on a parameter $r$ (p. 154):

<Formula label="A one-dimensional flow with a parameter" tex="\dot x = f(x, r)" cite="reading1" at="p. 154, eq. (7.19)" />

The fixed points $x_*(r)$ are then curves in the $r$–$x$ plane. Drawing them, solid where stable and dashed where unstable, gives the **bifurcation diagram**.

## Saddle-node bifurcation

Fixed points come into being and vanish, most commonly, through a **saddle-node bifurcation**: as $r$ changes, a stable and an unstable fixed point move towards each other, coalesce and are gone (p. 154). The standard example is (p. 155)

<Formula label="Saddle-node example" tex="\dot x = r + x^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 155, eq. (7.20)" />

For $r < 0$ there are two fixed points, $x_* = -\sqrt{-r}$ (stable, since $f$ falls through zero there: $f' = 2x_* < 0$) and $x_* = +\sqrt{-r}$ (unstable). At $r = 0$ they merge into one half-stable point at $x = 0$, approached from the left and left from the right. For $r > 0$ the parabola lies wholly above the axis and every trajectory runs off to $+\infty$ (p. 155). The mirror case $\dot x = r - x^2$ follows by changing $x \to -x$, $r \to -r$: its fixed points $\pm\sqrt r$ exist for $r > 0$ (p. 155).

**A graphical method.** For $\dot x = r - x - e^{-x}$ the fixed points obey the transcendental equation $r - x_* = e^{-x_*}$ and cannot be written down (p. 156):

<Formula label="A flow with transcendental fixed points" tex="\dot x = r - x - e^{-x} = f(x, r), \qquad r - x_* = e^{-x_*}" cite="reading1" at="p. 156, eqs. (7.21), (7.22)" />

Instead, draw the straight line $r - x$ and the curve $e^{-x}$: they intersect at the fixed points, and whichever is higher sets the direction of the flow. Raising $r$ slides the line upward. At one value $r_C$ the line just touches the curve, and that tangency is the bifurcation: there $f$ and its slope vanish together (p. 156),

<Formula label="Condition for the tangency" tex="\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_{x = x_*,\; r = r_C} = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 156, eq. (7.23)" />

Here $-1 + e^{-x_*} = 0$ gives $x_* = 0$, and then (7.22) gives $r_C = 1$. Two fixed points exist for $r > 1$, none for $r < 1$ (p. 156 and Fig. 7.14).

    { expr: "r - x", label: "r − x", dash: true }

  params={{ r: { value: 1.5, min: 0.5, max: 2.5, step: 0.05, label: "r" } }}
  caption="Fixed points of ẋ = r − x − e^(−x) as the crossings of the line r − x with the curve e^(−x), computed from the two formulas. At r = 1 the line is tangent at x = 0; for r = 1.5 it crosses twice (near x = −0.86 and x = 1.20), for r = 0.5 not at all." />

**Why $r \pm x^2$ is the general case.** Expand $f$ about the bifurcation point $(x_*, r_C)$ (p. 156):

<Formula label="Taylor expansion at a saddle-node" tex="\dot x = f(x_*, r_C) + (x - x_*)\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + (r - r_C)\frac{\partial f}{\partial r} + \frac{(x - x_*)^2}{2}\,\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} + \ldots = a(r - r_C) + b(x - x_*)^2 + \ldots" cite="reading1" at="p. 156, eq. (7.24)" />

with derivatives taken at $(x_*, r_C)$. Two terms drop out because of the two defining conditions (p. 157):

<Formula label="Saddle-node conditions" tex="f(x_*, r_C) = 0, \qquad \left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_{x = x_*,\; r = r_C} = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 157, eq. (7.25)" />

and the constants are $a = \partial f/\partial r$ and $b = \frac12\partial^2 f/\partial x^2$ there. Rescaling $x - x_*$ and $r - r_C$ turns this into $\dot x = r \pm x^2$, the sign being that of $b$: $r \pm x^2$ is the **normal form** of the saddle-node bifurcation (p. 157). (The notes' substitution, $r' = a(r - r_C)$ and $x' = \sqrt{|b|}(x - x_*)$, leaves an overall factor $\sqrt{|b|}$, removed by also rescaling time; alternatively $x' = |b|(x - x_*)$, $r' = |b|a(r - r_C)$ works directly.)

## Transcritical bifurcation

A **transcritical bifurcation** removes no fixed point: one is present for every $r$, but as another passes through it the two trade their stability (p. 157). The normal form is (p. 157)

<Formula label="Transcritical normal form" tex="\dot x = x(r - x)" cite="reading1" at="p. 157, eq. (7.26)" />

with fixed points $x_* = 0$ and $x_* = r$. Since $f'(x) = r - 2x$, the point $0$ is stable for $r < 0$ and unstable for $r > 0$, while $x_* = r$ does the opposite. The two lines cross at $r = 0$ and swap stability; neither disappears (pp. 157–158). Expanding a general $f$ near such a crossing gives this form again (p. 158).

    { expr: "r > 0 ? r : NaN" },
    { expr: "r > 0 ? 0 : NaN", label: "unstable", dash: true },
    { expr: "r < 0 ? r : NaN", dash: true }
  ]}
  caption="Bifurcation diagram of ẋ = x(r − x), from the fixed points x* = 0 and x* = r and the slope f′ = r − 2x: solid where f′(x*) < 0 (stable), dashed where it is positive. At r = 0 the two branches cross and exchange stability." />

**The laser threshold.** A physical example: $N$ excited atoms and $n$ laser photons, with stimulated-emission gain $G$, photon loss rate $K$, spontaneous decay rate $f$ and pump strength $p$ (p. 158):

<Formula label="Rate equations of a laser" tex="\dot n = GnN - Kn, \qquad \dot N = -GnN - fN + p" cite="reading1" at="p. 158, eq. (7.27)" />

This is a two-variable system. If the atoms relax quickly, $\dot N \approx 0$, and the second equation gives $N = p/(Gn + f)$ (eq. 7.28). Substituting into the first (p. 158),

<Formula label="Photon equation after eliminating the atoms" tex="\dot n = \frac{n}{Gn + f}\bigl[pG - K(Gn + f)\bigr]" cite="reading1" at="p. 158, eq. (7.29)" />

and expanding for small $n$ (p. 159),

<Formula label="Laser near threshold" tex="\dot n = n(r - bn) + O(n^3), \qquad r \equiv \frac{pG}{f} - K, \qquad b \equiv \frac{G^2p}{f^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 159, eqs. (7.30), (7.31)" />

a transcritical form (rescale $n$ by $b$ to get exactly (7.26)). Only $n \ge 0$ is physical. Below threshold, $p < Kf/G$, $r$ is negative and the only stable state is $n = 0$: the device is a lamp, with spontaneous emission but no build-up of photons in the mode. Above threshold $n = 0$ turns unstable and the stable state is $n_* = r/b > 0$: coherent laser action (p. 159). (The notes write the expansion in (7.29) as $n(r - x)$ and the non-zero fixed point as $n = r$; the first is a misprint for $n(r - bn)$, and the second is $n_* = r/b$ with $b$ scaled to 1, as in their Figs. 7.17 and 7.18.)

## Supercritical pitchfork bifurcation

Systems with a symmetry often create or destroy fixed points in pairs. In a **supercritical pitchfork bifurcation** the symmetric fixed point survives throughout, but at the critical parameter it becomes unstable, and at that same value a pair of new, stable fixed points splits off from it (p. 159). The normal form is (p. 159)

<Formula label="Supercritical pitchfork normal form" tex="\dot x = rx - x^3" cite="reading1" at="p. 159, eq. (7.32)" />

It is unchanged by $x \to -x$, so $x_* = 0$ is always a fixed point; $x_* = \pm\sqrt r$ appear only once $r > 0$. With $f' = r - 3x^2$, the origin is stable for $r < 0$ and unstable for $r > 0$, and the new points have $f' = -2r < 0$: stable (pp. 159–160).

<Plot variable="r" domain={[-2, 2]} range={[-1.6, 1.6]} xLabel="r" yLabel="x*"
  curves={[
    { expr: "r < 0 ? 0 : NaN", label: "stable" },
    { expr: "r >= 0 ? sqrt(r) : NaN" },
    { expr: "r >= 0 ? -sqrt(r) : NaN" },
    { expr: "r > 0 ? 0 : NaN", label: "unstable", dash: true }
  ]}
  caption="Supercritical pitchfork, ẋ = rx − x³, computed from x* = 0 and x* = ±√r: the symmetric state loses stability at r = 0 exactly where two stable states branch off, the shape of a pitchfork." />

The symmetric equation does not say which branch the system takes when $r$ crosses zero: a tiny perturbation decides. (A picture of our own, not in the notes:) the flow is $\dot x = -dV/dx$ with $V = -\frac12 rx^2 + \frac14 x^4$, a single well for $r < 0$ that becomes a double well for $r > 0$, with the system rolling into one of two equally deep minima.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Picking one of equal minima">
The end state of the pitchfork, a system that has settled at random into one of several equally good minima of a symmetric potential, is the picture behind [spontaneous symmetry breaking by the Higgs field](/courses/8-286/higgs-field) in 8.286 Lecture 22. There the Higgs potential has its minimum at a non-zero field magnitude, reached in infinitely many equivalent ways; as the universe cools, the field settles into one of them at random, and the symmetry of the equations is no longer visible in the state. The pitchfork is the one-variable, two-minimum version of that mathematics (the Higgs case is a field with many components, and the lecture does not use the language of bifurcations).
</Callout>

## Subcritical pitchfork bifurcation and hysteresis

The **subcritical pitchfork** is the reverse: the always-present fixed point changes between stable and unstable while two *unstable* fixed points branch off it (p. 160). Its normal form is (p. 160)

<Formula label="Subcritical pitchfork normal form" tex="\dot x = rx + x^3" cite="reading1" at="p. 160, eq. (7.33)" />

again symmetric under $x \to -x$, but now the cubic term pushes outward. The branches $x_* = \pm\sqrt{-r}$ exist for $r < 0$ and are unstable ($f' = -2r > 0$ there); $x = 0$ is stable for $r < 0$ and unstable for $r > 0$ (pp. 160–161). For $r > 0$ nothing in the cubic model stops $x$ from running away.

A stabilizing higher-order term changes that (p. 161):

<Formula label="Subcritical pitchfork with a stabilizing term" tex="\dot x = rx + x^3 - x^5" cite="reading1" at="p. 161, eq. (7.34)" />

Its non-zero fixed points satisfy $x_*^2 = \frac12\bigl(1 \pm \sqrt{1 + 4r}\bigr)$ (our solution of $r + x^2 - x^4 = 0$). For $-\frac14 < r < 0$ there are five real fixed points: $0$ and the stable outer pair (both stable), separated by the unstable inner pair (p. 161). At $r = -\frac14$ inner and outer branches meet in saddle-node bifurcations at $x_*^2 = \frac12$ and vanish.

This produces **hysteresis** (p. 161). Start at $x = 0$ with $r$ negative, say $-0.2$, and increase $r$ slowly. Nothing happens until $r = 0$, where the origin turns unstable and a small perturbation sends the system to an outer branch, at $|x| = 1$; it then rides up that branch as $r$ grows. Now decrease $r$. The system stays on the outer branch, also below $r = 0$, until the branch ends at the saddle-node $r_C = -\frac14$, $|x| = 0.71$; there it loses stability and drops back to $x = 0$. The jumps up and down happen at different values of $r$, and the cycle can be repeated (pp. 161–162).

**A bead on a spinning hoop** (p. 162). The notes place this example after the subcritical case, but it is a supercritical pitchfork. A bead of mass $m$ slides with friction on a hoop of radius $a$ that spins at constant angular velocity $\omega$ about a vertical diameter; $\theta$ is measured from the bottom:

<Formula label="Bead on a rotating hoop" tex="ma\ddot\theta + b\dot\theta = m\sin\theta\,(a\omega^2\cos\theta - g)" cite="reading1" at="p. 162, eq. (7.35)" />

In the overdamped limit the inertial term $ma\ddot\theta$ is dropped, leaving a first-order equation. The fixed points are $\theta_* = 0$, stable while $a\omega^2 < g$ and unstable once $a\omega^2 > g$; $\theta_* = \pi$, always present and unstable; and, for $a\omega^2 > g$, the stable pair $\theta_* = \pm\arccos\bigl(g/(a\omega^2)\bigr)$ (p. 162). This is a supercritical pitchfork at $a\omega^2 = g$: spin the hoop fast enough and the bottom becomes unstable, the bead moving out to one side, further the faster the spin (pp. 162–163).

<WorkedExample title="A bead on a fast hoop">
<Problem>
A hoop of radius $a = 0.20$ m spins about a vertical diameter, $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$, and the bead is strongly damped. (a) Above what angular velocity does the bottom become unstable? (b) Where does the bead settle at $\omega = 10$ rad/s?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The bifurcation">
The pitchfork is at $a\omega^2 = g$: $\omega_C = \sqrt{g/a} = \sqrt{49.05} = 7.00$ rad/s, about 67 revolutions per minute.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The new fixed point">
$a\omega^2 = 0.20 \times 100 = 20\ \text{m/s}^2$, so $\cos\theta_* = 9.81/20 = 0.4905$ and $\theta_* = \pm 60.6°$. Which side the bead chooses depends on how it was disturbed when $\omega$ passed $\omega_C$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A bottleneck: the pendulum with constant torque

Finally, an overdamped pendulum driven by a constant torque (p. 163):

<Formula label="Overdamped pendulum with constant torque" tex="\dot\theta = \tau - b\sin\theta" cite="reading1" at="p. 163, eq. (7.36)" />

with $\tau > 0$, $b > 0$ and $\theta$ in $[-\pi, \pi]$. For $b > \tau$ the torque is too weak to lift the pendulum over: $\sin\theta_* = \tau/b$ has two solutions, one stable and one unstable, and the pendulum comes to rest at an angle. For $b < \tau$ no angle balances the torque, there are no fixed points, and the pendulum keeps rotating (p. 163). The change at $\tau = b$ is a saddle-node bifurcation. Even just beyond it, the vanished fixed points leave a trace: the pendulum slows down sharply as it passes the angle where they were, a **bottleneck**, before speeding up again on the far side (p. 163).

<WorkedExample title="Time through the bottleneck">
<Problem>
For $\dot\theta = \tau - b\sin\theta$ with $\tau > b$, find the time for one full turn, and evaluate it for $b = 1\ \text{s}^{-1}$ at $\tau = 2$ and $\tau = 1.01\ \text{s}^{-1}$. (This calculation is ours; the notes describe the slowing in words.)
</Problem>
<Step title="The period">
$T = \int_0^{2\pi} d\theta/(\tau - b\sin\theta) = 2\pi/\sqrt{\tau^2 - b^2}$, a standard integral.
</Step>
<Step title="Two cases">
At $\tau = 2$: $T = 2\pi/\sqrt3 = 3.63$ s. At $\tau = 1.01$: $T = 2\pi/\sqrt{0.0201} = 44.3$ s. Close to the bifurcation the period diverges as $(\tau - b)^{-1/2}$, and nearly all of it is spent near $\theta = \pi/2$, where $\dot\theta$ falls to $\tau - b = 0.01$ rad/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A **bifurcation** is a qualitative change in the fixed points (or cycles, or attractors) of $\dot x = f(x, r)$ as $r$ varies; the **bifurcation diagram** plots $x_*(r)$, solid where stable and dashed where unstable. In one dimension a fixed point is stable if $f'(x_*) < 0$; in $n$ dimensions one linearizes, $\dot{\vec\eta} = M\vec\eta$.
- **Saddle-node**: a stable and an unstable fixed point merge and vanish, where $f = 0$ and $\partial f/\partial x = 0$; normal form $r \pm x^2$. Example: $\dot x = r - x - e^{-x}$ at $r_C = 1$.
- **Transcritical**: two fixed points exchange stability; normal form $x(r - x)$. Example: the laser threshold $p = Kf/G$.
- **Supercritical pitchfork**: with $x \to -x$ symmetry, $x = 0$ turns unstable as two stable points $\pm\sqrt r$ appear; normal form $rx - x^3$. Example: a bead on a hoop spinning faster than $\sqrt{g/a}$.
- **Subcritical pitchfork**: the side branches are unstable; normal form $rx + x^3$. With $-x^5$ added the system jumps between branches and shows hysteresis between $r = -\frac14$ and $r = 0$.
- A saddle-node leaves a **bottleneck** behind: the pendulum with torque, $\dot\theta = \tau - b\sin\theta$, rotates for $\tau > b$ with a period $2\pi/\sqrt{\tau^2 - b^2}$ that diverges at the bifurcation.
