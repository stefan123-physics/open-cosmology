The previous sections mostly assumed $\nabla\cdot\vec v = 0$. This section is the last ideal-fluid example, and here compressibility, $\nabla\cdot\vec v \neq 0$, is the whole point: it gives sound (pp. 124–127). Compare the one-dimensional treatment of [sound waves in 8.03](/courses/8-03/sound-waves); here the waves come straight out of the three-dimensional fluid equations. The notes' figures of the subsonic and supersonic patterns are not reproduced; the worked examples are our own.

## Small disturbances of a fluid at rest

Start with a compressible fluid at rest, with pressure $P_0$ and density $\rho_0$, held in equilibrium by an external force density $\vec f_0$. With $\vec v = 0$ the Euler equation gives (p. 124)

<Formula label="Equilibrium" tex="\frac{\nabla P_0}{\rho_0} = \frac{\vec f_0}{\rho_0}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.5, eq. (6.91), p. 124" />

($P_0$ and $\rho_0$ are taken constant and uniform, so in fact $\vec f_0$ must vanish; the notes keep it to show that it cancels; our remark.) Now disturb the fluid slightly (p. 125):

<Formula label="Small disturbances" tex="P = P_0 + P', \qquad \rho = \rho_0 + \rho'" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.5, eqs. (6.92)–(6.93), p. 125" />

with $P' \ll P_0$ and $\rho' \ll \rho_0$. The disturbance sets the fluid moving with a velocity $\vec v(\vec r, t)$, also small. This is perturbation theory: keep only terms of first order in $P'$, $\rho'$ and $\vec v$ (p. 125). In the Euler equation (p. 125)

<Formula label="Expanding the Euler equation" tex="0 = \rho\,\frac{\partial\vec v}{\partial t} + \rho(\vec v\cdot\nabla)\vec v + \nabla P - \vec f_0 = \nabla P_0 - \vec f_0 + \rho_0\,\frac{\partial\vec v}{\partial t} + \nabla P' + \ldots" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.5, eq. (6.94), p. 125" />

the advective term is of order $\rho_0v^2$ and $\rho'\,\partial\vec v/\partial t$ is of order $\rho'v$; both are second order and dropped. The equilibrium (6.91) cancels the first two terms, leaving the **linearized Euler equation** (p. 125):

<Formula label="Linearized Euler equation" tex="\frac{\partial\vec v}{\partial t} = -\frac{\nabla P'}{\rho_0}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.5, eq. (6.95), p. 125" />

To the same order the continuity equation becomes (p. 125)

<Formula label="Linearized continuity equation" tex="\frac{\partial\rho'}{\partial t} = -\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\vec v\right) = -\rho_0\nabla\cdot\vec v" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.5, eq. (6.96), p. 125" />

## The bulk modulus and the wave equation

One more relation is needed, between $P'$ and $\rho'$: an equation of state (p. 125),

<Formula label="Equation of state for small disturbances" tex="\rho' = \frac{\rho_0}{B}\,P'" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.5, eq. (6.97), p. 125" />

The constant $B$ is the **bulk modulus**, a material's resistance to being compressed; the relation follows from its definition $B = \rho\,\partial P/\partial\rho \approx \rho_0P'/\rho'$ (p. 125). A stiff material has a large $B$: a big pressure change for a small change of density. Putting (6.97) into the continuity equation gives an equation for the pressure and velocity alone (p. 125):

<Formula label="Pressure disturbance and divergence" tex="\frac{\partial P'}{\partial t} = \frac{B}{\rho_0}\,\frac{\partial\rho'}{\partial t} = -B\,\nabla\cdot\vec v" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.5, eq. (6.98), p. 125" />

Differentiate once more in time and use the linearized Euler equation for $\partial\vec v/\partial t$ (p. 125):

<Formula label="Eliminating the velocity" tex="\frac{\partial^2P'}{\partial t^2} = -B\,\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial\vec v}{\partial t} = \frac{B}{\rho_0}\,\nabla^2P'" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.5, eq. (6.99), p. 125" />

This is a **wave equation** (p. 125):

<Formula label="Wave equation for the pressure" tex="\frac{\partial^2P'}{\partial t^2} - c_S^2\,\nabla^2P' = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.5, eq. (6.100), p. 125" />

Its solutions travel at the **speed of sound** (p. 125)

<Formula label="Speed of sound" tex="c_S = \sqrt{\frac{B}{\rho_0}}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.5, p. 125 (after eq. 6.100)" />

and since $\rho'$ is just a constant times $P'$, the density disturbance obeys the same equation (p. 125):

<Formula label="Wave equation for the density" tex="\frac{\partial^2\rho'}{\partial t^2} - c_S^2\,\nabla^2\rho' = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.5, eq. (6.101), p. 125" />

## The velocity field

For the velocity, first take the curl of the linearized Euler equation (p. 126):

<Formula label="Curl of the linearized Euler equation" tex="\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\nabla\times\vec v\right) = -\frac{1}{\rho_0}\,\nabla\times\nabla P' = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.5, eq. (6.102), p. 126" />

The vorticity does not change in time, so if it is zero to begin with, as it is for a fluid at rest that is then disturbed, it stays zero: a sound wave is an irrotational flow (p. 126). Now differentiate (6.95) in time and use (6.98) (p. 126):

<Formula label="Wave equation for the velocity, derivation" tex="\frac{\partial^2\vec v}{\partial t^2} = -\frac{1}{\rho_0}\,\nabla\left(\frac{\partial P'}{\partial t}\right) = \frac{B}{\rho_0}\,\nabla\left(\nabla\cdot\vec v\right) = \frac{B}{\rho_0}\left(\nabla^2\vec v + \nabla\times\left(\nabla\times\vec v\right)\right) = \frac{B}{\rho_0}\,\nabla^2\vec v" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.5, eq. (6.103), p. 126" />

where the last step used $\nabla\times\vec v = 0$. [The notes write $\nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec v) = \nabla^2\vec v - \nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec v)$; the identity has a plus sign, $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec v) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec v) - \nabla^2\vec v$. The sign does not matter here, since the term vanishes.] So the velocity obeys the same wave equation (p. 126):

<Formula label="Wave equation for the velocity" tex="\frac{\partial^2\vec v}{\partial t^2} - c_S^2\,\nabla^2\vec v = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.5, eq. (6.104), p. 126" />

Pressure, density and velocity disturbances are all **sound waves** moving at $c_S$. A plane wave travelling in the direction $\hat n$ is, for the pressure (p. 126),

<Formula label="A plane sound wave" tex="P' = P'\left(\vec r\cdot\hat n - c_St\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.5, eq. (6.105), p. 126" />

any shape, moving along $\hat n$ without change.

<WorkedExample title="The speed of sound in water and in air">
<Problem>
(a) Water has a bulk modulus of about $2.2 \times 10^9$ Pa and a density of 1000 kg/m³. Find its speed of sound. (b) For air at sea level, $P_0 = 101{,}325$ Pa and $\rho_0 = 1.225$ kg/m³. Compare $c_S$ with $B = P_0$ (the bulk modulus if the gas stayed at constant temperature) and with $B = 1.4\,P_0$ (if no heat flows during the rapid compressions, so that $P \propto \rho^{1.4}$).
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Water">
$c_S = \sqrt{2.2 \times 10^9/1000} \approx 1480$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Air">
With $B = P_0$: $c_S = \sqrt{101{,}325/1.225} \approx 288$ m/s. With $B = 1.4P_0$: $c_S \approx 288 \times \sqrt{1.4} \approx 340$ m/s, the measured value. In a sound wave the compressions are too quick for heat to flow, which is the no-heat-exchange (entropy-conserving) ideal fluid of Section 6.2. (This comparison is ours; the notes do not quote numbers.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The Mach number and shock cones

The derivation needed $\nabla\cdot\vec v \neq 0$. When may it be ignored, as in the previous sections? For a flow with typical speed $v_0$, the size of the compressibility terms is set by the **Mach number** (p. 126):

<Formula label="The Mach number" tex="M = \frac{v_0}{c_S}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.5, eq. (6.106), p. 126" />

If $M \ll 1$ the flow may be treated as incompressible, $\nabla\cdot\vec v = 0$ (p. 126). Wind at 30 m/s, for example, has $M \approx 0.09$ (our example).

Large $M$ leads to **shock waves** (pp. 126–127). Let a fluid flow at speed $v_0$ past a small disturbance that sends out sound. If $M < 1$ the flow is **subsonic**: sound outruns the flow, so the disturbance spreads in every direction, upstream as well. If $M > 1$ the flow is **supersonic**: each sound front is swept downstream faster than it spreads, and all of them stay inside a cone pointing downstream, whose half-angle $\gamma$ obeys (p. 127)

<Formula label="Half-angle of the Mach cone" tex="\sin\gamma = \frac{c_S}{v_0} = \frac{1}{M}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.5, unnumbered, p. 127" />

For a **supersonic aircraft**, turn the picture round: the air is still and the disturbance, the aircraft, moves through it faster than sound (p. 127). The cone trailing it is a pressure wave moving outward at $c_S$, heard on the ground as a **sonic boom**. Thunder is another shock wave: lightning heats air into a plasma so fast that it expands violently (p. 127).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Sound waves in the early universe">
In 8.902 the same steps, linearizing the Euler and continuity equations of a uniform medium, are done with gravity included: Poisson's equation for the perturbation adds a term to the wave equation, and the result is the [Jeans criterion](/courses/8-902/stability-jeans-toomre). Without gravity it is exactly this section's sound wave. Before recombination the photons and baryons formed one fluid whose pressure was mostly that of the radiation, with a sound speed of about $c/\sqrt3$; the sound waves in it are what produce the [acoustic peaks](/courses/8-902/cmb-anisotropies) in the cosmic microwave background.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Disturb a fluid at rest slightly and keep first-order terms: $\partial\vec v/\partial t = -\nabla P'/\rho_0$ and $\partial\rho'/\partial t = -\rho_0\nabla\cdot\vec v$.
- With the bulk modulus, $\rho' = (\rho_0/B)P'$, pressure and density disturbances obey the wave equation with the **speed of sound** $c_S = \sqrt{B/\rho_0}$.
- The vorticity stays zero, so the velocity obeys the same wave equation; a plane wave is $P'(\vec r\cdot\hat n - c_St)$.
- The **Mach number** $M = v_0/c_S$: for $M \ll 1$ the flow is effectively incompressible; for $M > 1$ disturbances stay in a cone with $\sin\gamma = 1/M$, the shock cone of a supersonic aircraft.
