Problem 4 also meets the Kepler problem of [Section 4.5](/courses/8-09/kepler-problem-hamilton-jacobi). The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 5, in our own words and in MIT's order. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own. As the sheet says of the last problem, it applies the Hamilton-Jacobi method to a motion whose answer you already know, which makes it a good test of the method.

A few things to know about the source. The sheet has printed page numbers; the problems are on pp. 2 to 4, cited below as "p. 2" and so on. The notes are cited by their printed page and equation number. The sheet has no figures. It points to a mechanics textbook in Problems 1, 2 and 4 and in the title of Problem 6; those pointers are left out, since every problem is stated in full without them. The sheet writes a Poisson bracket with square brackets, $[u, v]$; here it is written with curly brackets, $\{u, v\}$, as in the notes and the lessons. The two mean the same thing.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit named in the question. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `q0*cos(omega*t)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. It treats its letters as independent, so each question says which letters to type: for example `omega` for $\omega$, `mu` for $\mu$, `theta` for $\theta$, `v0` for $v_0$, `x1` for $x_1$. Type `exp(x)` for $e^x$, `log` for the natural logarithm, and `asinh`, `cosh`, `tanh` for the hyperbolic functions.

The points per problem are MIT's; their split between the parts of Problems 1, 2, 4 and 5 is MIT's too. Problem 6 is one question on the sheet; its split into three parts is ours. Several parts ask you to show something. Those parts have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The conventions are those of the notes and the lessons. The Poisson bracket is

<Formula label="The Poisson bracket" tex="\{u, v\} = \sum_i\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial v}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial v}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial p_i}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.2, eq. (4.43), p. 66" />

so that $\{q_i, p_j\} = \delta_{ij}$. A generating function of the second type, $F_2(q, P, t)$, gives the transformation by

<Formula label="Rules of a type-2 generating function" tex="p_i = \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial q_i}, \qquad Q_i = \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial P_i}, \qquad K = H + \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eq. (4.25), p. 63" />

and the other three types by the rules in the table of the notes (p. 64) and of the [lesson](/courses/8-09/canonical-transformations-generating-functions). An overdot is $d/dt$.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Canonical transformations" points={12} mit="Problem 1">

This problem practises transformations from $(q, p)$ to $(Q, P)$ and the four types of generating function: $F_1(q, Q, t)$, $F_2(q, P, t)$, $F_3(p, Q, t)$ and $F_4(p, P, t)$. The rules for $F_1$, which part (b) and part (c) need, are

<Formula label="Rules of a type-1 generating function" tex="p_i = \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial q_i}, \qquad P_i = -\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial Q_i}, \qquad K = H + \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eq. (4.13), p. 62" />

(The notes' eq. (4.13) prints $\partial F_1/\partial Q_i$ in the first rule, a misprint; the table on p. 64 has $\partial F_1/\partial q_i$.)

and for $F_3$ they are $q_i = -\partial F_3/\partial p_i$, $P_i = -\partial F_3/\partial Q_i$ (notes, table on p. 64).

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Find two generating functions, of two different types, for the identity transformation, $Q_i = q_i$ and $P_i = p_i$. The box asks for the one of type 3, $F_3(p, Q)$, for one degree of freedom, typed with `p` and `Q`.

<Hint>

A generating function must depend on one old and one new variable that are independent of each other. For the identity, is $Q$ independent of $q$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The type-2 function is in the notes' table. For type 3, try a product of $p$ and $Q$ and fix its sign with $q = -\partial F_3/\partial p$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1a-f3" label="$F_3(p, Q) =$" expr="-p*Q"
  vars={{ p: [0.5, 2], Q: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "p*Q", hint: "With F₃ = pQ the rules q = −∂F₃/∂p and P = −∂F₃/∂Q give Q = −q and P = −p: a change of sign of both, not the identity." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For the identity, $Q = q$ and $P = p$, so a pair $(q, Q)$ or $(p, P)$ is not independent and cannot be the variables of a generating function. That rules out $F_1(q, Q)$ and $F_4(p, P)$ and leaves $F_2(q, P)$ and $F_3(p, Q)$.

<Step title="Type 2">

Take $F_2 = \sum_i q_i P_i$. Then $p_i = \partial F_2/\partial q_i = P_i$ and $Q_i = \partial F_2/\partial P_i = q_i$, and $K = H$. This is the simplest case in the notes' table.

</Step>

<Step title="Type 3">

Take

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): a type-3 identity" tex="F_3(p, Q) = -\sum_i p_i Q_i" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Then $q_i = -\partial F_3/\partial p_i = Q_i$ and $P_i = -\partial F_3/\partial Q_i = p_i$. The table's simplest case, $F_3 = p_iQ_i$, gives $Q = -q$ and $P = -p$ instead; the minus sign turns it into the identity.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Neither function depends on time, so both give $K = H$, as the identity must. Trying the excluded types confirms the idea: $F_1(q, Q)$ would need $p = \partial F_1/\partial q$ to be a function of $q$ and $Q = q$ alone, which cannot hold for every $p$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Find a generating function $F_1(q, Q, t)$ for the transformation

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): the transformation" tex="Q = \frac{p}{t}, \qquad P = -q\,t" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2" />

Type it with `q`, `Q` and `t`.

<Hint>

Write $p$ in terms of $q$, $Q$ and $t$, and integrate $p = \partial F_1/\partial q$ over $q$. The constant of integration may depend on $Q$ and $t$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1b-f1" label="$F_1(q, Q, t) =$" expr="q*Q*t"
  vars={{ q: [0.5, 2], Q: [0.5, 2], t: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-q*Q*t", hint: "Check the sign with p = ∂F₁/∂q: this gives p = −Qt, but Q = p/t means p = +Qt." },
    { expr: "q*Q/t", hint: "This gives p = ∂F₁/∂q = Q/t, so Q = pt, not p/t." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Solve the first relation for $p$, integrate over $q$, and fix what the integration leaves free with the second relation.

<Step title="Integrate">

$p = Qt$, so $F_1 = qQt + g(Q, t)$ with some function $g$. Then $P = -\partial F_1/\partial Q = -qt - \partial g/\partial Q$, which equals $-qt$ only if $\partial g/\partial Q = 0$. A function of $t$ alone changes nothing in the transformation, so take $g = 0$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): the generating function" tex="F_1(q, Q, t) = q\,Q\,t" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Poisson brackets give an independent test: $\{Q, P\} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial q}\frac{\partial P}{\partial p} - \frac{\partial Q}{\partial p}\frac{\partial P}{\partial q} = 0 - \frac{1}{t}(-t) = 1$. The function depends on time, so the new Hamiltonian is $K = H + \partial F_1/\partial t = H + qQ = H - QP/t$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

For which values of the four constants $k$, $\ell$, $m$, $n$ is there a generating function $F_1(q, Q)$ for the transformation below?

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): the transformation" tex="Q = q^k p^\ell, \qquad P = q^m p^n" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2" />

Take $q > 0$ and $p > 0$ (our choice). Give $\ell$, $m$ and $n$ in terms of $k$ (type `k`), and say which value of $k$ has to be left out. Then (our addition) find $F_1(q, Q)$ for $k = 3$, typed with `q` and `Q`.

<Hint>

The transformation has no time in it, so it is canonical exactly when $\{Q, P\} = 1$. Work out the bracket: it is a number times a power of $q$ times a power of $p$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The bracket equals 1 for all $q$ and $p$ only if both powers are zero and the number is 1. That gives three conditions on four constants. Then ask when $q$ and $Q$ are independent, as $F_1(q, Q)$ needs.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $k = 3$, solve $Q = q^3p^2$ for $p$ and integrate $p = \partial F_1/\partial q$ over $q$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1c-l" label="$\ell =$" expr="k-1" vars={{ k: [2, 4] }} />

<Answer id="p1c-m" label="$m =$" expr="1-k" vars={{ k: [2, 4] }} />

<Answer id="p1c-n" label="$n =$" expr="2-k" vars={{ k: [2, 4] }} />

<Answer id="p1c-excluded" label="The value of $k$ that must be left out:" choices={["k = 0", "k = 2", "k = 1", "none: every k works"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="p1c-k3" label="For $k = 3$, $F_1(q, Q) =$" expr="-2*sqrt(Q/q)"
  vars={{ q: [0.5, 2], Q: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*sqrt(Q/q)", hint: "This gives p = ∂F₁/∂q = −√Q q^(−3/2), which is negative; from Q = q³p² with p > 0, p = +√Q q^(−3/2)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Use the Poisson-bracket test for a canonical transformation, then the condition that $F_1$ needs $q$ and $Q$ to be independent.

<Step title="The bracket">

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): the bracket" tex="\{Q, P\} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial q}\frac{\partial P}{\partial p} - \frac{\partial Q}{\partial p}\frac{\partial P}{\partial q} = (kn - \ell m)\,q^{k+m-1}\,p^{\ell+n-1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The test of the notes (eq. (4.52), p. 66) asks for $\{Q, P\} = 1$ at every point, so $k + m = 1$, $\ell + n = 1$ and $kn - \ell m = 1$. Put the first two into the third: $k(1 - \ell) - \ell(1 - k) = k - \ell = 1$. So

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): the exponents" tex="\ell = k - 1, \qquad m = 1 - k, \qquad n = 2 - k" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Which k">

$F_1(q, Q)$ needs $q$ and $Q$ to be independent, that is, $Q$ must depend on $p$: $\ell \neq 0$. So $k = 1$ is left out. There the transformation is $Q = q$, $P = p$, the identity, which Part (a) showed has no $F_1$. Every other $k$ works.

</Step>

<Step title="The generating function">

For any $k \neq 1$, $p = (Q q^{-k})^{1/(k-1)}$, and integrating over $q$ gives $F_1 = (1 - k)(Q/q)^{1/(k-1)}$ (no added function of $Q$ is needed: check $P = -\partial F_1/\partial Q$). For $k = 2$ this is $F_1 = -Q/q$, the example of eqs. (4.15)–(4.17) in the notes (p. 62). For $k = 3$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): our example" tex="Q = q^3p^2, \quad P = q^{-2}p^{-1}, \qquad F_1(q, Q) = -2\sqrt{\frac{Q}{q}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own example)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $k = 3$: $p = \partial F_1/\partial q = \sqrt{Q}\,q^{-3/2}$, so $p^2 = Q/q^3$, which is $Q = q^3p^2$. And $P = -\partial F_1/\partial Q = 1/\sqrt{Qq} = 1/(q^2 p)$, as it should be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

A particle of charge $q$ and mass $m$ moves in an electromagnetic field. (In this part $q$ is the charge; the coordinates are $x_i$ and the momenta $p_i$, canonical.) Its Hamiltonian is

<Formula label="Problem 1(d): a charge in a field" tex="H = \frac{1}{2m}\left(\vec p - q\vec A\right)^2 + q\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 2, eq. (1)" />

with the vector potential $\vec A(\vec x, t)$ and the scalar potential $\phi(\vec x, t)$. A gauge transformation changes the potentials by a function $f(\vec x, t)$,

<Formula label="Problem 1(d): the gauge transformation" tex="\vec A \to \vec A\,' = \vec A + \vec\nabla f(\vec x, t), \qquad \phi \to \phi' = \phi - \frac{\partial f(\vec x, t)}{\partial t}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 2" />

while $\vec p - q\vec A$ stays the same. Show that the change of the coordinates and momenta that goes with it is a canonical transformation, and find a generating function $F_2(\vec x, \vec P, t)$ for it. In the box, give it in one dimension, typed with `x`, `P`, `q` and `f` (for $f(x, t)$). Then (our addition) say what the new Hamiltonian $K$ is.

<Hint>

The coordinates do not change. What must the new momentum $\vec P$ be, if $\vec P - q\vec A\,' = \vec p - q\vec A$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Start from the identity $F_2 = \vec x\cdot\vec P$ and add a function of $\vec x$ and $t$ that shifts the momentum by the right amount. Then find $K = H + \partial F_2/\partial t$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1d-f2" label="In one dimension, $F_2(x, P, t) =$" expr="x*P - q*f"
  vars={{ x: [0.5, 2], P: [0.5, 2], q: [0.5, 2], f: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "x*P + q*f", hint: "This gives p = ∂F₂/∂x = P + q ∂f/∂x, so P = p − q ∂f/∂x: the momentum shifts the wrong way, and P − qA′ would differ from p − qA." },
    { expr: "x*P", hint: "This is the identity, P = p. Then P − qA′ = p − qA − q∇f, which is not the same as p − qA." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1d-k" label="The new Hamiltonian $K$ is:" choices={["(P − qA′)²/2m + qφ′", "H itself, unchanged in value", "(P − qA)²/2m + qφ", "(P − qA′)²/2m + qφ"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Keep the coordinates, shift the momentum by $q\vec\nabla f$, and find the generating function that does this. A transformation with a generating function is canonical.

<Step title="The transformation">

The coordinates do not change. From $\vec P - q\vec A\,' = \vec p - q\vec A$, the new momentum is $\vec P = \vec p + q\vec\nabla f$. The test of the notes confirms it is canonical: the coordinates keep their brackets; $\{x_i, P_j\} = \delta_{ij}$, since the extra term depends on the coordinates only; and $\{P_i, P_j\}$ is $q$ times the second derivative of $f$ with respect to $x_i$ and $x_j$, taken in the two orders and subtracted, which is zero.

</Step>

<Step title="The generating function">

<Formula label="Problem 1(d): the generating function" tex="F_2(\vec x, \vec P, t) = \vec x\cdot\vec P - q\,f(\vec x, t)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Then $p_i = \partial F_2/\partial x_i = P_i - q\,\partial f/\partial x_i$, and $\partial F_2/\partial P_i = x_i$ says the coordinates stay the same, as required.

</Step>

<Step title="The new Hamiltonian">

<Formula label="Problem 1(d): the new Hamiltonian" tex="K = H + \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{2m}\left(\vec P - q\vec A\,'\right)^2 + q\phi - q\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{2m}\left(\vec P - q\vec A\,'\right)^2 + q\phi'" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

using $\vec p - q\vec A = \vec P - q\vec A\,'$. The new Hamiltonian has the same form as the old one, with the new potentials: a gauge transformation of the potentials is a canonical transformation of the particle's variables.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With $f = 0$ the generating function is $\vec x\cdot\vec P$, the identity, as it must be. The velocity, $\partial K/\partial\vec P = (\vec P - q\vec A\,')/m$, is the same as before, $(\vec p - q\vec A)/m$: the particle moves in the same way, as a change of gauge must leave it.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Harmonic oscillator" points={7} mit="Problem 2">

Let $a$ be a constant and $(q, p)$ canonical variables. Consider the transformation

<Formula label="Problem 2: a complex transformation" tex="Q = p + iaq, \qquad P = \frac{p - iaq}{2ia}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Show that this transformation is canonical, using the theorem that tests it with Poisson brackets.

<Hint>

With one degree of freedom, $\{Q, Q\}$ and $\{P, P\}$ vanish anyway. Only $\{Q, P\}$ needs work.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The theorem of the notes: a transformation is canonical exactly when the new variables have the fundamental brackets,

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the test" tex="\{Q_i, Q_j\} = \{P_i, P_j\} = 0, \qquad \{Q_i, P_j\} = \delta_{ij}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.2, eq. (4.52), p. 66" />

<Step title="The bracket">

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the bracket" tex="\{Q, P\} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial q}\frac{\partial P}{\partial p} - \frac{\partial Q}{\partial p}\frac{\partial P}{\partial q} = ia\cdot\frac{1}{2ia} - 1\cdot\frac{-ia}{2ia} = \frac12 + \frac12 = 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

With one degree of freedom, $\{Q, Q\} = \{P, P\} = 0$ hold for any functions. So the transformation is canonical, for any $a \neq 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The bracket does not depend on $a$, and the factor $1/(2ia)$ in $P$ is exactly what makes it 1: without it $\{Q, P\}$ would be $2ia$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The transformation mixes $q$ and $p$ into two complex combinations. For the right $a$ these are the combinations in which the oscillator simply turns its phase, so the new Hamiltonian is a product $QP$.

<Step title="Invert">

Adding $Q = p + iaq$ and $2iaP = p - iaq$ gives $p = (Q + 2iaP)/2$; subtracting gives $q = (Q - 2iaP)/(2ia)$.

</Step>

<Step title="The new Hamiltonian">

$K = H$, since the transformation does not depend on time (eq. (4.29) of the notes, p. 64). Then

<Formula label="Problem 2(b): the Hamiltonian in the new variables" tex="K = \frac{(Q + 2iaP)^2}{8m} - \frac{m\omega^2}{8a^2}\left(Q - 2iaP\right)^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

With $a^2 = m^2\omega^2$ the two prefactors are equal, the $Q^2$ and $P^2$ terms cancel, and the cross terms add: $K = \frac{1}{8m}\cdot 8iaQP$. With $a = m\omega$,

<Formula label="Problem 2(b): the new Hamiltonian" tex="a = m\omega, \qquad K = i\omega\,QP" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The new motion">

$\dot Q = \partial K/\partial P = i\omega Q$ and $\dot P = -\partial K/\partial Q = -i\omega P$, so $Q = Q_0e^{i\omega t}$ and $P = P_0e^{-i\omega t}$, with $Q_0 = p_0 + im\omega q_0$ and $P_0 = (p_0 - im\omega q_0)/(2im\omega)$ from the transformation at $t = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Back to q and p">

<Formula label="Problem 2(b): the motion" tex="q(t) = \frac{Q - 2im\omega P}{2im\omega} = q_0\cos\omega t + \frac{p_0}{m\omega}\sin\omega t, \qquad p(t) = \frac{Q + 2im\omega P}{2} = p_0\cos\omega t - m\omega q_0\sin\omega t" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\dot q = -\omega q_0\sin\omega t + (p_0/m)\cos\omega t = p/m$, as Hamilton's equation $\dot q = \partial H/\partial p$ says. $K = i\omega QP = i\omega Q_0P_0$ is constant, and $i\omega Q_0P_0 = \frac{1}{2m}(p_0^2 + m^2\omega^2q_0^2)$ is the energy: real, as it must be. The other sign, $a = -m\omega$, gives $K = -i\omega QP$ and the same $q(t)$ and $p(t)$.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Projectile with Hamilton-Jacobi" points={11} mit="Problem 6">

Solve the motion of a point projectile of mass $m$ in a vertical plane with the Hamilton-Jacobi method: find both the equation of its path and how its coordinates depend on time. It is fired at $t = 0$ from the origin with speed $v_0$, at an angle $\theta$ above the horizontal. We take $x$ horizontal and $y$ up (our choice), so that

<Formula label="Problem 6: the Hamiltonian" tex="H = \frac{p_x^2 + p_y^2}{2m} + mgy" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 4 (our own set-up)" />

The split of the problem into three parts, and of its points, is ours.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Write the Hamilton-Jacobi equation and solve it by separating the variables. Name the separation constants $\alpha_x = p_x$ and $E$, and give $\alpha_x$ in terms of the launch data, typed with `m`, `v0` and `theta`.

<Hint>

$H$ does not depend on time, so $S = W(x, y) - Et$ (eq. (4.129) of the notes). $x$ is cyclic, so $W = \alpha_x x + W_y(y)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The equation for $W_y$ is $\left(\alpha_x^2 + (dW_y/dy)^2\right)/(2m) + mgy = E$. Solve for $dW_y/dy$ and integrate: the integrand is a square root of a linear function of $y$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6a-alpha" label="$\alpha_x =$" expr="m*v0*cos(theta)"
  vars={{ m: [0.5, 2], v0: [2, 5], theta: [0.2, 0.7] }} names={{ v0: "v_0", theta: "\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m*v0*sin(theta)", hint: "θ is measured from the horizontal, so the horizontal momentum is m v₀ cos θ." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a Hamiltonian without time, the principal function separates into $W - Et$, and the cyclic coordinate $x$ separates off as $\alpha_x x$.

<Step title="The equation">

With $p_x = \partial S/\partial x$ and $p_y = \partial S/\partial y$, the Hamilton-Jacobi equation $H + \partial S/\partial t = 0$ (eq. (4.104) of the notes, p. 73) reads

<Formula label="Problem 6(a): the Hamilton-Jacobi equation" tex="\frac{1}{2m}\left[\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial x}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial y}\right)^2\right] + mgy + \frac{\partial S}{\partial t} = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

The notes' form for a Hamiltonian without time is

<Formula label="Problem 6(a): separating time" tex="S(q, \alpha, t) = W(q, \alpha) - \alpha_1 t" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.129), p. 76" />

with $\alpha_1 = E$ here.

</Step>

<Step title="Separate">

With $S = \alpha_x x + W_y(y) - Et$: $dW_y/dy = \sqrt{2mE - \alpha_x^2 - 2m^2gy}$, and

<Formula label="Problem 6(a): the principal function" tex="S = \alpha_x x - \frac{\left(2mE - \alpha_x^2 - 2m^2gy\right)^{3/2}}{3m^2g} - Et" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

At the launch, $p_x = m v_0\cos\theta$, and $p_x = \partial S/\partial x = \alpha_x$ is constant, so $\alpha_x = mv_0\cos\theta$; and $E = \frac12mv_0^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\partial S/\partial y = \sqrt{2mE - \alpha_x^2 - 2m^2gy}$, and $(\partial S/\partial x)^2 + (\partial S/\partial y)^2 = 2mE - 2m^2gy$: with the $mgy$ term, $H = E$. At $y = 0$, $p_y = \sqrt{m^2v_0^2 - m^2v_0^2\cos^2\theta} = mv_0\sin\theta$, the vertical launch momentum.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
