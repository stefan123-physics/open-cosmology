Chapter 3 of Stewart's notes returns to a subject that MIT teaches in detail in 8.03, the physics of waves: [coupled oscillators and their normal modes](/courses/8-03/coupled-oscillators-normal-modes). What is new here is the language. Instead of masses on springs written in Cartesian coordinates, the chapter works with any set of generalised coordinates $q_1, \ldots, q_n$, and shows that near a minimum of the potential the problem always takes the same form: two constant symmetric matrices, one for the kinetic and one for the potential energy, and a so-called generalised eigenvalue problem. This page covers the chapter's opening and Section 3.1 (notes pp. 52–55); the next section turns the result into a change of coordinates. (The source is the course's typed lecture notes, without videos; this page follows them in order, in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Expanding about a minimum

Most equations of motion met so far in the course are nonlinear. They become linear if the system stays close to a point $\vec q_0$ where the potential $V(q_1, \ldots, q_n)$ has a minimum. Write each coordinate as its equilibrium value plus a small displacement, $q_i = q_{0i} + \eta_i$, and expand the potential in powers of the $\eta_i$. From here on a repeated index is summed (p. 52):

<Formula label="Potential near the minimum" tex="V(q_1, \ldots, q_n) = V(q_{01}, \ldots, q_{0n}) + \frac{\partial V}{\partial q_i}(\vec q_0)\, \eta_i + \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial q_i \partial q_j}(\vec q_0)\, \eta_i \eta_j + \ldots" cite="reading1" at="Chapter 3 introduction, eq. (3.1), p. 52" />

The derivatives are taken at the minimum $\vec q_0$ (the notes mark this with a vertical bar and a subscript 0). Three simplifications follow. The first derivatives vanish, because $\vec q_0$ is a minimum. The constant $V(\vec q_0)$ can be set to zero, which only fixes where energy is counted from. And the second derivatives at the minimum are just numbers, which the notes call $V_{ij}$. Since mixed partial derivatives do not depend on the order of differentiation, the matrix $\hat V$ they form is symmetric.

The kinetic energy, for coordinates that do not depend on time explicitly, is a quadratic form in the velocities, $T = \tfrac12 m_{ij}(q)\,\dot q_i \dot q_j$, with coefficients that may depend on where the system is. Since $\dot q_i = \dot\eta_i$ is already small, only the value of $m_{ij}$ at the minimum matters to second order (p. 52):

<Formula label="Kinetic energy near the minimum" tex="T = \tfrac12\, m_{ij}(q_1, \ldots, q_n)\,\dot q_i \dot q_j = \tfrac12\, m_{ij}(q_{01}, \ldots, q_{0n})\,\dot\eta_i \dot\eta_j + \mathcal{O}(\eta\dot\eta^2)" cite="reading1" at="Chapter 3 introduction, eq. (3.2), p. 52" />

The constants $m_{ij}(\vec q_0)$ are named $T_{ij}$. To quadratic order in the displacements the Lagrangian and the Euler-Lagrange equations are then (p. 52):

<Formula label="Lagrangian to quadratic order" tex="L = \tfrac12\,\big(T_{ij}\,\dot\eta_i \dot\eta_j - V_{ij}\,\eta_i \eta_j\big)" cite="reading1" at="Chapter 3 introduction, eq. (3.3), p. 52" />

<Formula label="Linearised equations of motion" tex="T_{ij}\,\ddot\eta_j + V_{ij}\,\eta_j = 0" cite="reading1" at="Chapter 3 introduction, eq. (3.4), p. 52" />

These are $n$ linear equations, but coupled: each $\ddot\eta_j$ is tied to several of the displacements, through $\hat V$ and, in general, through $\hat T$ as well. Both matrices are real and symmetric.

## One frequency for all coordinates

To solve the coupled equations, look for a motion in which every coordinate oscillates with the same frequency (p. 53):

<Formula label="Trial solution" tex="\eta_i = a_i\, e^{-i\omega t}" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.1, eq. (3.5), p. 53" />

The amplitudes $a_i$ may be complex. (The notes use an upright i for $\sqrt{-1}$ to keep it apart from the index $i$; on this page the context tells them apart.) Each time derivative brings down a factor $-i\omega$, so two of them give $-\omega^2$, and the equations of motion become $V_{ij}a_j = \omega^2 T_{ij}a_j$, or in matrix form, with $\lambda = \omega^2$ (p. 53):

<Formula label="Generalised eigenvalue problem" tex="\hat V \cdot \vec a = \lambda\, \hat T \cdot \vec a" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.1, eq. (3.7), p. 53" />

This is almost an eigenvalue problem, but not quite: acting with $\hat V$ on $\vec a$ does not give back a multiple of $\vec a$, it gives a multiple of $\hat T\cdot\vec a$. Written as $(\hat V - \lambda\hat T)\cdot\vec a = 0$, it has a solution other than $\vec a = 0$ only if the matrix in brackets cannot be inverted (p. 53):

<Formula label="Condition for the frequencies" tex="\det(\hat V - \lambda \hat T) = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.1, eq. (3.9), p. 53" />

The determinant is a polynomial of degree $n$ in $\lambda$, so there are $n$ roots $\lambda_\alpha$, $\alpha = 1, \ldots, n$. For each root the equation $(\hat V - \lambda_\alpha\hat T)\cdot\vec a^{(\alpha)} = 0$ gives an eigenvector (p. 53):

<Formula label="The eigenvectors" tex="\hat V \cdot \vec a^{(\alpha)} = \lambda_\alpha\, \hat T \cdot \vec a^{(\alpha)}" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.1, eq. (3.10), p. 53" />

A bookkeeping rule for the rest of the chapter: the coordinate indices $i, j$ are still summed when repeated, but the mode label $\alpha$ is never summed unless a sum is written out.

## When the kinetic matrix is a multiple of the unit matrix

The notes first take the easiest case, $T_{ij} = m\,\delta_{ij}$: for instance, particles of equal mass $m$ in Cartesian coordinates. The equations of motion are $m\ddot\eta_i + V_{ij}\eta_j = 0$, and the problem is now a standard eigenvalue problem (p. 53):

<Formula label="Standard eigenvalue problem" tex="\hat V \cdot \vec a^{(\alpha)} = m\, \lambda_\alpha\, \vec a^{(\alpha)}" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.1, eq. (3.12), p. 53" />

The results are those of [real symmetric matrices](/courses/18-06/symmetric-matrices-positive-definite). The $\lambda_\alpha = \omega_\alpha^2$ are real and non-negative, and their square roots $\omega_\alpha$ are the **normal mode frequencies**. The eigenvectors are orthogonal and can be normalised (p. 54):

<Formula label="Orthonormal eigenvectors (unit kinetic matrix)" tex="m\, \vec a^{(\beta)\dagger} \cdot \vec a^{(\alpha)} = \delta_{\alpha\beta}" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.1, eq. (3.13), p. 54" />

(or without the $m$, if one prefers unit vectors). Dotting $\vec a^{(\alpha)\dagger}$ into the eigenvalue equation then gives $\lambda_\alpha = \vec a^{(\alpha)\dagger}\cdot\hat V\cdot\vec a^{(\alpha)}$ (eq. 3.14, p. 54). Each eigenvector, multiplied by its own time dependence, is a **normal mode**: a motion in which every coordinate oscillates at the single frequency $\omega_\alpha$ (p. 54):

<Formula label="Normal mode solution" tex="\vec\eta^{(\alpha)} = \vec a^{(\alpha)}\, e^{-i\omega_\alpha t}" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.1, eq. (3.15), p. 54" />

There are $n$ of them, and because the equations are linear, the general motion is a superposition with complex constants $C_\alpha$ set by the initial conditions; the physical coordinates are the real part (p. 54):

<Formula label="General solution" tex="\vec\eta = \sum_\alpha C_\alpha\, \vec\eta^{(\alpha)}" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.1, eq. (3.16), p. 54" />

Each complex $C_\alpha$ carries two real numbers, an amplitude and a phase, so the $n$ modes supply the $2n$ constants that $n$ second-order equations need.

## Why the eigenvalues are real and the modes orthogonal

The notes prove these statements directly (p. 54). Dot $\vec a^{(\beta)\dagger}$ into the eigenvalue equation for mode $\alpha$:

<Formula label="Projected eigenvalue equation" tex="\vec a^{(\beta)\dagger} \cdot \hat V \cdot \vec a^{(\alpha)} = m\,\lambda_\alpha\, \vec a^{(\beta)\dagger} \cdot \vec a^{(\alpha)}" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.1, eq. (3.17), p. 54" />

Now take the Hermitian conjugate of the same equation written for the pair the other way round. Because $\hat V$ is real and symmetric, $\hat V^\dagger = \hat V$, and the left side comes out the same, while the right side carries $\lambda_\beta^\star$. Subtracting the two (p. 54):

<Formula label="Difference of the two forms" tex="(\lambda_\alpha - \lambda_\beta^\star)\, \vec a^{(\beta)\dagger} \cdot \vec a^{(\alpha)} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.1, eq. (3.18), p. 54" />

Two conclusions follow. With $\beta = \alpha$, the product $\vec a^{(\alpha)\dagger}\cdot\vec a^{(\alpha)}$ is a sum of squared moduli and is positive, so $\lambda_\alpha = \lambda_\alpha^\star$: the eigenvalue is real. With $\lambda_\alpha \neq \lambda_\beta$, the bracket is not zero, so the product of the eigenvectors must be: they are orthogonal. If two different modes happen to share an eigenvalue, any combination of their eigenvectors is again an eigenvector, and one can simply pick two orthogonal ones. Finally, with $\beta = \alpha$ and the normalisation, $\lambda_\alpha = \vec a^{(\alpha)\dagger}\cdot\hat V\cdot\vec a^{(\alpha)}$ is the curvature of the potential along the mode. At a minimum (rather than a saddle point or a maximum) the potential curves upward in every direction, so $\lambda_\alpha \geq 0$.

## When the kinetic matrix is not diagonal

In general coordinates $\hat T$ is not diagonal, as in the double pendulum below. The notes list what survives (pp. 54–55). The $\lambda_\alpha = \omega_\alpha^2$ are still real and non-negative. What changes is the meaning of "orthogonal": eigenvectors with different eigenvalues satisfy $\vec a^{(\beta)\dagger}\cdot\hat T\cdot\vec a^{(\alpha)} = 0$ (eq. 3.19, p. 54), with the kinetic matrix as a weight between them, and the normalisation becomes (p. 55):

<Formula label="Orthonormality with the kinetic matrix" tex="\vec a^{(\beta)\dagger} \cdot \hat T \cdot \vec a^{(\alpha)} = \delta_{\alpha\beta}" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.1, eq. (3.20), p. 55" />

For $\hat T = m\hat 1$ this is the earlier condition again. As before, $\lambda_\alpha = \vec a^{(\alpha)\dagger}\cdot\hat V\cdot\vec a^{(\alpha)}$ (eq. 3.21), the modes are $\vec\eta^{(\alpha)} = \vec a^{(\alpha)}e^{-i\omega_\alpha t}$ (eq. 3.22), and the general solution is $\vec\eta = \sum_\alpha C_\alpha\vec\eta^{(\alpha)}$ (eq. 3.23), all on p. 55.

The proof runs as before, with one extra ingredient (p. 55). Dotting $\vec a^{(\beta)\dagger}$ into the eigenvector equation, conjugating and subtracting gives

<Formula label="Difference of the two forms, general kinetic matrix" tex="(\lambda_\alpha - \lambda_\beta^\star)\, \vec a^{(\beta)\dagger} \cdot \hat T \cdot \vec a^{(\alpha)} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.1, eq. (3.25), p. 55" />

For $\beta = \alpha$ we now need $\vec a^{(\alpha)\dagger}\cdot\hat T\cdot\vec a^{(\alpha)} > 0$. Physics supplies it: $\hat T$ is real and symmetric, and the kinetic energy $\tfrac12\dot{\vec\eta}\cdot\hat T\cdot\dot{\vec\eta}$ is positive for any motion at all, so $\hat T$ is positive definite (its real and imaginary parts each give a positive contribution). Hence $\lambda_\alpha$ is real; for different eigenvalues the weighted product vanishes; and at a minimum of $V$, $\lambda_\alpha \geq 0$. (In passing the notes say $\vec a^{(\alpha)\dagger}\cdot\hat V\cdot\vec a^{(\alpha)}$ "is positive, so $\lambda_\alpha > 0$", and then, in the next sentence, $\lambda_\alpha \geq 0$. The second is the right statement: a zero eigenvalue is allowed, and belongs to a direction in which the potential is flat, such as a free translation or rotation.)

A useful way to see the whole construction: since $\hat T$ is positive definite, it defines a dot product of its own, and in that dot product $\hat V$ behaves like a symmetric matrix. Everything known about real symmetric matrices carries over, with "orthogonal" read as "orthogonal with $\hat T$ as weight".

## A worked example

<WorkedExample title="The double pendulum">
<Problem>
A pendulum of length $l$ with a bob of mass $m$ hangs from a fixed point, and a second, identical pendulum hangs from the first bob. Using the two angles from the vertical, $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$, as generalised coordinates, find $\hat T$ and $\hat V$ for small swings, the two normal mode frequencies and the mode shapes. Then check the orthogonality relation, and give the frequencies for $l = 0.50$ m and $g = 9.81$ m/s².
</Problem>
<Step title="The two matrices">
The upper bob moves with speed $l\dot\theta_1$. The lower bob's velocity is the sum of $l\dot\theta_1$ and $l\dot\theta_2$ along two directions at angle $\theta_1 - \theta_2$, so its squared speed is $l^2(\dot\theta_1^2 + \dot\theta_2^2 + 2\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2))$. At the minimum, $\theta_1 = \theta_2 = 0$, the cosine is 1, and $T = \tfrac12 ml^2(2\dot\theta_1^2 + 2\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2 + \dot\theta_2^2)$. Measured from their lowest positions, the bobs are raised by $l(1 - \cos\theta_1)$ and $l(1 - \cos\theta_1) + l(1 - \cos\theta_2)$, so $V = mgl(3 - 2\cos\theta_1 - \cos\theta_2)$, which is zero at the minimum as the notes' convention asks, and $V \approx \tfrac12 mgl(2\theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2)$. Reading off the coefficients, $\hat T = ml^2\,(2\ 1;\ 1\ 1)$ and $\hat V = mgl\,(2\ 0;\ 0\ 1)$. The potential is diagonal in these coordinates; the coupling sits entirely in the kinetic matrix.
</Step>
<Step title="The frequencies">
Write $\lambda = \mu\, g/l$. Dividing by $mgl$, the condition $\det(\hat V - \lambda\hat T) = 0$ becomes $(2 - 2\mu)(1 - \mu) - \mu^2 = 0$, that is $\mu^2 - 4\mu + 2 = 0$, so $\mu = 2 \mp \sqrt 2 \approx 0.586$ and $3.414$. The frequencies are $\omega_1^2 = (g/l)(2 - \sqrt 2)$ and $\omega_2^2 = (g/l)(2 + \sqrt 2)$, and their ratio is $\omega_2/\omega_1 = 1 + \sqrt 2 \approx 2.414$, because $(2 + \sqrt 2)/(2 - \sqrt 2) = 3 + 2\sqrt 2 = (1 + \sqrt 2)^2$. With $g/l = 19.62$ s⁻², $\omega_1 \approx 3.39$ rad/s and $\omega_2 \approx 8.18$ rad/s.
</Step>
<Step title="The mode shapes">
The first row of $(\hat V - \lambda\hat T)\cdot\vec a = 0$ reads $(2 - 2\mu)a_1 - \mu a_2 = 0$, so $a_2/a_1 = (2 - 2\mu)/\mu$. For $\mu = 2 - \sqrt 2$ this is $+\sqrt 2$: in the slow mode both pendulums swing the same way, the lower one about 1.41 times further out. For $\mu = 2 + \sqrt 2$ it is $-\sqrt 2$: in the fast mode they swing in opposite directions.
</Step>
<Step title="Orthogonality">
The ordinary dot product of $(1, \sqrt 2)$ and $(1, -\sqrt 2)$ is $1 - 2 = -1$: the mode shapes are not perpendicular. With the kinetic matrix in between, $(1, \sqrt 2)\cdot(2\ 1;\ 1\ 1)\cdot(1, -\sqrt 2) = (1, \sqrt 2)\cdot(2 - \sqrt 2,\ 1 - \sqrt 2) = 2 - \sqrt 2 + \sqrt 2 - 2 = 0$, exactly as the theory requires. To normalise, divide each vector by the square root of its own weighted length: $\vec a^{(\alpha)\dagger}\cdot\hat T\cdot\vec a^{(\alpha)} = ml^2(4 \pm 2\sqrt 2)$ for the two shapes.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Near a minimum $\vec q_0$ of the potential, write $q_i = q_{0i} + \eta_i$. To second order $L = \tfrac12(T_{ij}\dot\eta_i\dot\eta_j - V_{ij}\eta_i\eta_j)$, with constant, real, symmetric matrices: $V_{ij}$ the second derivatives of $V$ at the minimum, $T_{ij}$ the kinetic coefficients there.
- The equations $T_{ij}\ddot\eta_j + V_{ij}\eta_j = 0$ are linear but coupled. The trial solution $\eta_i = a_ie^{-i\omega t}$ turns them into $\hat V\cdot\vec a = \lambda\hat T\cdot\vec a$ with $\lambda = \omega^2$, solved by $\det(\hat V - \lambda\hat T) = 0$.
- The $n$ roots $\lambda_\alpha$ are real, and non-negative at a minimum; the $\omega_\alpha$ are the normal mode frequencies. Eigenvectors with different eigenvalues are orthogonal with $\hat T$ as weight and are normalised to $\vec a^{(\beta)\dagger}\cdot\hat T\cdot\vec a^{(\alpha)} = \delta_{\alpha\beta}$; then $\lambda_\alpha = \vec a^{(\alpha)\dagger}\cdot\hat V\cdot\vec a^{(\alpha)}$.
- The general small motion is a superposition $\sum_\alpha C_\alpha\vec a^{(\alpha)}e^{-i\omega_\alpha t}$, with the real part taken.
- For an equal double pendulum $\omega^2 = (g/l)(2 \mp \sqrt 2)$, a frequency ratio of $1 + \sqrt 2$; the modes $(1, \pm\sqrt 2)$ are orthogonal only with the kinetic matrix as weight.
