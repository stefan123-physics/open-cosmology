This is the second page on Section 6.2 of the notes (Sections 6.2.2 and 6.2.3, pp. 112–115). The [first page](/courses/8-09/continuity-equation-vorticity) derived the continuity equation; here Newton's law gives the equation for the velocity, and from the two come the conservation of momentum and energy. The notes' figure of the fluid box is not reproduced, and the worked examples are our own.

## The ideal fluid and the pressure force

An **ideal fluid** loses no energy to internal friction (no viscosity) and passes no heat from one part to another (no thermal conduction) (p. 112). The forces on an element are then pressure and external forces such as gravity.

Take a small box $\delta V = \delta x\,\delta y\,\delta z$. In the $x$ direction the fluid on the left face pushes it with $P(x)\,\delta y\,\delta z$ and the fluid on the right face pushes back with $P(x + \delta x)\,\delta y\,\delta z$ (p. 112):

<Formula label="Pressure force on a box, x direction" tex="\delta F_x = \frac{P(x) - P(x + \delta x)}{\delta x}\,\delta y\,\delta z\,\delta x = -\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}\,\delta V" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.2, eq. (6.30), p. 112" />

and with all three directions (p. 112),

<Formula label="Pressure force on a fluid element" tex="\delta\vec F = -\nabla P\,\delta V" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.2, eq. (6.31), p. 112" />

A uniform pressure squeezes the element equally from all sides and gives no net force; only its gradient pushes, from high pressure towards low.

## The Euler equation

External forces are written per unit volume, $\delta\vec F = \vec f\,\delta V$ with **force density** $\vec f$. Newton's law $m\vec a = \vec F$ for the element, whose mass is $\rho\,\delta V$ and whose acceleration is the total derivative of its velocity, reads (p. 112)

<Formula label="Newton's law for a fluid element" tex="\rho\,\delta V\,\frac{d\vec v}{dt} = \left(-\nabla P + \vec f\right)\delta V" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.2, eq. (6.32), p. 112" />

Writing out $d/dt = \partial/\partial t + \vec v\cdot\nabla$ gives the **Euler equation** (p. 112):

<Formula label="The Euler equation" tex="\frac{\partial\vec v}{\partial t} + \left(\vec v\cdot\nabla\right)\vec v + \frac{\nabla P}{\rho} = \frac{\vec f}{\rho}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.2, eq. (6.33), p. 112" />

The second term, the **advective** term, appears because the element moves to places where the velocity field is different. It holds products of velocity components, $v_j\,\partial v_i/\partial x_j$, so the equation is non-linear (p. 113).

The notes give two rewritings (pp. 112–113).

**1. A potential force.** Often $\vec f = -\rho\nabla\Phi$, with $\Phi$ a potential energy per unit mass; for gravity $\Phi = gz$ and $\vec f = -\rho g\hat z$. Then (p. 112)

<Formula label="Euler equation with a potential" tex="\frac{\partial\vec v}{\partial t} + \left(\vec v\cdot\nabla\right)\vec v + \frac{\nabla P}{\rho} + \nabla\Phi = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.2, eq. (6.34), p. 112" />

**2. With the vorticity.** The vector identity $\vec v\times\vec\Omega = \vec v\times(\nabla\times\vec v) = \nabla(v^2/2) - (\vec v\cdot\nabla)\vec v$ trades the advective term for a gradient and a cross product (p. 113):

<Formula label="Euler equation with the vorticity" tex="\frac{\partial\vec v}{\partial t} + \nabla\left(\frac{v^2}{2} + \Phi\right) + \frac{\nabla P}{\rho} - \vec v\times\vec\Omega = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.2, eq. (6.35), p. 113" />

If $\rho$ is constant, $\nabla P/\rho$ is a gradient too. Taking the curl kills every gradient, since $\nabla\times(\nabla h) = 0$ for any $h$, and leaves an equation for the vorticity alone (p. 113):

<Formula label="How vorticity evolves (constant density)" tex="\frac{\partial\vec\Omega}{\partial t} = \nabla\times\left(\vec v\times\vec\Omega\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.2, eq. (6.36), p. 113" />

This result is used again in [Section 6.4](/courses/8-09/potential-flow) to show that a flow that starts without vorticity keeps none.

## Boundary conditions and the fifth equation

The partial differential equations need boundary conditions. For an ideal fluid the only one is that it cannot pass through a solid surface (p. 113). At a wall at rest, with normal $\hat n$,

<Formula label="Boundary condition at a fixed wall" tex="\vec v\cdot\hat n = 0 \quad \text{at the surface}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.2, eq. (6.37), p. 113" />

and at a moving wall the normal velocities must agree, $\vec v\cdot\hat n = v_{\text{surface}}$ (eq. 6.38). Nothing restricts the velocity along the wall; that changes once viscosity is included in Section 6.6.

Counting: continuity is one equation and Euler three, four in all, for five unknowns ($\rho$, $P$ and the three components of $\vec v$) (p. 113). For an ideal fluid the fifth is that no heat is exchanged, so the entropy of each element is conserved:

<Formula label="Entropy conservation" tex="\frac{dS}{dt} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.2, eq. (6.39), p. 113" />

In effect this supplies an equation of state $P = P(\rho, S)$ linking pressure to density. The notes' simple example is an ideal gas at constant temperature, $P = \rho R'T$ (p. 113). [Strictly, a gas kept at constant temperature exchanges heat; it serves here as the simplest example of a relation $P(\rho)$.]

## Conservation of momentum

Momentum is the first conservation law (p. 113). The mass flux $\rho\vec v$ is also the **momentum density**, as $\vec p = m\vec v$ for a particle. Take $\partial(\rho v_i)/\partial t = (\partial\rho/\partial t)v_i + \rho\,\partial v_i/\partial t$ and replace the two time derivatives using the continuity and Euler equations, with repeated indices summed (p. 113):

<Formula label="Time derivative of the momentum density" tex="\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\rho v_i\right) = -v_i\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\left(\rho v_j\right) + \rho\left(-v_j\,\frac{\partial v_i}{\partial x_j} - \frac{1}{\rho}\,\frac{\partial P}{\partial x_i}\right) + f_i" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.3, eq. (6.40), p. 113" />

The two velocity terms combine into one derivative of $\rho v_iv_j$ (p. 113):

<Formula label="Momentum conservation in components" tex="\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\rho v_i\right) + \frac{\partial P}{\partial x_i} + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\left(\rho v_iv_j\right) = f_i" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.3, eq. (6.41), p. 113" />

Writing $\partial P/\partial x_i = \partial(P\delta_{ij})/\partial x_j$ collects everything into the **stress tensor** of an ideal fluid (p. 114),

<Formula label="Stress tensor of an ideal fluid" tex="T_{ij} = P\delta_{ij} + \rho v_iv_j" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.3, eq. (6.42), p. 114" />

the **momentum flux density**: the amount of the $i$-th component of momentum carried across unit area facing the $j$ direction, per unit time. It is symmetric. In vector form (p. 114),

<Formula label="Momentum conservation" tex="\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\rho\vec v\right) + \nabla\cdot\mathbb{T} = \vec f" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.3, eq. (6.43), p. 114" />

The momentum density changes only by its flux and by the external force density, which acts as a source. The pattern repeats one rank up (p. 114): the density $\rho$ is a scalar whose flux $\rho\vec v$ is a vector; the momentum density $\rho\vec v$ is a vector whose flux $\mathbb{T}$ is a tensor.

<WorkedExample title="A water jet on a wall">
<Problem>
A horizontal jet of water, density 1000 kg/m³, with speed 5 m/s and cross-section 2 cm², hits a vertical wall head-on and spreads out sideways along it. Using the momentum flux, find the force on the wall (beyond the atmospheric pressure, which acts on all sides).
</Problem>
<Step title="The momentum flux in the jet">
With the jet along $x$, the $x$-momentum carried in the $x$ direction per unit area per unit time is $T_{xx} = P + \rho v_x^2$. The excess over the surrounding pressure is $\rho v^2 = 1000 \times 5^2 = 25{,}000$ Pa.
</Step>
<Step title="The force">
Draw a fixed surface around the place where the jet hits. In a steady state $\partial(\rho\vec v)/\partial t = 0$, so the $x$-momentum flowing in each second, $\rho v^2A = 25{,}000 \times 2 \times 10^{-4} = 5$ N, must be removed by the wall (the water leaves sideways, with no $x$-momentum). The wall pushes the water with 5 N, and by Newton's third law the jet pushes the wall with 5 N, about the weight of half a kilogram.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Conservation of energy and Bernoulli's equation

Energy conservation also comes from the Euler equation, written with the total derivative, $d\vec v/dt + \nabla P/\rho + \nabla\Phi = 0$ (p. 114). Take its dot product with $\rho\vec v\,\delta V$ (p. 114):

<Formula label="Euler equation dotted with the velocity" tex="\delta V\,\rho\,\vec v\cdot\frac{d\vec v}{dt} + \delta V\,\vec v\cdot\nabla P + \delta V\,\rho\,\vec v\cdot\nabla\Phi = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.3, eq. (6.44), p. 114" />

Since the mass $\rho\,\delta V$ is constant it can be moved inside total time derivatives, and $\vec v\cdot\nabla\Phi = d\Phi/dt - \partial\Phi/\partial t$ (p. 114):

<Formula label="Kinetic and external potential energy" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2\,\delta V\right) + \left(\vec v\cdot\nabla P\right)\delta V + \frac{d}{dt}\left(\rho\Phi\,\delta V\right) - \rho\,\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t}\,\delta V = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.3, eq. (6.45), p. 114" />

The pressure term is turned into a time derivative with (p. 114)

<Formula label="Time derivative of P δV" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(P\,\delta V\right) = \frac{\partial P}{\partial t}\,\delta V + \left(\vec v\cdot\nabla P\right)\delta V + P\left(\nabla\cdot\vec v\right)\delta V" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.3, eq. (6.46), p. 114" />

where $d\,\delta V/dt = (\nabla\cdot\vec v)\,\delta V$ was used. Eliminating $(\vec v\cdot\nabla P)\,\delta V$ (p. 114):

<Formula label="Energy balance before the internal energy" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2\,\delta V + \rho\Phi\,\delta V + P\,\delta V\right) = \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial t} + \rho\,\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t}\right)\delta V + P\,\nabla\cdot\vec v\,\delta V" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.3, eq. (6.47), p. 114" />

On the left are the kinetic energy, the external potential energy and $P\,\delta V$, an internal potential energy of the pressure. The terms with partial time derivatives act as sources (p. 114). The last term is the work the element does on its surroundings when it expands against their pressure, $W_u$; the notes write it as the loss of an internal potential energy $U$ per unit mass (p. 114):

<Formula label="Work done in expanding" tex="\frac{dW_u}{dt} = P\,\frac{d(\delta V)}{dt} = P\,\nabla\cdot\vec v\,\delta V \equiv -\frac{d}{dt}\left(U\,\delta m\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.3, eq. (6.48), p. 114" />

Given the equation of state, $U$ follows from the density or from the pressure, using $\delta m = \rho\,\delta V$ fixed so that $d(\delta V) = -\delta m\,d\rho/\rho^2$ (p. 115):

<Formula label="Internal energy per unit mass" tex="U = -\int d(\delta V)\,\frac{P}{\delta m} = \int_{\rho_0}^{\rho} d\rho'\,\frac{P(\rho')}{\rho'^2} = \int_{P_0}^{P} P'\left(\frac{1}{\rho^2}\,\frac{d\rho}{dP'}\right)(P')\,dP'" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.3, eq. (6.49), p. 115" />

[The notes' first expression has no minus sign; since $\delta V$ shrinks as $\rho$ grows, it is needed for the equality with the second, which is the one used.] Now every term is a time derivative (p. 115):

<Formula label="Energy conservation for an ideal fluid" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left[\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2 + \rho\Phi + P + \rho U\right)\delta V\right] = \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial t} + \rho\,\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t}\right)\delta V" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.3, eq. (6.50), p. 115" />

If $P$ and $\Phi$ do not depend explicitly on time at any fixed point, which is common, each fluid element keeps its total energy as it moves (p. 115). Dividing by the fixed mass $\delta m = \rho\,\delta V$ gives **Bernoulli's equation** (p. 115):

<Formula label="Bernoulli's equation" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{v^2}{2} + \Phi + \frac{P}{\rho} + U\right) = \frac{1}{\rho}\,\frac{\partial P}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.3, eq. (6.51), p. 115" />

[The notes print $\partial\rho/\partial t$ in the first term on the right; dividing (6.50) by $\rho\,\delta V$ gives $\partial P/\partial t$, as here.]

<WorkedExample title="The internal energy of an isothermal gas">
<Problem>
For a gas held at constant temperature, $P = \rho R'T$. Find $U(\rho)$ from (6.49), and evaluate it for air ($R' = 287$ J/(kg K)) at 288 K compressed to twice its starting density.
</Problem>
<Step title="The integral">
$U = \int_{\rho_0}^{\rho}R'T\,d\rho'/\rho' = R'T\ln(\rho/\rho_0)$. Compressing the gas ($\rho > \rho_0$) raises $U$: work was done on it.
</Step>
<Step title="Numbers">
$R'T = 287 \times 288 = 82{,}656$ J/kg, so for $\rho = 2\rho_0$, $U = 82{,}656 \times \ln 2 \approx 57{,}300$ J/kg, about 57 kJ per kilogram.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The Euler equation in astrophysics">
In 8.902 the stars of a galaxy are treated as a collisionless "fluid". Taking moments of their distribution gives the [Jeans equations](/courses/8-902/jeans-equations), which have the form of the Euler equation (6.34), with the stars' random motions in place of the pressure (a pressure that can differ from one direction to another, a tensor rather than one number) and the gravitational potential as $\Phi$. Perturbing the Euler and continuity equations of a uniform medium, together with Poisson's equation for gravity, then gives the [Jeans criterion](/courses/8-902/stability-jeans-toomre): small disturbances travel as sound waves, large ones collapse.
</Callout>

## Summary

- An **ideal fluid** has no viscosity and no heat conduction. Pressure pushes a small element with $\delta\vec F = -\nabla P\,\delta V$.
- Newton's law gives the **Euler equation** $\partial\vec v/\partial t + (\vec v\cdot\nabla)\vec v + \nabla P/\rho = \vec f/\rho$, non-linear through the advective term. With $\vec f = -\rho\nabla\Phi$ the force becomes $\nabla\Phi$; with the vorticity, $\vec v\times\vec\Omega$ replaces the advective term, and for constant $\rho$, $\partial\vec\Omega/\partial t = \nabla\times(\vec v\times\vec\Omega)$.
- Ideal fluids cannot cross walls, $\vec v\cdot\hat n = 0$ (or $v_{\text{surface}}$). Entropy conservation $dS/dt = 0$ is the fifth equation and fixes $P(\rho, S)$.
- **Momentum:** $\partial(\rho\vec v)/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\mathbb{T} = \vec f$ with the symmetric flux $T_{ij} = P\delta_{ij} + \rho v_iv_j$.
- **Energy:** $\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{v^2}{2} + \Phi + \frac{P}{\rho} + U\right) = \frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial P}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t}$ (Bernoulli), with $U = \int P\,d\rho/\rho^2$ the internal energy stored by compression.
