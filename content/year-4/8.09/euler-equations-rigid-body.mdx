With the inertia tensor in hand, Section 2.4 of the notes (pp. 44–45) writes down the equations of motion for a turning rigid body. The trick is to work in the body's own principal axes, where the moments of inertia never change, and to use the rule from [rotating coordinates](/courses/8-09/rotating-frames-coriolis-force) for the time derivative of the angular momentum. The result is Euler's three coupled equations for the components of $\vec\omega$. The section ends with their most famous consequence: rotation about two of the three principal axes is stable, about the third it is not.

## From torque to Euler's equations

Consider a rigid body turning about a fixed point, which may be the centre of mass or any other point held still. Choose the body axes at that point along the **principal axes**, labelled $\alpha = 1, 2, 3$. Torque equals the rate of change of angular momentum in the fixed (inertial) frame (see [torque and angular impulse](/courses/8-01/torque-and-angular-impulse)), and that rate can be expressed through the rate seen in the rotating body frame:

<Formula label="Torque in the body frame" tex="\vec\tau = \frac{d\vec L}{dt} = \frac{d_R\vec L}{dt} + \vec\omega \times \vec L" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.4, eq. (2.66), p. 44" />

where $\vec\omega = \vec\omega(t)$. The first component, for example, reads

<Formula label="First component" tex="\tau_1 = \frac{d_R L_1}{dt} + \omega_2 L_3 - \omega_3 L_2" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.4, eq. (2.67), p. 44" />

Three facts simplify this. In principal axes $L_\alpha = I_\alpha\omega_\alpha$ (no sum). The principal moments are fixed in the body, $d_R I_\alpha/dt = 0$. And the angular velocity changes at the same rate in both frames, since the extra term in its derivative is $\vec\omega \times \vec\omega = 0$; so $\dot\omega_\alpha$ is unambiguous. Hence $d_R L_\alpha/dt = I_\alpha\dot\omega_\alpha$, and substituting gives **Euler's equations**:

<Formula label="Euler's equations" tex="\begin{aligned} I_1\dot\omega_1 - (I_2 - I_3)\,\omega_2\omega_3 &= \tau_1 \\ I_2\dot\omega_2 - (I_3 - I_1)\,\omega_3\omega_1 &= \tau_2 \\ I_3\dot\omega_3 - (I_1 - I_2)\,\omega_1\omega_2 &= \tau_3 \end{aligned}" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.4, eq. (2.68), p. 44" />

Every quantity here, $\vec\omega$ and $\vec\tau$ alike, is a component along the body axes. The second and third equations follow from the first by the cyclic shift $1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 1$. All three fit in one line with the Levi-Civita symbol $\epsilon_{abc}$, which is $+1$ for $(a, b, c)$ a cyclic order of $(1, 2, 3)$, $-1$ for the other orders, and $0$ when two indices are equal (for fixed $\alpha$, with sums over $l$ and $k$):

<Formula label="Euler's equations with the Levi-Civita symbol" tex="\tau_\alpha = I_\alpha\,\dot\omega_\alpha + \epsilon_{\alpha lk}\,\omega_l\,\omega_k\,I_k" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.4, eq. (2.69), p. 44" />

For $\alpha = 1$ the only non-zero terms are $\epsilon_{123}\,\omega_2\omega_3 I_3 + \epsilon_{132}\,\omega_3\omega_2 I_2 = (I_3 - I_2)\,\omega_2\omega_3$, which is the first line of (2.68).

These are first-order equations for the three numbers $\omega_\alpha(t)$, but they are **non-linear**: products of the $\omega$'s appear. When two principal moments are equal, or when there is no torque, they can often be solved exactly; Section 2.5 does this for a symmetric top.

## Back to the fixed frame

Solving Euler's equations gives $\omega_\alpha(t)$ in the body frame. That says how fast the body turns about each of its own axes, but not directly how it moves as seen from the room. To connect the two, express the body components of $\vec\omega$ through the [Euler angles](/courses/8-09/rigid-body-coordinates-euler-angles) and their rates (notes p. 45, left as an exercise there):

<Formula label="Angular velocity from the Euler angles" tex="\begin{aligned} \omega_1 = \omega_{x'} &= \dot\phi\sin\theta\sin\psi + \dot\theta\cos\psi \\ \omega_2 = \omega_{y'} &= \dot\phi\sin\theta\cos\psi - \dot\theta\sin\psi \\ \omega_3 = \omega_{z'} &= \dot\phi\cos\theta + \dot\psi \end{aligned}" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.4, eq. (2.70), p. 45" />

Each Euler angle turns the body about its own axis, and the angular velocities simply add. The rate $\dot\phi$ is about the fixed $z$ axis, which in body components is $(\sin\theta\sin\psi, \sin\theta\cos\psi, \cos\theta)$; the rate $\dot\theta$ is about the line of nodes, $(\cos\psi, -\sin\psi, 0)$ in body components; the rate $\dot\psi$ is about $z'$ itself, $(0, 0, 1)$. (The first two follow by applying $U_\psi U_\theta$ to $(0, 0, 1)$ and to $(1, 0, 0)$, since $U_\phi$ leaves both the $z$ axis and, in the intermediate axes, the line of nodes in place.) Collecting terms gives (2.70). Given $\omega_\alpha(t)$, these are three differential equations for $\phi$, $\theta$ and $\psi$.

## Stability of free rotation

With no torque, $\vec\tau = 0$, can a body spin steadily about a principal axis, and if it is nudged, does it stay close? Take steady spin $\vec\omega = \omega_1\vec e_1$ about principal axis 1 and add small components along the other two (notes p. 45):

$\vec\omega = \omega_1\vec e_1 + \kappa_2\vec e_2 + \kappa_3\vec e_3$, with $\kappa_2$ and $\kappa_3$ small, kept to first order.

The first Euler equation says $\omega_1$ hardly changes:

<Formula label="The spin component stays constant" tex="\dot\omega_1 = \frac{I_2 - I_3}{I_1}\,\kappa_2\kappa_3 = O(\kappa^2) \approx 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.4, eq. (2.71), p. 45" />

The other two are linear in the small components:

<Formula label="The small components" tex="\dot\kappa_2 = \frac{I_3 - I_1}{I_2}\,\omega_1\,\kappa_3 \quad\text{and}\quad \dot\kappa_3 = \frac{I_1 - I_2}{I_3}\,\omega_1\,\kappa_2" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.4, eq. (2.72), p. 45" />

Differentiate the first and insert the second:

<Formula label="One equation for the wobble" tex="\ddot\kappa_2 = \left[\frac{(I_3 - I_1)(I_1 - I_2)\,\omega_1^2}{I_2 I_3}\right]\kappa_2" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.4, eq. (2.73), p. 45" />

The bracket is a constant, and its sign decides everything:

- If it is negative, call it $-\Omega^2$: then $\kappa_2 \propto \cos(\Omega t + \delta)$, a small wobble that stays small. This happens when $I_1$ is the **largest** or the **smallest** of the three, since then $(I_3 - I_1)$ and $(I_1 - I_2)$ have opposite signs.
- If it is positive, call it $\alpha^2$: then $\kappa_2 = a\,e^{\alpha t} + b\,e^{-\alpha t}$, and the growing part soon dominates. This happens when $I_1$ is the **intermediate** moment, $I_2 < I_1 < I_3$ or $I_3 < I_1 < I_2$.

(The notes write the oscillation frequency as w and the phase as $\phi$; this page uses $\Omega$ and $\delta$ to avoid a clash with $\omega$ and the Euler angle.) Rotation about the axes of largest and smallest moment is stable; rotation about the intermediate axis is **unstable**. Anyone can see this with an object whose three moments differ: a book held shut with a rubber band, a tennis racket, a phone. Tossed spinning about its longest or its shortest dimension, it turns cleanly; tossed about the middle axis, it flips over as it spins. The linear analysis says only that the wobble starts to grow; once it is no longer small, the full non-linear equations take over, and the body turns over and back again periodically (this last point goes beyond the notes).

<WorkedExample title="Flipping a phone">
<Problem>
Model a phone as a uniform box 15 cm long, 7 cm wide and 0.8 cm thick. It is tossed spinning at two turns per second, $\omega = 4\pi\ \text{s}^{-1} \approx 12.6\ \text{s}^{-1}$. How fast does a small wobble grow, or how fast does it oscillate, for spin about each of its three principal axes?
</Problem>
<Step title="Principal moments">
For a uniform box, the moment about an axis is $\tfrac{M}{12}$ times the sum of the squares of the two other side lengths. In units of $M\ \text{cm}^2$: about the long axis $I_1 = (7^2 + 0.8^2)/12 = 4.14$; about the width axis $I_2 = (15^2 + 0.8^2)/12 = 18.8$; about the thickness axis, perpendicular to the screen, $I_3 = (15^2 + 7^2)/12 = 22.8$. So $I_1 < I_2 < I_3$, and the width axis is the intermediate one.
</Step>
<Step title="Spin about the width axis (intermediate)">
Relabel (2.73) so that the spin axis comes first. With the moments $I_2$ for the spin axis and $I_1$, $I_3$ for the others, the constant is $\omega^2(I_3 - I_2)(I_2 - I_1)/(I_1I_3) = \omega^2 \times 4.03 \times 14.7/(4.14 \times 22.8)$, positive, so $\alpha = 0.791\,\omega \approx 9.94\ \text{s}^{-1}$. A wobble grows by a factor $e$ every $0.10$ s; a growing wobble of 1% reaches order one after about $\ln 100/\alpha \approx 0.46$ s (a little longer, 0.53 s, if it starts with no rate of change, as in the graph below), within one flight of a toss.
</Step>
<Step title="Spin about the long axis or the thickness axis (stable)">
About the long axis the constant is $-\omega^2(I_2 - I_1)(I_3 - I_1)/(I_2I_3)$, so the wobble oscillates at $\Omega = 0.799\,\omega \approx 10.0\ \text{s}^{-1}$, with period about $0.63$ s. About the thickness axis, $\Omega = \omega\sqrt{(I_3 - I_1)(I_3 - I_2)/(I_1I_2)} = 0.984\,\omega \approx 12.4\ \text{s}^{-1}$. Both stay small.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Working in principal body axes, where the $I_\alpha$ are constant, $\vec\tau = d_R\vec L/dt + \vec\omega \times \vec L$ with $L_\alpha = I_\alpha\omega_\alpha$ gives **Euler's equations**: $I_1\dot\omega_1 - (I_2 - I_3)\omega_2\omega_3 = \tau_1$ and cyclic, or $\tau_\alpha = I_\alpha\dot\omega_\alpha + \epsilon_{\alpha lk}\omega_l\omega_k I_k$. Both $\vec\omega$ and $\vec\tau$ are body components; $\dot\omega_\alpha$ is the same in either frame.
- The equations are non-linear. Their solution, in the body frame, is translated to the fixed frame with $\omega_1 = \dot\phi\sin\theta\sin\psi + \dot\theta\cos\psi$, $\omega_2 = \dot\phi\sin\theta\cos\psi - \dot\theta\sin\psi$, $\omega_3 = \dot\phi\cos\theta + \dot\psi$.
- **Free rotation** about a principal axis is stable if that axis has the largest or the smallest moment (small wobbles oscillate) and unstable about the intermediate axis (wobbles grow exponentially), as a tossed book, racket or phone shows.
