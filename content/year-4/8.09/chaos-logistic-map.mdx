[Section 7.4](/courses/8-09/limit-cycles-hopf-bifurcations) showed that two continuous variables cannot produce chaos, and that three can. Before tackling three-variable flows, Stewart looks for chaos in a simpler setting: **maps**, where time advances in discrete steps. (Citations give the printed page numbers 182 to 188 of the Chapter 7 notes. On this page $n$ counts iterations, $r$ is the parameter of the map, and $p$, $q$ are the two points of a 2-cycle.)

## From flows to maps

Flows with two variables are understood through their fixed points and limit cycles, and they include every one-dimensional particle with its two phase-space variables; none of them is chaotic (p. 182). Chaos was found in Section 7.1, however, in the Poincaré section of the driven pendulum. Sampling at $\varphi = 2\pi N$, the state at one drive period determines the next one, so the section defines a map of two discrete variables (p. 182):

<Formula label="The pendulum's Poincaré map" tex="\theta_{N+1} = f_1(\theta_N, \omega_N), \qquad \omega_{N+1} = f_2(\theta_N, \omega_N)" cite="reading1" at="p. 182, eq. (7.68)" />

The functions $f_1$ and $f_2$ exist because the three-variable flow has unique solutions. One can go further: a one-dimensional map $x_{n+1} = f(x_n)$ with a nonlinear $f$ is already enough for chaos (p. 182). The standard example is the **logistic map** (p. 182)

<Formula label="Logistic map" tex="x_{n+1} = f(x_n) = rx_n(1 - x_n)" cite="reading1" at="p. 182, eq. (7.69)" />

For $0 < r \le 4$, an $x_n$ in $[0, 1]$ stays in $[0, 1]$, because the largest value of $f$ is $f(\frac12) = r/4$ (p. 182). Its graph in the $x_n$–$x_{n+1}$ plane is a parabola whose height grows with $r$.

## Fixed points of maps and their stability

A fixed point of a map is a point the map leaves unchanged (p. 183),

<Formula label="Fixed point of a map" tex="x_* = f(x_*)" cite="reading1" at="p. 183, eq. (7.70)" />

which differs from the condition $f(x_*) = 0$ for flows, because a map is an iterated difference equation. For the logistic map (p. 183)

<Formula label="Fixed points of the logistic map" tex="x_* = rx_*(1 - x_*) \quad\Rightarrow\quad x_* = 0 \ \text{for all } r, \qquad x_* = 1 - \frac{1}{r} \ \text{for } r > 1" cite="reading1" at="p. 183, eq. (7.71)" />

**Stability.** Perturb the fixed point, $x_n = x_* + \eta_n$, and expand (p. 183):

<Formula label="Perturbing a fixed point of a map" tex="x_{n+1} = x_* + \eta_{n+1} = f(x_* + \eta_n) = f(x_*) + \left.\frac{df}{dx}\right|_{x_*}\eta_n + O(\eta_n^2)" cite="reading1" at="p. 183, eq. (7.72)" />

so, since $f(x_*) = x_*$ (p. 183),

<Formula label="Linearized map" tex="\eta_{n+1} = \left.\frac{df}{dx}\right|_{x_*}\eta_n" cite="reading1" at="p. 183, eq. (7.73)" />

Each step multiplies the deviation by the same factor. If $|f'(x_*)| < 1$ the deviation shrinks to zero and the fixed point is stable; if $|f'(x_*)| > 1$ it grows and the fixed point is unstable; if $|f'(x_*)| = 1$ the fixed point is marginal and the linear analysis does not decide (p. 183). (The notes write these steps with the index $N$.) Unlike in a one-dimensional flow, where a deviation keeps its sign, the multiplier of a one-dimensional map can be negative, and then the deviation changes sign at every step.

For the logistic map $f'(x) = r - 2rx$, so (p. 184)

<Formula label="Multipliers of the logistic map's fixed points" tex="\left.\frac{df}{dx}\right|_{x_* = 0} = r, \qquad \left.\frac{df}{dx}\right|_{x_* = 1 - 1/r} = 2 - r" cite="reading1" at="p. 184, eq. (7.74)" />

The origin is stable for $r < 1$; the other fixed point is stable for $1 < r < 3$ and unstable otherwise (p. 184). Hence, for almost every starting point in $(0, 1)$ (p. 184; the qualifier is ours),

<Formula label="Long-term behaviour for r < 3" tex="\lim_{n\to\infty} x_n = \begin{cases} 0, & r < 1 \\ 1 - \dfrac{1}{r}, & 1 < r < 3 \end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="p. 184, eq. (7.75)" />

<WorkedExample title="Converging to the fixed point at r = 2.5">
<Problem>
Iterate the logistic map at $r = 2.5$ from $x_0 = 0.3$ and compare with the linear prediction.
</Problem>
<Step title="The fixed point">
$x_* = 1 - 1/2.5 = 0.6$, with multiplier $f'(x_*) = 2 - 2.5 = -0.5$: stable.
</Step>
<Step title="The iterates">
$x_1 = 2.5 \times 0.3 \times 0.7 = 0.525$, then $x_2 = 0.6234$, $x_3 = 0.5869$, $x_4 = 0.6061$, $x_5 = 0.5968$. The deviations from 0.6 are $-0.075$, $+0.023$, $-0.013$, $+0.006$, $-0.003$: they alternate in sign and, once small, roughly halve each step, as the multiplier $-0.5$ predicts.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Period doubling

For $r > 3$ no fixed point is stable, so what happens? Look at two steps at once (p. 184):

<Formula label="The twice-iterated map" tex="x_{n+2} = f(f(x_n))" cite="reading1" at="p. 184, eq. (7.76)" />

a fourth-order polynomial in $x_n$. Besides the origin, for $r$ just above 3 its graph crosses the diagonal three times: at the old fixed point $1 - 1/r$, now unstable, and at two new stable points $p$ and $q$ (p. 184). Since $f(f(p)) = p$ but $f(p) \ne p$, the map sends $p$ to $q$ and $q$ back to $p$: the long-term state is a **2-cycle**, oscillating between them (pp. 184–185). The period has doubled, and the notes call this a pitchfork bifurcation of the map at $r = 3$ (p. 185): it is one for $f \circ f$, whose two new fixed points branch off the old one, while for $f$ itself it is usually called a flip or period-doubling bifurcation. Solving $f(f(x)) = x$ (our step) gives

<Formula label="The 2-cycle of the logistic map" tex="p,\ q = \frac{r + 1 \pm \sqrt{(r - 3)(r + 1)}}{2r}" cite="reading1" at="p. 185 (worked out here)" />

real only for $r > 3$.

  curves={[
    { expr: "r*(r*x*(1 - x))*(1 - r*x*(1 - x))", label: "f(f(x))" },
    { expr: "x", label: "diagonal", dash: true }

  params={{ r: { value: 3.2, min: 2.5, max: 4, step: 0.02, label: "r" } }}
  caption="The twice-iterated logistic map f(f(x)), computed by substituting f into itself, with the diagonal. For r < 3 it crosses the diagonal only at 0 and 1 − 1/r. Above r = 3 two more crossings appear, the 2-cycle: at r = 3.2 they are p = 0.7995 and q = 0.5130, on either side of the now unstable 1 − 1/r = 0.6875." />

**When the 2-cycle fails** (p. 185). By the chain rule, at $x_* = p$ or $q$,

<Formula label="Multiplier of the 2-cycle" tex="\left.\frac{d}{dx}f(f(x))\right|_{x = x_*} = \frac{df(p)}{dp}\,\frac{df(q)}{dq}" cite="reading1" at="p. 185, eq. (7.77)" />

the same for both points, so $p$ and $q$ lose stability together, when $|f'(p)f'(q)| > 1$. The 2-cycle then splits into a 4-cycle (p. 185). For the logistic map the product works out to $4 + 2r - r^2$ (our step), which falls from $+1$ at $r = 3$ to $-1$ at $r = 1 + \sqrt6 \approx 3.449$, the second bifurcation quoted in the notes (p. 186).

The doublings continue, $2 \to 4 \to 8 \to 16 \to 32 \to \ldots$, coming ever closer together, until $r = 3.5699456\ldots$; beyond that the map is chaotic (p. 185). In the bifurcation diagram (the long-term values of $x$ plotted against $r$, the notes' Fig. 7.64) the branches split again and again and then dissolve into a band of points, broken by narrow periodic windows (p. 186). (The caption of Fig. 7.64 puts one window "near $r = 0.384$", a slip for 3.84.) This is the **period-doubling route to chaos**, one of the common ways chaos sets in; the driven pendulum of Section 7.1 follows it too (p. 186).

## The Lyapunov exponent

How do we know the motion beyond 3.5699 is chaotic? Chaos means sensitive dependence on initial conditions. Follow two orbits that start $\delta_0$ apart, $x_0 \to x_1 \to x_2 \ldots$ and $x_0 + \delta_0 \to x_1 + \delta_1 \to x_2 + \delta_2 \ldots$; for chaos we expect $|\delta_n| \approx |\delta_0|e^{\lambda n}$ for large $n$ with $\lambda > 0$, the **Lyapunov exponent** (p. 186). For a map it can be computed (p. 186):

<Formula label="Lyapunov exponent from two orbits" tex="\lambda = \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\ln\left|\frac{\delta_n}{\delta_0}\right| = \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\ln\left|\frac{f^n(x_0 + \delta_0) - f^n(x_0)}{\delta_0}\right|" cite="reading1" at="p. 186, eq. (7.78)" />

where $f^n$ means $f$ applied $n$ times (not a power). For small $\delta_0$ the ratio is the derivative of $f^n$, and the chain rule turns it into a product along the orbit (p. 186):

<Formula label="Chain rule along the orbit" tex="\lambda \simeq \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\ln\left|\frac{d}{dx_0}f^n(x_0)\right| = \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\ln\left|\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{df(x_j)}{dx_j}\right|" cite="reading1" at="p. 186, eq. (7.79)" />

and so (p. 187)

<Formula label="Lyapunov exponent of a map" tex="\lambda = \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\ln\left|\frac{df(x_j)}{dx_j}\right|" cite="reading1" at="p. 187, eq. (7.80)" />

the average of the logarithmic stretching along the orbit, easy to accumulate on a computer. For the logistic map, $\lambda < 0$ in the period-doubling region and $\lambda > 0$ in the chaotic regions, which contain periodic windows with chaos on both sides; the largest is a 3-cycle near $r \approx 3.83$ (p. 187). On a periodic orbit the sum repeats, so $\lambda$ can be written down: $\ln|2 - r|$ on the fixed point and $\frac12\ln|4 + 2r - r^2|$ on the 2-cycle (our step). It touches zero at each bifurcation, where a multiplier reaches $\pm1$.

<WorkedExample title="Stability of the 2-cycle at r = 3.2">
<Problem>
At $r = 3.2$, find the 2-cycle, check its stability, and give its Lyapunov exponent.
</Problem>
<Step title="The cycle">
$\sqrt{(r - 3)(r + 1)} = \sqrt{0.84} = 0.9165$, so $p = (4.2 + 0.9165)/6.4 = 0.7995$ and $q = (4.2 - 0.9165)/6.4 = 0.5130$. Check: $3.2 \times 0.7995 \times 0.2005 = 0.5130$.
</Step>
<Step title="Stability and λ">
$f'(p) = 3.2(1 - 1.599) = -1.917$ and $f'(q) = 3.2(1 - 1.026) = -0.0835$; the product is $0.160 = 4 + 6.4 - 10.24$. Since $|0.160| < 1$ the cycle is stable, and $\lambda = \frac12\ln 0.160 = -0.916$: nearby orbits converge onto it, by a factor 0.4 per step on average.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Feigenbaum's number

The period doublings come at shorter and shorter intervals, and for a wide class of maps (and nonlinear differential equations) the rate at which they speed up is a **universal** number. If $r_\alpha$ is the parameter of the $\alpha$-th period doubling (p. 187),

<Formula label="Feigenbaum number" tex="\delta_F = \lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{r_\alpha - r_{\alpha-1}}{r_{\alpha+1} - r_\alpha} \approx 4.669201" cite="reading1" at="p. 187, eq. (7.81)" />

For the logistic map the ratio is close to $\delta_F$ already for small $\alpha$ (p. 187): with $r_1 = 3$, $r_2 = 1 + \sqrt6 = 3.4495$ and $r_3 = 3.5441$ (the standard numerically computed value, not in the notes), the first ratio is $0.4495/0.0946 = 4.75$. Assuming every interval is $1/\delta_F$ times the previous one (p. 187),

<Formula label="Shrinking intervals" tex="\delta_1 = r_2 - r_1 = \sqrt6 - 2, \qquad \delta_n = r_{n+1} - r_n = \frac{\delta_{n-1}}{\delta_F} = \ldots = \frac{\delta_1}{\delta_F^{\,n-1}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 187, eq. (7.82)" />

(here $\delta_n$ are intervals of $r$, not separations of orbits), the doublings accumulate at a geometric sum (p. 188):

<Formula label="Estimated onset of chaos" tex="r_\infty = \lim_{n\to\infty} r_n = 3 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\delta_n \simeq 3 + \frac{\delta_1}{1 - 1/\delta_F} = 3.572" cite="reading1" at="p. 188, eq. (7.83)" />

close to the true $3.5699456$ (p. 188).

## Stretching and folding

How can orbits separate exponentially and yet stay inside $[0, 1]$? By **stretching and folding**, as when a drop of food colouring is kneaded into dough (p. 188). Take $r \simeq 4$ (p. 188):

<Formula label="Stretching and folding at r = 4" tex="x_n \in \left(0, \tfrac12\right) \ \text{is sent to}\ x_{n+1} \in (0, 1), \qquad x_n \in \left(\tfrac12, 1\right) \ \text{is sent to}\ x_{n+1} \in (1, 0)" cite="reading1" at="p. 188, eqs. (7.84), (7.85)" />

Each half is stretched to the full interval, the second in reverse: the interval is stretched to twice its length and folded back onto itself. Repeating this makes the structure ever more intricate, while nearby points are pulled apart on average (p. 188).

Finally, the bifurcation diagram is **self-similar**: blow up a small enough range of $r$ and the period doublings, chaotic bands and periodic windows reappear in miniature. Self-similarity is a property of fractals, the subject of the next section (p. 188).

## Summary

- Two-variable flows are never chaotic, but maps can be: the driven pendulum's Poincaré section is a two-variable map, and the one-variable logistic map $x_{n+1} = rx_n(1 - x_n)$ already shows chaos.
- A fixed point of a map, $x_* = f(x_*)$, is stable if $|f'(x_*)| < 1$. The logistic map's fixed points are 0 (stable for $r < 1$) and $1 - 1/r$ (stable for $1 < r < 3$).
- At $r = 3$ a stable 2-cycle appears as fixed points of $f(f(x))$; it loses stability at $r = 1 + \sqrt6$ when $|f'(p)f'(q)| > 1$. Period doublings accumulate at $r = 3.5699\ldots$, after which the map is chaotic with periodic windows (the 3-cycle near 3.83).
- The Lyapunov exponent $\lambda = \lim \frac1n\sum\ln|f'(x_j)|$ is negative on periodic orbits and positive in chaos.
- Successive period-doubling intervals shrink by the universal Feigenbaum ratio $\delta_F \approx 4.669$, which predicts the onset of chaos at about 3.572.
- Bounded chaos comes from stretching and folding; the bifurcation diagram is self-similar.
