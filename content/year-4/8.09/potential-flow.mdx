The equations of the previous sections are non-linear and hard to solve in general. [Section 6.3](/courses/8-09/static-fluids-steady-flows) handled special cases by Bernoulli's equation along single stream lines; this section finds whole velocity fields for a large and important class of flows, then meets a famous failure of the ideal fluid (pp. 120–124). The notes' figures are not reproduced, and the worked example and the plot are our own.

## The velocity potential

A flow that is everywhere both **irrotational** and **incompressible** is a **potential flow**; it may be steady or not (p. 120). With $\vec\Omega = \nabla\times\vec v = 0$ the velocity field is conservative, like an electrostatic field, so the line integral from a fixed point $\vec r_0$,

<Formula label="The velocity potential" tex="\phi(\vec r) = \int_{\vec r_0}^{\vec r}\vec v(\vec r', t)\cdot d\vec r'" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.4, eq. (6.69), p. 120" />

does not depend on the path, only on its end points, and the velocity is its gradient (p. 121):

<Formula label="Velocity from the potential" tex="\vec v = \nabla\phi" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.4, eq. (6.70), p. 121" />

Incompressibility, $\nabla\cdot\vec v = 0$, then becomes **Laplace's equation** for the potential (p. 121):

<Formula label="Laplace's equation for the potential" tex="\nabla^2\phi = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.4, eq. (6.71), p. 121" />

So the hard non-linear problem for $\vec v$ turns into a linear problem for a single scalar, the same one as in electrostatics: solve for $\phi$ with the right boundary conditions and the velocity follows at once.

## The pressure

The Euler equation then gives the pressure directly (p. 121). With $\vec\Omega = 0$, its form (6.35) loses the $\vec v\times\vec\Omega$ term (p. 121):

<Formula label="Euler equation for irrotational flow" tex="\frac{\partial\vec v}{\partial t} + \nabla\left(\frac{v^2}{2} + \Phi\right) + \frac{\nabla P}{\rho} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.4, eq. (6.72), p. 121" />

For constant $\rho$, every term is a gradient, since $\partial\vec v/\partial t = \nabla(\partial\phi/\partial t)$ (p. 121):

<Formula label="Everything is a gradient" tex="\nabla\left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + \frac{v^2}{2} + \Phi + \frac{P}{\rho}\right) = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.4, eq. (6.73), p. 121" />

The bracket therefore cannot depend on position, only on time (p. 121):

<Formula label="Bernoulli for potential flow" tex="\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + \frac{v^2}{2} + \Phi + \frac{P}{\rho} = b(t)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.4, eq. (6.74), p. 121" />

The potential is only defined up to adding a function of time, which changes no velocity: replacing $\phi$ by $\phi - \int^t b(t')\,dt'$ removes the time dependence of $b$, so $b$ can be taken constant [the notes write the shift with a plus sign, which would double $b(t)$ instead of cancelling it], with its value set by a boundary condition on the pressure (p. 121). The pressure everywhere is then (p. 121)

<Formula label="The pressure in a potential flow" tex="\frac{P}{\rho} = -\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} - \frac{v^2}{2} - \Phi + b" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.4, eq. (6.75), p. 121" />

where the first three terms on the right can depend on both position and time. In a steady flow, $\partial\phi/\partial t = 0$ (p. 121).

## Simple potential flows

Because Laplace's equation is linear, simple solutions can be added. The notes give three (p. 121):

- **Uniform flow:** $\phi = v_0x$ gives $\vec v = v_0\hat x$.
- **Point source** in two dimensions: $\phi = A\ln r$, with (p. 121)

<Formula label="A point source in two dimensions" tex="\vec v = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r}\,\hat r + \frac{1}{r}\,\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta}\,\hat\theta = \frac{A}{r}\,\hat r" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.4, eq. (6.76), p. 121" />

for all $r > 0$: fluid streams straight out of the origin. In three dimensions the source is $\phi = -A/r$, with $\vec v = (A/r^2)\hat r$. At $r = 0$ itself there is a delta-function source, and Laplace's equation fails there (p. 121).

- **Potential vortex:** $\phi = \Gamma\theta/2\pi$ in the plane, so $v_r = \partial\phi/\partial r = 0$ and $v_\theta = (1/r)\,\partial\phi/\partial\theta = \Gamma/(2\pi r)$ for $r > 0$ (p. 121). The fluid circles the origin, faster closer in. (Our remarks:) although the fluid goes round, it has no vorticity anywhere off the axis, since a small element is carried round without turning about its own centre; and all the circulation, $\oint\vec v\cdot d\vec l = \Gamma$ round any loop enclosing the axis, sits on the axis.

## Flow past a sphere

A sphere of radius $R$ moves at constant velocity $\vec u$ through an incompressible ideal fluid at rest far away (p. 122). Equivalently, and more easily, hold the sphere still and let the fluid stream past it with velocity $-\vec u$ far away; the two velocity fields differ only by the constant $-\vec u$. Centre the coordinates on the sphere with $\vec u = u\hat x$.

Only $\vec u$ singles out a direction; everything else about the problem looks the same from every angle. Laplace's equation is linear and $\vec u$ enters only through the boundary condition, which is linear in it, so $\vec v$ and $\phi$ are proportional to $\vec u$; dimensional analysis, which the notes take up later, leads to the same conclusion (p. 122). Split off the uniform stream (p. 122):

<Formula label="Uniform stream plus a disturbance" tex="\phi(\vec x) = -ux + \phi'(\vec x)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.4, eq. (6.77), p. 122" />

The term $-ux$ already gives the right flow far away, so the disturbance must die off there (p. 122):

<Formula label="The disturbance vanishes far away" tex="\lim_{r\to\infty}\phi'(\vec x) = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.4, eq. (6.78), p. 122" />

Taking the gradient, $\vec v = -u\hat x + \vec v'$ (eq. 6.79), and $\vec v'$ is the velocity field of the moving sphere in still fluid, which should indeed vanish far from the sphere.

Which solution of $\nabla^2\phi' = 0$ to choose? The point source $1/r$ is ruled out: its flow points straight out of the sphere, so it cannot satisfy the condition on the surface, and it is not proportional to $\vec u$ (p. 122). The next possibility is a **dipole**, one derivative of $1/r$ along $\vec u$ (p. 122):

<Formula label="A dipole disturbance" tex="\phi' = A\,\vec u\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.4, eq. (6.80), p. 122" />

It is linear in $\vec u$, and it solves Laplace's equation for $r > 0$ because derivatives commute: $\nabla^2\phi' = A(\vec u\cdot\nabla)\nabla^2(1/r) = 0$ (p. 123). With $\nabla(1/r) = -\vec r/r^3$ (eq. 6.81) it is $\phi' = -A\,\vec u\cdot\vec r/r^3$ (eq. 6.82), and using $\nabla(\vec u\cdot\vec r) = \vec u$ and $\nabla r^{-n} = -nr^{-(n+2)}\vec r$ (eq. 6.83) its gradient is (p. 123)

<Formula label="Velocity of the dipole flow" tex="\vec v' = \nabla\phi' = \frac{A}{r^3}\left(-\vec u + \frac{3(\vec u\cdot\vec r)\,\vec r}{r^2}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.4, eq. (6.84), p. 123" />

On the surface of the fixed sphere the fluid may not flow in or out: $\vec v\cdot\hat r = 0$ at $r = R$ (p. 123). With $\vec v = -\vec u + \vec v'$,

<Formula label="Boundary condition on the sphere" tex="0 = -\vec u\cdot\hat r - \frac{A\,\vec u\cdot\hat r}{R^3} + \frac{3A\,\vec u\cdot\hat r}{R^3}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.4, eq. (6.85), p. 123" />

which holds in every direction if $A = R^3/2$. The full flow is (p. 123)

<Formula label="Potential flow past a sphere" tex="\vec v = -\vec u + \frac{R^3}{2r^3}\left(\frac{3(\vec u\cdot\vec r)\,\vec r}{r^2} - \vec u\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.4, eq. (6.86), p. 123" />

It has the right dimensions and meets both boundary conditions, on the sphere and at infinity.

**The pressure on the sphere.** The flow is steady, so along the stream lines from far upstream $P + \tfrac12\rho v^2$ takes its value at infinity (p. 123). On the surface, with $\vec u\cdot\vec r = ur\cos\theta$, (6.86) gives a velocity along the surface of size $\tfrac32u\sin\theta$; squaring and simplifying (p. 123):

<Formula label="Pressure on the sphere" tex="P = P_\infty + \frac{\rho u^2}{8}\left(9\cos^2\theta - 5\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.4, eq. (6.87), p. 123" />

The pressure is highest, $P_\infty + \tfrac12\rho u^2$, at the front and back stagnation points ($\theta = 0$ and $\pi$), and lowest at the equator, where the fluid moves fastest. And it is the same in front and behind: $\cos^2\theta$ does not change when $\theta \to \pi - \theta$. [The notes write $\theta \to \pi/2 - \theta$; the reflection that swaps front and back is $\theta \to \pi - \theta$.] So the pressure pushes equally on both halves, and the net force along the flow is **zero**, a result usually called d'Alembert's paradox (the name is ours, not the notes'). This is strange, since a sphere held in a stream clearly feels a push downstream (p. 123). The fault lies in treating the fluid as ideal: near surfaces, viscosity cannot be neglected.

<WorkedExample title="A sphere in a water current">
<Problem>
A sphere is held fixed in a stream of water ($\rho = 1000$ kg/m³) flowing at 2 m/s. In the potential-flow model, find the fastest water speed on its surface and the pressure (relative to the far-away pressure) at the front stagnation point and at the equator.
</Problem>
<Step title="Speeds">
On the surface the speed is $\tfrac32u\sin\theta$, largest at the equator: $\tfrac32 \times 2 = 3$ m/s, half again as fast as the stream.
</Step>
<Step title="Pressures">
$\tfrac18\rho u^2 = 1000 \times 4/8 = 500$ Pa. At the front, $\theta = 0$: $500 \times (9 - 5) = +2000$ Pa, which is $\tfrac12\rho u^2$, as for any stagnation point. At the equator: $500 \times (0 - 5) = -2500$ Pa, which agrees with Bernoulli, $\tfrac12\rho(u^2 - (3u/2)^2) = \tfrac12 \times 1000 \times (4 - 9) = -2500$ Pa.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Is the flow really irrotational?

Perhaps the fault is the assumption of potential flow rather than of an ideal fluid. How natural is a potential flow? (pp. 123–124) Take the curl of the Euler equation in the form (6.35). The gradient terms drop out, and what remains is the vorticity equation (6.36) (p. 124):

<Formula label="Curl of the Euler equation" tex="\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\nabla\times\vec v\right) + \nabla\times\left(\nabla\left(\frac{v^2}{2}\right) - \vec v\times\left(\nabla\times\vec v\right)\right) = \frac{\partial\vec\Omega}{\partial t} - \nabla\times\left(\vec v\times\vec\Omega\right) = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.4, eqs. (6.88)–(6.89), pp. 123–124" />

[The notes reach this by writing $d(\nabla\times\vec v)/dt = \nabla\times(d\vec v/dt)$, eq. (6.88); the total derivative and the curl do not commute in general, but the equation (6.89) itself is just the curl of the Euler equation (6.35) and is correct.] The notes conclude that vorticity cannot arise along the flow (p. 124). Said precisely (our wording): following a fluid element in an incompressible flow, (6.89) becomes $d\vec\Omega/dt = (\vec\Omega\cdot\nabla)\vec v$, so an element that has no vorticity never gains any. [The notes call $\nabla\times\vec v$ "conserved along flow lines"; only this weaker statement follows, and it is all that is needed.] A steady flow that is uniform far upstream has no vorticity there, so (p. 124)

<Formula label="No vorticity anywhere" tex="\nabla\times\vec v = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.4, eq. (6.90), p. 124" />

on every stream line, throughout the flow. The potential-flow solution is consistent: within the ideal-fluid equations, the flow past the sphere really is irrotational.

Keeping the fluid ideal, the escape route would be to allow flows that jump discontinuously (p. 124). Picture stream lines peeling off the sphere along its surface, which respects the boundary condition; because they never connect back to the uniform stream at infinity, nothing forbids them to carry vorticity, and the region behind the sphere could hold still fluid. The proper description of the thin **boundary layers** next to bodies and of the **wakes** behind them needs no such tricks: it needs viscosity, which the notes add in Section 6.6, after one more ideal-fluid example, sound (p. 124).

## Summary

- **Potential flow:** irrotational and incompressible. Then $\vec v = \nabla\phi$ and $\nabla^2\phi = 0$, a linear problem; the pressure follows from $P/\rho = -\partial\phi/\partial t - v^2/2 - \Phi + b$.
- Simple solutions: uniform flow $v_0x$; point source $A\ln r$ (2D) or $-A/r$ (3D); potential vortex $\Gamma\theta/2\pi$ with $v_\theta = \Gamma/2\pi r$.
- **Sphere in a stream:** uniform flow plus a dipole, $\phi' = A\,\vec u\cdot\nabla(1/r)$ with $A = R^3/2$. The surface pressure $P_\infty + (\rho u^2/8)(9\cos^2\theta - 5)$ is symmetric front to back, so the ideal fluid gives **no drag**.
- Vorticity that is zero upstream stays zero ($\partial\vec\Omega/\partial t = \nabla\times(\vec v\times\vec\Omega)$), so the potential flow is self-consistent; the missing drag, boundary layers and wakes need **viscosity**.
