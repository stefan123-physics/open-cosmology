Often the equations of motion cannot be solved in closed form, or the orbit equation is hopeless, and yet what we most want to know is simple: how fast does the system repeat? For periodic motion, Section 4.6 of the notes (pp. 83–91) answers this with **action-angle variables**, a canonical transformation built from Hamilton's characteristic function $W$ of [Section 4.4](/courses/8-09/hamilton-jacobi-equation). This page covers the one-dimensional theory with the pendulum and the harmonic oscillator (notes pp. 83–87); [the next page](/courses/8-09/action-angle-variables-kepler) treats several degrees of freedom and the Kepler problem (pp. 87–91).

## Two kinds of periodic motion

Take one degree of freedom, $(q, p)$, with a conserved Hamiltonian $H(q, p) = \alpha_1$. Solving for the momentum gives $p = p(q, \alpha_1)$: the orbit in phase space is fixed by the energy. Two kinds of periodic motion are possible (notes pp. 83–84):

1. **Libration** (oscillation): the phase-space orbit is a closed loop. Both $q$ and $p$ are periodic, with the same period $\tau$.
2. **Rotation**: $p$ is periodic but $q$ keeps growing, as an angle does for a wheel. The path in phase space is open, but each period it repeats, shifted along $q$.

The pendulum shows both. For a bob of mass $m$ on a rod of length $a$, with $\theta$ measured from the lowest point (notes p. 84),

<Formula label="Pendulum Hamiltonian" tex="E = H = \frac{p_\theta^2}{2ma^2} - mga\cos\theta" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.171), p. 84" />

so the momentum is

<Formula label="Pendulum momentum" tex="p_\theta = \pm\sqrt{2ma^2\left(E + mga\cos\theta\right)}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.172), p. 84" />

which must be real. If $E < mga$ the pendulum swings back and forth between turning points: a libration. If $E > mga$ it goes over the top and keeps turning: a rotation. The borderline $E = mga$, where the pendulum just reaches the top, is the **separatrix**, the curve dividing the two kinds of orbit. ## The action variable

For either kind of periodic motion, the notes replace the new momentum $P = \alpha_1$ of the Hamilton-Jacobi method by another constant, the **action variable** (notes p. 85):

<Formula label="Action variable" tex="J = \oint p\,dq" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.173), p. 85" />

where $\oint$ is a definite integral over one period of the motion in phase space: once round the loop for a libration, over one cycle of $q$ (such as $2\pi$ of an angle) for a rotation. For a libration it is the area enclosed by the orbit. Since $p = p(q, \alpha_1)$, the integral depends only on $\alpha_1$: $J = J(\alpha_1)$ is a constant. Inverting it, $\alpha_1 = H = H(J)$, and the characteristic function can be written in terms of $J$ instead, $W = W(q, \alpha_1) = W(q, H(J)) = W(q, J)$.

## The angle variable

The coordinate conjugate to $J$ follows from the type-2 rule, with $W(q, J)$ as the generating function:

<Formula label="Angle variable" tex="\omega = \frac{\partial W}{\partial J}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.174), p. 85" />

(Here $\omega$ is a coordinate, not an angular velocity; in this section the notes reserve the letter for the angle variable.) The new Hamiltonian depends only on $J$, so Hamilton's equation for $\omega$ gives a constant rate,

<Formula label="The angle variable advances steadily" tex="\dot\omega = \frac{\partial H(J)}{\partial J} = \nu(J) \text{ is a constant}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.175), p. 85" />

and the angle variable grows linearly in time, $\omega = \nu t + \beta$, with $\beta$ set by the initial conditions (eq. (4.176)). As for dimensions: $J$ is a momentum times a coordinate, the units of angular momentum (J s), and $\omega$ is a pure number, like an angle or a phase.

## Why ω is the right angle: the frequency

What makes the pair $(\omega, J)$ useful is how much $\omega$ changes during one full cycle of $q$, at fixed $J$ (notes p. 85):

<Formula label="Change of the angle variable over one cycle" tex="\Delta\omega = \oint\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial q}\,dq = \oint\frac{\partial^2 W}{\partial q\,\partial J}\,dq = \frac{\partial}{\partial J}\oint\frac{\partial W}{\partial q}\,dq = \frac{\partial}{\partial J}\oint p\,dq = 1" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.177), p. 85" />

The last step is the definition of $J$ itself. So $\omega$ advances by exactly 1 per period. Since it advances at the rate $\nu$, one period $\tau$ gives $\Delta\omega = \nu\tau = 1$, and

<Formula label="Frequency" tex="\nu = \frac{1}{\tau}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.178), p. 85" />

$\nu$ is the **frequency** of the motion, in cycles per unit time. The practical result: once $H$ is known as a function of $J$,

<Formula label="Frequency from the action" tex="\nu = \frac{\partial H(J)}{\partial J}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.179), p. 85" />

gives the frequency directly. In practice one computes $J(E)$ and differentiates: $\nu = dE/dJ = (dJ/dE)^{-1}$, so the period is simply $\tau = dJ/dE$. Nothing else about the motion needs to be found.

## The pendulum

Measure the energy in units of $mga$ (notes pp. 85–86),

<Formula label="Scaled energy" tex="\tilde E = \frac{E}{mga}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.180), p. 85" />

so that $\tilde E > 1$ is a rotation, $\tilde E < 1$ a libration, and $\tilde E = -1$ the pendulum at rest at the bottom. The momentum becomes

<Formula label="Pendulum momentum, scaled" tex="p_\theta = \pm\sqrt{2m^2ga^3}\,\sqrt{\tilde E + \cos\theta}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.181), p. 86" />

For a rotation, one period is one turn, $\theta$ from $-\pi$ to $\pi$, with $p_\theta$ of one sign:

<Formula label="Action of a rotating pendulum" tex="J = \sqrt{2m^2ga^3}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\theta\,\sqrt{\tilde E + \cos\theta}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.182), p. 86" />

an elliptic integral. For a libration between the turning points $\pm\theta_0$, where $\tilde E = -\cos\theta_0$, the loop has an upper half ($p_\theta > 0$, from $-\theta_0$ to $\theta_0$) and a lower half ($p_\theta < 0$, back again). The four quarter swings contribute equally:

<Formula label="Action of a librating pendulum" tex="J = 4\sqrt{2m^2ga^3}\int_{0}^{\theta_0}d\theta\,\sqrt{\tilde E + \cos\theta}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.183), p. 86" />

The frequency then follows from

<Formula label="Pendulum frequency" tex="\nu = \frac{\partial E}{\partial J} = \left(\frac{\partial J}{\partial E}\right)^{-1}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.184), p. 86" />

The notes evaluate this graphically, plotting $J$, then $dJ/dE$, then $\nu$ against $\tilde E$ (Figures 4.6–4.8, p. 86). What the plots show: $J$ rises with energy but drops to exactly half its value as $\tilde E$ crosses 1, because just below the separatrix one cycle covers the loop's upper and lower halves, while just above it one cycle covers only one of them. $dJ/dE$ has a spike at $\tilde E = 1$ that diverges only logarithmically, so it can still be integrated. And the frequency $\nu$ starts at the small-oscillation value $\sqrt{g/a}/2\pi$ as $\tilde E \to -1$, decreases as the swing widens, falls to zero at the separatrix, where the pendulum needs an ever longer time to creep up to the top, and then rises again for rotations.

</Problem>
<Step title="The scaled energy">
At release $p_\theta = 0$ and $\theta = 90°$, so $\tilde E = -\cos 90° = 0$: below the separatrix, a libration.
</Step>
<Step title="The period from the action">
Differentiating eq. (4.183) with respect to $E = mga\tilde E$ (the boundary term vanishes, because the integrand is zero at $\theta_0$): $\tau = \dfrac{dJ}{dE} = \dfrac{4\sqrt{2m^2ga^3}}{mga}\displaystyle\int_0^{\theta_0}\dfrac{d\theta}{2\sqrt{\tilde E + \cos\theta}} = 2\sqrt{\dfrac{2a}{g}}\int_0^{\pi/2}\dfrac{d\theta}{\sqrt{\cos\theta}}$. </Step>

For small swings, $\nu_0 = \sqrt{g/a}/2\pi = 0.498$ Hz, a period of $2.007$ s. Swinging out to the horizontal makes the period about 18% longer (the frequency about 15% lower).
</Step>

## Small swings: the harmonic oscillator

For $|\theta| \ll 1$, $\cos\theta \approx 1 - \theta^2/2$, and the pendulum Hamiltonian becomes (notes pp. 86–87)

<Formula label="Pendulum for small angles" tex="H = \frac{p_\theta^2}{2ma^2} + \frac{mga}{2}\theta^2 - mga" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.185), p. 86" />

which, apart from the constant $-mga$, is a general harmonic oscillator

<Formula label="Harmonic oscillator" tex="H = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{m\Omega^2}{2}x^2" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.186), p. 87" />

with $\Omega = \sqrt{g/a}$, $x = a\theta$ and $p = p_\theta/a$ (eq. (4.187)). The notes write $\Omega$ for the oscillator's angular frequency to keep it apart from the angle variable $\omega$. The coordinate need not be an angle for the method to work. The action is

<Formula label="Oscillator action as an integral" tex="J = \oint p\,dq = \oint\pm\sqrt{2mE - m^2\Omega^2x^2}\,dx = 4\sqrt{2mE}\int_0^{x_0}\sqrt{1 - \frac{x^2}{x_0^2}}\,dx, \qquad x_0 \equiv \frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{m\Omega}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eqs. (4.188)–(4.189), p. 87" />

The last integral is a quarter of the area of an ellipse, $\pi x_0/4$, so

<Formula label="Oscillator action" tex="J = \pi\sqrt{2mE}\,x_0 = \frac{2\pi mE}{m\Omega} = \frac{2\pi E}{\Omega}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.190), p. 87" />

and

<Formula label="Oscillator frequency" tex="\frac{\partial E}{\partial J} = \frac{\Omega}{2\pi}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.191), p. 87" />

the familiar cyclic frequency, $\nu = \Omega/2\pi$, independent of the amplitude. Comparing with Section 4.1, where the oscillator's new momentum was $P = E/\Omega$ (written $E/\omega$ there): the action is $J = 2\pi P$, and the angle variable is the phase $Q$ divided by $2\pi$. The 8.06 page on [adiabatic invariants](/courses/8-06/adiabatic-invariants-classical-quantum) meets the same integral $\oint p\,dx = 2\pi E/\Omega$ as the quantity that stays fixed when a parameter changes slowly, and in the old quantum theory the action was set equal to a whole number of Planck constants.

## Summary

- Periodic motion of one degree of freedom is a **libration** (closed orbit) or a **rotation** ($q$ unbounded, $p$ periodic); the pendulum does both, divided by the separatrix $E = mga$.
- The **action** $J = \oint p\,dq$ over one period depends only on the energy, $J = J(E)$; it serves as the new constant momentum.
- The **angle** $\omega = \partial W(q, J)/\partial J$ grows as $\omega = \nu t + \beta$ and advances by exactly 1 per period, so $\nu = \partial H/\partial J = (dJ/dE)^{-1}$ is the frequency and $\tau = dJ/dE$ the period.
- For the pendulum, $\nu$ falls from $\sqrt{g/a}/2\pi$ at small amplitude to zero at the separatrix; for the harmonic oscillator, $J = 2\pi E/\Omega$ and $\nu = \Omega/2\pi$.
