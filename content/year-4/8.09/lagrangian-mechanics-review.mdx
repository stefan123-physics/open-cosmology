The prerequisite course (8.223) teaches Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics, including bound Kepler orbits, scattering by a central force and the first ideas about canonical transformations, so Chapter 1 of the notes is a brisk review that assumes some familiarity. The notes name the books used: Goldstein, Poole and Safko's *Classical Mechanics* (3rd edition), Landau and Lifshitz volume 6 on fluid mechanics and Symon's *Mechanics* for ideal fluids, Strogatz's *Nonlinear Dynamics and Chaos*, and Landau and Lifshitz volume 1 for the review (notes Section 1.1, p. 1). (MIT's material for 8.09 is these typed notes; there are no lecture videos. ## Newton's law in two forms

A system of $N$ particles is described by their trajectories: position vectors $\vec r_i(t)$ with $i = 1, \dots, N$, or, more generally, $3N$ **generalized coordinates** $q_i(t)$, which may be angles or any other numbers that fix the configuration (notes p. 1). Velocities are $\vec v_i = \dot{\vec r}_i$ or $\dot q_i$, and throughout the course a dot means the total time derivative $d/dt$. Momenta are $\vec p_i = m_i \vec v_i$ for particles, or generalized momenta $p_i$, defined on the [next page](/courses/8-09/hamiltonian-mechanics-review).

For fixed masses, Newton's second law can be set up in two equivalent ways (notes p. 2):

1. As $N$ **second-order** vector equations, $\vec F_i = \frac{d}{dt}(m_i \dot{\vec r}_i)$, with the starting positions $\vec r_i(0)$ and velocities $\dot{\vec r}_i(0)$ given. The unknowns are the $N$ vectors $\vec r_i(t)$.
2. As $2N$ **first-order** vector equations, $\vec F_i = \dot{\vec p}_i$ together with $\vec p_i / m_i = \dot{\vec r}_i$, with $\vec r_i(0)$ and $\vec p_i(0)$ given. Now positions and momenta are both unknowns.

The first form leads to Lagrangian mechanics, the second to Hamiltonian mechanics.

## Inertial frames

$\vec F = m\vec a$ holds only in **inertial frames**: frames in which a particle with no force on it moves in a straight line at constant velocity (notes p. 2). (The notes add that the converse does not hold.) In an inertial frame space is the same everywhere and in every direction, and time runs uniformly. A frame that is not inertial shows **fictitious forces**, such as the centrifugal and Coriolis effects. Special relativity also rests on inertial frames; general relativity replaces them by free motion along geodesics.

Real inertial frames are approximations: the ground of a fixed Earth, or better the frame of the distant stars. Small departures can be added as corrections. Stewart gives two signs that a frame is not inertial: a force that pulls towards some origin where nothing is there to cause it, or a force that changes in an unusual way when we pass from one frame to another.

Primes mark a change of frame. If $\vec r$ is measured in an inertial frame $S$ and $\vec r' = \vec f(\vec r, t)$ in a frame $S'$, then $S'$ is inertial exactly when $\ddot{\vec r} = 0$ and $\ddot{\vec r}' = 0$ always go together. The transformations that do this are the **Galilean transformations**:

<Formula label="Galilean transformation" tex="\vec r' = \vec r + \vec v_0\,t, \qquad t' = t" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, p. 2" />

Under them $\vec F = m\ddot{\vec r}$ and $\vec F' = m\ddot{\vec r}'$ say the same thing. They are the slow-speed limit, $v \ll c$, of the Lorentz transformations of special relativity.

A frame turned by an angle $\theta$ about the $z$ axis has

<Formula label="Rotated frame" tex="\begin{pmatrix} x' \\ y' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, p. 2" />

For a fixed angle this is harmless. But if the frame turns, $\theta = \omega t$, then a particle with $\ddot{\vec r} = 0$ has $\ddot{\vec r}' \neq 0$: a straight line at constant speed looks curved and accelerated from the rotating frame, which is therefore not inertial.

## Non-inertial coordinates

A different issue arises when we keep an inertial frame but describe it with curved coordinates. In polar coordinates, $\vec r = r\hat r$, and the unit vectors turn as the particle moves (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="How the polar unit vectors change" tex="\frac{d\hat r}{dt} = \dot\theta\,\hat\theta, \qquad \frac{d\hat\theta}{dt} = -\dot\theta\,\hat r" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.1), p. 3" />

Differentiating $\vec r = r\hat r$ twice then gives

<Formula label="Acceleration in polar coordinates" tex="\ddot{\vec r} = \ddot r\,\hat r + 2\dot r\dot\theta\,\hat\theta + r\left(\ddot\theta\,\hat\theta - \dot\theta^2\,\hat r\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.2), p. 3" />

A free particle has $\ddot{\vec r} = 0$, yet $\ddot r$ and $\ddot\theta$ need not vanish: a straight line passing the origin has changing $r$ and $\theta$. So "$m\ddot q = F_q$" cannot be written coordinate by coordinate. Nothing is wrong with the frame here; it is the coordinates. The notes call such coordinates **non-inertial coordinates**; curvilinear coordinates generally are.

## The Lagrangian and the Euler–Lagrange equations

Lagrangian mechanics rests on one function (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="The Lagrangian" tex="L = L(q, \dot q, t)" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.3), p. 3" />

where $q$ stands for all $N$ coordinates $(q_1, \dots, q_N)$ and $\dot q$ for all their velocities; from here on $N$ counts scalar coordinates, not vectors. Usually $L = T - V$, kinetic minus potential energy. In the simplest problems $T$ depends only on the velocities and $V$ only on the coordinates, but both may depend on $q$, $\dot q$ and $t$. (The notes warn that even this does not cover every problem; friction, in Section 1.4, needs more.) The motion obeys the $N$ second-order **Euler–Lagrange equations**:

<Formula label="Euler–Lagrange equations" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_i}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.4), p. 3" />

They contain the accelerations $\ddot q_i$ and reproduce $\vec F = m\vec a$. They come from **Hamilton's principle**, or the principle of stationary action: among all paths between fixed end points at times $t_1$ and $t_2$, the real one makes the action stationary,

<Formula label="Hamilton's principle" tex="\delta S = \delta \int_{t_1}^{t_2} L(q, \dot q, t)\,dt = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.5), p. 4" />

The calculus of variations that turns this into the Euler–Lagrange equations is reviewed in Section 1.5 of the notes.

## Why use the Lagrangian?

The notes list three advantages (notes pp. 4–5).

**Scalars instead of vectors.** $T$ and $V$ are numbers, easier to handle than force vectors.

**Any coordinates will do.** Take new coordinates that are functions of the old ones and possibly of time, a **point transformation**:

<Formula label="Point transformation" tex="Q_i = Q_i(q_1, \dots, q_N, t)" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.6), p. 4" />

Define the new Lagrangian as the old one written in the new variables, $L'(Q, \dot Q, t) = L(q, \dot q, t)$. The claim is that

<Formula label="Euler–Lagrange in the new coordinates" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L'}{\partial \dot Q_i}\right) - \frac{\partial L'}{\partial Q_i} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.8), p. 4" />

The notes prove it for one coordinate. By the chain rule $\dot Q = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial q}\dot q + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial t}$, and since this is linear in $\dot q$,

<Formula label="Cancellation of dots" tex="\frac{\partial \dot Q}{\partial \dot q} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial q}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.10), p. 4" />

an identity used again later in the course. Then $\partial L/\partial \dot q = (\partial L'/\partial \dot Q)(\partial Q/\partial q)$, because $Q$ itself does not depend on $\dot q$, while $\partial L/\partial q$ picks up two terms, one through $Q$ and one through $\dot Q$. Putting both into the old Euler–Lagrange equation, the terms containing $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial Q}{\partial q} = \frac{\partial \dot Q}{\partial q}$ cancel, and what is left is

<Formula label="What the old equation becomes" tex="0 = \left[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L'}{\partial \dot Q}\right) - \frac{\partial L'}{\partial Q}\right]\frac{\partial Q}{\partial q}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.12), p. 5" />

For a genuine change of coordinates $\partial Q/\partial q \neq 0$, so the bracket vanishes. Two consequences: the Euler–Lagrange equations may be used freely in non-inertial coordinates, and we may write $L$ in whatever coordinates are easiest and then change to ones that suit the symmetry, say from Cartesian to spherical.

**Constraints are easy.** A mass held on a surface, or a disk rolling without slipping, is constrained. With $L$ we can often avoid the forces of constraint (such as the normal force of the surface) altogether, by choosing $N - k$ independent coordinates for a system with $k$ constraints. A bead threaded on a helix needs one coordinate, its height $z$. A pendulum bob $m_2$ hanging from a block $m_1$ that slides along a horizontal rail needs two: the block's position $x$ and the pendulum angle $\theta$.

## Polar coordinates at work

Take a mass $m$ moving in a plane under a potential $V(r, \theta)$ (notes p. 5). Since $\dot{\vec r} = \dot r\hat r + r\dot\theta\hat\theta$, the kinetic energy is $\frac m2(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2)$ and

<Formula label="Lagrangian in polar coordinates" tex="L = \frac{m}{2}\left(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2\right) - V(r, \theta)" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.14), p. 5" />

The radial equation, $\frac{d}{dt}(m\dot r) - mr\dot\theta^2 + \partial V/\partial r = 0$, rearranges to

<Formula label="Radial equation" tex="m\ddot r - mr\dot\theta^2 = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial r} = F_r" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.16), p. 6" />

so the radial force is not $m\ddot r$: the centripetal term $mr\dot\theta^2$ appears automatically. The angular equation gives

<Formula label="Angular equation" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(mr^2\dot\theta\right) = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial \theta} = F_\theta" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.18), p. 6" />

which is the rate of change of the angular momentum about the axis perpendicular to the plane, equal to the torque about it, $\dot L_z = \tau_z$. ($F_\theta$ here is the generalized force for the angle, which is a torque.)

## A particle inside a cone

The notes then solve a model problem in four steps that work generally (notes p. 7). A particle slides without friction under gravity on the inside of a cone with its tip at the origin, axis vertical and half-opening angle $\alpha$, so that $\tan\alpha = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}/z$. (The notes call $\alpha$ the opening angle; it is the angle between the axis and the side, half the full opening. They also say "rolling", but a point particle on a frictionless surface slides.)

1. **Write $T$ and $V$ in $N = 3$ coordinates.** Cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$ suit the shape: $T = \frac m2(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2 + \dot z^2)$ and $V = mgz$.
2. **Impose the constraint, leaving $N - k = 2$ coordinates.** On the cone $z = r\cot\alpha$ and $\dot z = \dot r\cot\alpha$, so

<Formula label="Lagrangian for the cone" tex="L = \frac{m}{2}\left[\left(1 + \cot^2\alpha\right)\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2\right] - mgr\cot\alpha" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.19), p. 7" />

3. **Find the Euler–Lagrange equations.** For $r$ and for $\theta$:

<Formula label="Radial equation in the cone" tex="\left(1 + \cot^2\alpha\right)\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2 + g\cot\alpha = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.21), p. 7" />

<Formula label="Angular equation in the cone" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(mr^2\dot\theta\right) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left(2\dot r\dot\theta + r\ddot\theta\right)r = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eqs. (1.22)–(1.23), p. 7" />

4. **Solve,** by hand or numerically, or extract just the features wanted without a full solution.

For instance, the angular equation says $mr^2\dot\theta$ is constant, and the radial one then allows a circular orbit at any height, with $\dot\theta^2 = g\cot\alpha / r$. (This last remark is ours, not the notes'.)

<WorkedExample title="A bead on a helix">
<Problem>
A bead slides without friction on a wire bent into a helix of radius $R = 10$ cm with its axis vertical. The helix rises $5.0$ cm per turn. Find the bead's vertical acceleration.
</Problem>
<Step title="One coordinate">
The wire imposes two constraints, so one coordinate is enough. Use the azimuthal angle $\phi$ and write the helix as $x = R\cos\phi$, $y = R\sin\phi$, $z = b\phi$, where $2\pi b$ is the rise per turn: $b = 5.0/(2\pi) = 0.796$ cm. Then $\phi = z/b$, and $z$ itself can be the coordinate.
</Step>
<Step title="The Lagrangian">
$\dot x^2 + \dot y^2 = R^2\dot\phi^2 = (R^2/b^2)\dot z^2$, so $L = \frac m2 (1 + R^2/b^2)\,\dot z^2 - mgz$.
</Step>
<Step title="The equation of motion">
The Euler–Lagrange equation for $z$ gives $m(1 + R^2/b^2)\ddot z = -mg$, so $\ddot z = -g/(1 + R^2/b^2)$. Here $R/b = 12.6$ and $R^2/b^2 = 158$, so $\ddot z = -9.81/159 = -0.0617\ \text{m/s}^2$, about $g/159$. The wire's force never had to be found.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Writing $b/R = \tan\beta$, where $\beta$ is the angle of the wire above the horizontal, the result is $\ddot z = -g\sin^2\beta$: the same as for a straight frictionless ramp at angle $\beta$, which is what the helix is, wrapped around a cylinder.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The same equations for fields">
The Lagrangian method does not care what the coordinates are. In 8.902 Lecture 12 the inflaton field is defined by a Lagrangian density; the notes there get its equation of motion from energy conservation, but in an expanding universe the same equation is the Euler–Lagrange equation of that Lagrangian, with the field value playing the part of the coordinate. And where these notes say that general relativity replaces inertial frames by geodesic motion, 8.286 Lecture 13 finds the geodesics as the paths that make a length stationary.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Newton's second law is $N$ second-order vector equations, or $2N$ first-order ones for positions and momenta; it holds in inertial frames, which Galilean transformations $\vec r' = \vec r + \vec v_0 t$ connect.
- A rotating frame is not inertial; curvilinear coordinates such as polar ones are "non-inertial coordinates", in which $m\ddot q = F_q$ fails.
- The Lagrangian $L(q, \dot q, t)$, usually $T - V$, gives the Euler–Lagrange equations $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_i} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}$, which follow from Hamilton's principle $\delta S = 0$.
- They keep their form under any point transformation $Q = Q(q, t)$, thanks to $\partial \dot Q/\partial \dot q = \partial Q/\partial q$.
- Simple constraints are handled by choosing $N - k$ independent coordinates, so constraint forces never appear: the particle in a cone conserves $mr^2\dot\theta$, and a bead on a helix falls with $g\sin^2\beta$.
