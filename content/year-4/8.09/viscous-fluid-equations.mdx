The [ideal fluid](/courses/8-09/potential-flow) feels no drag from a sphere in its path and cannot form wakes. The missing ingredient is internal friction, **viscosity**, which this section adds to the equations of motion (pp. 127–130). The worked examples, the plot and their numbers are our own.

## The viscous stress tensor

Friction acts inside a fluid when neighbouring elements move at different velocities, that is when the velocity gradients $\partial v_i/\partial x_j$ are not zero; viscous stresses resist this relative motion (p. 127). The continuity equation is unchanged, but the Euler equation and the conservation laws of momentum, energy and entropy all change (p. 128).

The notes work to first order in the gradients, the most important terms when they are small. Arranged into a tensor, they define the **viscous stress tensor** (p. 128):

<Formula label="Viscous stress tensor" tex="\sigma_{ij} = \eta\left(\frac{\partial v_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial v_j}{\partial x_i} - \frac{2}{3}\,\delta_{ij}\,\nabla\cdot\vec v\right) + \zeta\,\delta_{ij}\,\nabla\cdot\vec v" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.6, eq. (6.107), p. 128" />

with two constants: the **shear viscosity** $\eta > 0$ and the **bulk viscosity** $\zeta > 0$. Why this form? There must be no friction when the fluid moves as a whole, with constant $\vec v$ (no gradients at all), or when it turns rigidly, $\vec v = \vec\omega\times\vec r$ (p. 128). Rigid rotation about $x$, for instance, is $\vec v = \omega_x(y\hat z - z\hat y)$. It has $\nabla\cdot\vec v = 0$ and $\partial v_z/\partial y + \partial v_y/\partial z = \omega_x - \omega_x = 0$, and similarly for the other pairs: the *symmetric* combinations of gradients vanish, while the antisymmetric ones (the vorticity) do not. So $\sigma_{ij}$ must be built from the symmetric combination and from $\nabla\cdot\vec v$. The last choice is bookkeeping: $\eta$ multiplies a traceless tensor (its diagonal adds to zero) and $\zeta$ the trace (p. 128).

Momentum conservation keeps its form (p. 128),

<Formula label="Momentum conservation" tex="\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\rho\vec v\right) + \nabla\cdot\mathbb{T} = \vec f" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.6, eq. (6.108), p. 128" />

but the momentum flux now includes the momentum carried by friction (p. 128):

<Formula label="Total stress tensor" tex="T_{ij} = P\delta_{ij} + \rho v_iv_j - \sigma_{ij}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.6, eq. (6.109), p. 128" />

## The Navier-Stokes equation

In practice friction enters by the replacement $P\delta_{ij} \to P\delta_{ij} - \sigma_{ij}$ (p. 128). In the Euler equation the pressure gradient becomes (p. 128)

<Formula label="Adding friction to the pressure force" tex="\left(\nabla P\right)_i = \frac{\partial}{\partial x_k}\,\delta_{ki}P \;\to\; \frac{\partial}{\partial x_k}\left(\delta_{ki}P - \sigma_{ki}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.6, eq. (6.110), p. 128" />

and with constant $\eta$ and $\zeta$ the divergence of the stress tensor is (p. 128)

<Formula label="Divergence of the viscous stress" tex="\frac{\partial}{\partial x_k}\,\sigma_{ki} = \eta\left(\frac{\partial^2v_i}{\partial x_k\,\partial x_k} + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\nabla\cdot\vec v - \frac{2}{3}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\nabla\cdot\vec v\right) + \zeta\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\nabla\cdot\vec v = \eta\nabla^2v_i + \left(\zeta + \frac{\eta}{3}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\nabla\cdot\vec v" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.6, eq. (6.111), p. 128" />

Putting it into the Euler equation gives the **Navier-Stokes equation** (p. 128):

<Formula label="The Navier-Stokes equation" tex="\frac{\partial\vec v}{\partial t} + (\vec v\cdot\nabla)\vec v + \frac{\nabla P}{\rho} - \frac{\eta}{\rho}\,\nabla^2\vec v - \frac{1}{\rho}\left(\zeta + \frac{\eta}{3}\right)\nabla\left(\nabla\cdot\vec v\right) = \frac{\vec f}{\rho}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.6, eq. (6.112), p. 128" />

The common case in what follows has no external force and incompressible flow, $\vec f = 0$ and $\nabla\cdot\vec v = 0$ (p. 129). The bulk viscosity then drops out:

<Formula label="Incompressible Navier-Stokes equation" tex="\frac{\partial\vec v}{\partial t} + \vec v\cdot\nabla\vec v = -\frac{\nabla P}{\rho} + \nu\nabla^2\vec v" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.6, eq. (6.113), p. 129" />

with the **kinematic viscosity** (p. 129)

<Formula label="Kinematic viscosity" tex="\nu \equiv \frac{\eta}{\rho}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.6, eq. (6.114), p. 129" />

whose unit, m²/s, is simpler than that of $\eta$, kg/(m s) (p. 129). The new term $\nu\nabla^2\vec v$ is a diffusion term (our remark): on its own it would make velocity differences spread out and even out, like heat.

## The pressure and the vorticity

Two consequences hold when $\nabla\cdot\vec v = 0$ and $\rho$ is constant (p. 129). Taking the **divergence** of (6.113) removes both $\partial\vec v/\partial t$ and $\nu\nabla^2\vec v$ (each is a divergence of $\vec v$ in disguise), leaving $\rho\nabla\cdot[(\vec v\cdot\nabla)\vec v] = -\nabla^2P$. In components (p. 129):

<Formula label="Pressure from the velocity" tex="\nabla^2P = -\rho\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\left(v_i\,\frac{\partial v_j}{\partial x_i}\right) = -\rho\,\frac{\partial v_i}{\partial x_j}\,\frac{\partial v_j}{\partial x_i}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.6, eq. (6.115), p. 129" />

using $\partial v_j/\partial x_j = 0$. Once the velocity is known, the right side is a source, and this Poisson equation gives the pressure (p. 129).

Taking the **curl** of (6.113), the left side gives the same terms as for the Euler equation in (6.36), and the friction adds a new one (p. 129):

<Formula label="Vorticity in a viscous fluid" tex="\frac{\partial\vec\Omega}{\partial t} - \nabla\times\left(\vec v\times\vec\Omega\right) = \nu\nabla^2\vec\Omega" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.6, eq. (6.116), p. 129" />

Vorticity is now not only carried along but also **diffuses**. (Our remark:) that undoes the ideal-fluid argument of Section 6.4, since vorticity can be created at a surface and spread into the flow.

## Boundary conditions and the force on a wall

Viscosity also changes the boundary conditions (p. 129). Molecular forces between a viscous fluid and a surface make the fluid stick to it, so both the normal and the tangential components of the velocity must match those of the surface. At a moving wall

<Formula label="No-slip condition" tex="\vec v = \vec v_{\text{wall}}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.6, p. 129" />

which includes $\vec v = 0$ at a wall at rest. (An ideal fluid had to match only the normal component.)

The force the fluid exerts on a surface, per unit area, has a pressure part and a friction part (p. 129):

<Formula label="Force per unit area on a surface" tex="F_i = -n_j\left(P\delta_{ji} - \sigma_{ji}\right) = -Pn_i + \sigma_{ij}n_j" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.6, eq. (6.117), p. 129" />

where $\hat n$ is the normal pointing out of the surface (into the fluid). The first term is the pressure along the normal; the second is the friction, which has tangential components.

<WorkedExample title="Kinematic viscosities, and a sheared layer of water">
<Problem>
(a) Water has $\eta \approx 1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ Pa s and $\rho = 1000$ kg/m³; air has $\eta \approx 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ Pa s and $\rho \approx 1.2$ kg/m³. Compare their kinematic viscosities. (b) A layer of water between a fixed floor and a plate above it moves as $v_x = sy$, with shear rate $s = 100$ s⁻¹. Find the friction force per unit area on the floor and the heat produced per unit volume per second.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Kinematic viscosity">
Water: $\nu = 10^{-3}/1000 = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}$ m²/s. Air: $\nu = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}/1.2 = 1.5 \times 10^{-5}$ m²/s, fifteen times more. Air is much less viscous in $\eta$, but much lighter, so viscosity damps its motion more.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Force on the floor">
The only non-zero gradient is $\partial v_x/\partial y = s$, so $\sigma_{xy} = \sigma_{yx} = \eta s = 10^{-3} \times 100 = 0.1$ Pa. The floor's normal is $\hat n = \hat y$, so by (6.117) the tangential force per area is $F_x = \sigma_{xy} = 0.1$ N/m², in the direction of the flow: the water drags the floor along.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Heating">
The dissipation rate per unit volume is $\sigma_{ij}\,\partial v_i/\partial x_j = \sigma_{xy}\,s = \eta s^2 = 10^{-3} \times 10^4 = 10$ W/m³, which becomes heat.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Energy dissipation and entropy

The Navier-Stokes equation also gives a modified energy balance. The notes quote the result in integral form, without the derivation (p. 129):

<Formula label="Kinetic energy balance with viscosity" tex="\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_V\frac{1}{2}\rho v^2\,dV = -\oint_{\partial V}\left(\rho\left(\frac{v^2}{2} + \frac{P}{\rho}\right)v_i - v_j\sigma_{ji}\right)dS_i - \int_V\sigma_{ij}\,\frac{\partial v_i}{\partial x_j}\,dV" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.6, eq. (6.118), p. 129" />

The left side is the rate of change of the kinetic energy in $V$. The surface integral is the energy flowing out through $\partial V$, and the volume integral is the loss to **dissipation** (p. 130). For the whole fluid, with $\vec v = 0$ at infinity (or on the boundary), the flux term vanishes. For incompressible flow, where $\sigma_{ij} = \eta(\partial v_i/\partial x_j + \partial v_j/\partial x_i)$, what remains is (p. 130)

<Formula label="Viscosity always dissipates energy" tex="\frac{\partial E}{\partial t} = -\int_V\eta\left(\frac{\partial v_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial v_j}{\partial x_i}\right)\frac{\partial v_i}{\partial x_j}\,dV = -\frac{\eta}{2}\int_V\left(\frac{\partial v_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial v_j}{\partial x_i}\right)^2dV < 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.6, eq. (6.119), p. 130" />

The second form follows by swapping the summed indices $i$ and $j$ in half of the integrand. The integrand is a square, so friction always removes energy, as it should, and this is also why $\eta$ must be positive (p. 130). (The step to $\sigma_{ij} = \eta(\ldots)$ is ours; the notes use it without comment.)

The energy lost is not destroyed: it heats the fluid. Entropy conservation becomes, at temperature $T$ (p. 130),

<Formula label="Entropy production by viscosity" tex="\rho T\dot S = \sigma_{ij}\,\frac{\partial v_i}{\partial x_j}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.6, p. 130" />

with the heat gained per unit volume on the left and the energy dissipated by viscosity on the right. If heat conduction is allowed too, a term $\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla T)$, with conductivity $\kappa$, joins the right side (p. 130).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Damping by diffusion in the early universe">
In the photon-baryon fluid before recombination, the photons and electrons were not perfectly coupled. As 8.902 describes in its lecture on [CMB anisotropies](/courses/8-902/cmb-anisotropies), photons random-walk out of the smallest hot and cold patches and mix them, smearing out the finest ripples: **Silk damping**. Momentum carried across a flow by particles that wander between layers is the microscopic origin of viscosity (our remark), so this damping is the early universe's version of the friction of this section.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Friction acts where $\partial v_i/\partial x_j \neq 0$. To first order, $\sigma_{ij} = \eta(\partial_jv_i + \partial_iv_j - \tfrac23\delta_{ij}\nabla\cdot\vec v) + \zeta\delta_{ij}\nabla\cdot\vec v$, built to vanish for uniform motion and rigid rotation.
- The momentum flux becomes $T_{ij} = P\delta_{ij} + \rho v_iv_j - \sigma_{ij}$, and the Euler equation becomes the **Navier-Stokes equation**; for incompressible flow, $\partial\vec v/\partial t + (\vec v\cdot\nabla)\vec v = -\nabla P/\rho + \nu\nabla^2\vec v$ with $\nu = \eta/\rho$.
- Its divergence gives $\nabla^2P = -\rho\,(\partial_jv_i)(\partial_iv_j)$; its curl shows vorticity diffusing, $\partial\vec\Omega/\partial t - \nabla\times(\vec v\times\vec\Omega) = \nu\nabla^2\vec\Omega$.
- **No slip:** $\vec v = \vec v_{\text{wall}}$. The force per area on a surface is $-Pn_i + \sigma_{ij}n_j$.
- Viscosity always dissipates: $\partial E/\partial t = -\frac{\eta}{2}\int(\partial_jv_i + \partial_iv_j)^2dV$, and the energy becomes heat, $\rho T\dot S = \sigma_{ij}\partial_jv_i$.
