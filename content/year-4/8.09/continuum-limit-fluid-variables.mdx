So far every system in 8.09 has been a set of masses, even the rigid body, which moved as one object. Chapter 6 turns to a system that is continuous from the start: a **fluid**. Liquids and gases are fluids (plasmas too, but they need electromagnetism and are left out). For the notes, a fluid is matter that can be treated as continuous, that flows, and that resists deformation only weakly: it supports very little **shear stress**, the force along a surface rather than across it (p. 104). Fluids turn up in weather and oceans, plumbing and medicine, aircraft, stars and the early universe.

Treating a fluid means replacing the discrete coordinates $q_i$ of earlier chapters by **fields**, functions of continuous position. This section first does that for the simplest case, a chain of masses, and then sets up the variables used for fluids for the rest of the chapter (pp. 104–109). The notes' figures are not reproduced; the worked example is our own.

## A chain of masses becomes an elastic rod

Take an infinite line of masses $m$, equally spaced a distance $a$ apart, each joined to its neighbours by a spring of constant $k$ (p. 104). It is a model of a long elastic rod. Let $\eta_i$ be how far the $i$-th mass has moved from its equilibrium place. The springs store energy in the stretches $\eta_{i+1} - \eta_i$ and the masses carry kinetic energy (p. 105):

<Formula label="Potential and kinetic energy of the chain" tex="V = \frac{k}{2}\sum_i\left(\eta_{i+1} - \eta_i\right)^2, \qquad T = \frac{m}{2}\sum_i\dot\eta_i^2" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.1, eq. (6.1), p. 105" />

The spacing $a$ is not a physical input here: every factor of $a$ inserted below cancels, so the Lagrangian and the equations of motion are unchanged. It is still useful to write the Lagrangian with factors of $a$ placed so that a limit $a \to 0$ is easy to see (p. 105):

<Formula label="The Lagrangian of the chain" tex="L = T - V = \frac{1}{2}\sum_i a\left[\frac{m}{a}\,\dot\eta_i^2 - ka\left(\frac{\eta_{i+1} - \eta_i}{a}\right)^2\right]" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.1, eq. (6.2), p. 105" />

Each mass appears in two spring terms, so the Euler-Lagrange equation for $\eta_i$ involves its two neighbours (p. 105):

<Formula label="Equation of motion of mass i" tex="\frac{m}{a}\,\ddot\eta_i - ka\left(\frac{\eta_{i+1} - \eta_i}{a^2}\right) + ka\left(\frac{\eta_i - \eta_{i-1}}{a^2}\right) = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.1, eq. (6.3), p. 105" />

Now let the masses crowd together, $a \to 0$. The real question is what to keep fixed. If we pack twice as many masses into each metre, each must be half as heavy for the rod to weigh the same, and each spring is half as long and so twice as stiff. The fixed quantities are therefore the **mass per unit length** $\mu = m/a$ and $Y = ka$, which plays the part of the rod's **Young's modulus**, its stiffness against stretching (strictly Young's modulus times the cross-section, which is why $Y$ comes out in newtons): a strain $\xi$ (extension per unit length) needs a force $F = Y\xi$ (p. 105).

In the limit the label $i$ becomes a continuous position $x$, and $\eta_i(t)$ becomes a function of two variables, $\eta(x, t)$. Differences turn into derivatives and sums into integrals (p. 105):

<Formula label="The continuum limit" tex="\frac{\eta(x + a, t) - \eta(x, t)}{a} \to \frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}, \qquad \frac{\eta(x + a, t) - 2\eta(x, t) + \eta(x - a, t)}{a^2} \to \frac{\partial^2\eta}{\partial x^2}, \qquad \sum_i a \to \int dx" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.1, eqs. (6.4)–(6.6), p. 105" />

(The notes write the middle line as a difference of two first differences, divided by $a$; it is the same expression.) The Lagrangian becomes $L = \int\mathcal{L}\,dx$, with the **Lagrangian density** (p. 105)

<Formula label="Lagrangian density of the elastic rod" tex="\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\mu\left(\frac{\partial\eta(x, t)}{\partial t}\right)^2 - Y\left(\frac{\partial\eta(x, t)}{\partial x}\right)^2\right]" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.1, eq. (6.7), p. 105" />

and the equation of motion becomes the **wave equation** (p. 105):

<Formula label="The wave equation for the rod" tex="\mu\,\frac{\partial^2\eta}{\partial t^2} - Y\,\frac{\partial^2\eta}{\partial x^2} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.1, eq. (6.8), p. 105" />

Its waves travel at $\sqrt{Y/\mu}$ (our remark; compare the [beaded string](/courses/8-03/beaded-string-wave-equation), where the same limit is taken for transverse motion).

## Hamilton's principle for a field

The Lagrangian density and the wave equation are not independent: the second follows from the first, as for particles (p. 106). Let $\mathcal{L}$ depend on the field, its two first derivatives, and possibly on $x$ and $t$. Write $\dot\eta = \partial\eta/\partial t$ and $\eta' = \partial\eta/\partial x$ for short (our notation). Then (p. 106)

<Formula label="A general Lagrangian density" tex="\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}\left(\eta, \eta', \dot\eta, x, t\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.1, eq. (6.9), p. 106" />

and **Hamilton's principle** asks the action, now an integral over both space and time, to be stationary (p. 106):

<Formula label="Hamilton's principle for a field" tex="\delta S = \delta\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\,dx\,dt = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.1, eq. (6.10), p. 106" />

Varying $\eta(x, t)$ changes $\mathcal{L}$ through its first three arguments. The terms with $\delta\dot\eta$ are integrated by parts in $t$, those with $\delta\eta'$ by parts in $x$, the surface terms are set to zero, and what multiplies the arbitrary $\delta\eta$ must vanish. That gives the **continuum Euler-Lagrange equation** (p. 106):

<Formula label="Continuum Euler-Lagrange equation" tex="\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot\eta}\right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\eta'}\right) - \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\eta} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.1, eq. (6.11), p. 106" />

$N$ particles have $N$ Euler-Lagrange equations; here there is only one. But it holds at every $x$, so it really stands for infinitely many equations, one per point, and the $x$-derivative is what couples neighbouring points, just as the springs coupled neighbouring masses (p. 106).

For the rod (p. 106),

<Formula label="Derivatives of the rod's Lagrangian density" tex="\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot\eta} = \mu\,\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\eta'} = -Y\,\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\eta} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.1, eq. (6.12), p. 106" />

and putting them into the Euler-Lagrange equation gives back $\mu\,\partial^2\eta/\partial t^2 - Y\,\partial^2\eta/\partial x^2 = 0$.

A function like $\eta(x, t)$, a generalized coordinate labelled by a continuous position, is a **classical field**; $t$ is a parameter and $x$ a continuous label (p. 106). In three dimensions the field depends on three labels, $\eta(\vec r, t)$. If its value is one number it is a **scalar field**; if it is a vector, a **vector field**, like the electric and magnetic fields $\vec E(\vec r, t)$ and $\vec B(\vec r, t)$. Classical fields are also where quantum field theory starts: one writes a Lagrangian density, usually Lorentz invariant, for fields such as the electromagnetic potentials $\phi(\vec r, t)$ and $\vec A(\vec r, t)$, and then quantizes them (p. 106). The fluid fields of this chapter stay classical.

## Lagrangian and Eulerian variables

Picture fluid moving along a tube (p. 107). One natural description labels every **fluid element** (a point of the fluid, or a very small region of it) by where it was at $t = 0$, $\vec r_0$, and follows it: $\vec r = \vec r(\vec r_0, t)$. This is the **Lagrangian** description. Its advantage is that the ordinary laws of mechanics apply directly to each element; its drawback is that it is awkward to connect to what one measures (p. 107).

The notes use the other description. Stand at a fixed point $\vec r$ and record, as time goes on, what the fluid there is doing: its velocity $\vec v(\vec r, t)$, density $\rho(\vec r, t)$ and pressure $P(\vec r, t)$. These five functions (two scalars and three velocity components) fix the state of the moving fluid: this is the **Eulerian** description (p. 107). Given an equation of state, $\rho$ and $P$ also fix every other thermodynamic quantity. For an ideal gas at temperature $T$, $PV = nRT$; dividing by the volume, the pressure is proportional to the density, $P = \rho R' T$ with a rescaled gas constant $R'$ (p. 107).

## Pressure is the same in every direction

Pressure is force per unit area, $P = \hat n\cdot\vec F/A$, with $\hat n$ perpendicular to the area $A$ (p. 107). In a fluid it does not depend on which way the area faces: it is **isotropic**, so at each point and time it is one number, a scalar field $P(\vec r, t)$.

The argument balances forces on a small wedge of fluid at rest in gravity (pp. 107–108). The wedge has a vertical face $dy\,dz$ with pressure $P_2$, a bottom face $dx\,dy$ with pressure $P_3$, and a slanted face of area $dA$, tilted at angle $\theta$, with pressure $P_1$. [The text on p. 107 names the three pressures "$P_1, P_1, P_1$"; the figure and equations make clear that $P_1$, $P_2$ and $P_3$ are meant.] Its volume is $dx\,dy\,dz/2$, so gravity pulls down with (p. 108)

<Formula label="Weight of the wedge" tex="F_g = \rho g\,\frac{dx\,dy\,dz}{2}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.1, eq. (6.13), p. 108" />

The slanted face has $dA = dy\,dz/\sin\theta = dy\,dx/\cos\theta$. Balancing forces in $x$ and in $z$ (p. 108):

<Formula label="Force balance on the wedge" tex="0 = dF_x = P_2\,dy\,dz - P_1\sin\theta\,dA = (P_2 - P_1)\,dy\,dz, \qquad 0 = dF_z = P_3\,dx\,dy - P_1\cos\theta\,dA - \tfrac{1}{2}\rho g\,dx\,dy\,dz" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.1, eq. (6.14), p. 108" />

The first gives $P_1 = P_2$. Dividing the second by $dx\,dy$ (p. 108),

<Formula label="Vertical balance" tex="P_3 = P_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho g\,dz" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.1, eq. (6.15), p. 108" />

and as the wedge shrinks, $dz \to 0$, so $P_1 = P_3$ (eq. 6.16). All three pressures agree. The reason is a matter of powers: pressure forces go with areas, gravity with the volume, which carries one more power of the small length and so vanishes faster as the wedge shrinks. The same happens if the fluid moves or accelerates: the extra term $\rho\vec a\,dx\,dy\,dz$ (eq. 6.17, p. 108) is again a volume term and drops out for a small enough element. [Strictly the wedge's volume brings a factor $\tfrac12$ here too, as in (6.13); it does not matter, since the term vanishes in the limit.]

## Two time derivatives

With Eulerian fields, "how fast does it change" has two meanings (p. 109). The **partial derivative** $\partial/\partial t$ is the rate at a fixed point $\vec r$. The **total derivative** $d/dt$ is the rate seen by a fluid element as it moves. For the pressure, by the chain rule with $dx/dt = v_x$ and so on (p. 109),

<Formula label="Total derivative of the pressure" tex="\frac{dP}{dt} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial P}{\partial x}\,\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\,\frac{dy}{dt} + \frac{\partial P}{\partial z}\,\frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial t} + \vec v\cdot\nabla P" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.1, eq. (6.18), p. 109" />

and the same holds for any fluid field, scalar, vector or tensor (p. 109):

<Formula label="The total (convective) time derivative" tex="\frac{d}{dt} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + \vec v\cdot\nabla" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.1, eq. (6.19), p. 109" />

The second term is there because an element is carried to places where the field has a different value, even if nothing changes in time at any fixed point.

<WorkedExample title="A warming station and a cooling parcel">
<Problem>
Air moves due east at a steady 10 m/s. Over the region the temperature falls towards the east by 2 K per 100 km, and a weather station records its temperature rising by 0.5 K per hour. At what rate does the temperature of a parcel of moving air change?
</Problem>
<Step title="The two terms">
The station measures the partial derivative, $\partial T/\partial t = +0.5$ K/h. The gradient is $\partial T/\partial x = -2\ \text{K}/10^5\ \text{m} = -2 \times 10^{-5}$ K/m, so the carried term is $v\,\partial T/\partial x = 10 \times (-2 \times 10^{-5}) = -2 \times 10^{-4}$ K/s, which is $-0.72$ K/h.
</Step>
<Step title="The total derivative">
$dT/dt = \partial T/\partial t + \vec v\cdot\nabla T = 0.5 - 0.72 = -0.22$ K/h. The parcel cools by about 0.22 K each hour, although the thermometer at the fixed station shows warming: the air arriving there comes from the warmer west, while each parcel itself drifts into colder surroundings faster than the region warms.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The fluid equations of the next sections are written instead in the Eulerian way, which is how 8.902 writes them for the matter of the universe.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A fluid is matter treated as continuous, able to flow, with little resistance to shear; describing it means working with **fields** instead of discrete coordinates.
- A chain of masses $m$ with springs $k$ at spacing $a$ becomes, as $a \to 0$ with $\mu = m/a$ and $Y = ka$ fixed, a rod with Lagrangian density $\mathcal{L} = \frac12[\mu(\partial\eta/\partial t)^2 - Y(\partial\eta/\partial x)^2]$ and the wave equation $\mu\,\partial^2\eta/\partial t^2 = Y\,\partial^2\eta/\partial x^2$.
- Hamilton's principle $\delta\int\!\!\int\mathcal{L}\,dx\,dt = 0$ gives the continuum Euler-Lagrange equation, one equation that holds at every point.
- **Lagrangian** variables follow elements, $\vec r(\vec r_0, t)$; **Eulerian** variables record $\rho$, $P$ and $\vec v$ at fixed points, five fields in all.
- Pressure is **isotropic**: on a shrinking wedge, area forces must balance on their own because volume forces vanish faster.
- Following an element, $d/dt = \partial/\partial t + \vec v\cdot\nabla$.
