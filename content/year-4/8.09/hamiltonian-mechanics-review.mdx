The [previous page](/courses/8-09/lagrangian-mechanics-review) reviewed the Lagrangian half of Section 1.2 of Stewart's notes. This page covers the Hamiltonian half (notes pp. 8–12), which the course builds on heavily in Chapters 4 and 5. It follows the notes' order, in its own words, with its own worked example.

## Momenta as coordinates

In Hamiltonian mechanics the **canonical momenta**

<Formula label="Canonical momentum" tex="p_i \equiv \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_i}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, p. 8" />

become independent variables, just as important as the $q_i$ themselves (notes p. 8). The pairs $(q, p)$ are **canonical variables**, and the space they span is **phase space**. In this language the Euler–Lagrange equations just say $\dot p_i = \partial L/\partial q_i$. The canonical momentum need not be the familiar $m_i\dot q_i$: it differs whenever the potential depends on the velocities, as for a charge in a magnetic field below.

From here on the notes use the **summation convention**: an index that appears twice in a term is summed over, unless stated otherwise.

## The Legendre transformation

The Hamiltonian is built from the Lagrangian by a **Legendre transformation**:

<Formula label="Legendre transformation" tex="H(q, p, t) = \dot q_i\,p_i - L(q, \dot q, t)" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.24), p. 8" />

where the velocities on the right are to be written in terms of $q$, $p$ and $t$. The motion then obeys **Hamilton's equations**, $2N$ first-order equations in place of $N$ second-order ones:

<Formula label="Hamilton's equations" tex="\dot q_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.25), p. 8" />

and the explicit time dependence passes over with a sign change:

<Formula label="Explicit time dependence" tex="\frac{\partial H}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.26), p. 8" />

The notes prove all three at once, for one coordinate, by comparing two expressions for the small change $dH$. On one side, $H$ is a function of $q$, $p$ and $t$:

<Formula label="The change in H, from H(q, p, t)" tex="dH = \frac{\partial H}{\partial q}\,dq + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\,dp + \frac{\partial H}{\partial t}\,dt" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.27), p. 8" />

On the other side, from the definition, $dH = p\,d\dot q + \dot q\,dp - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q}dq - \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q}d\dot q - \frac{\partial L}{\partial t}dt$. The two $d\dot q$ terms cancel because $p = \partial L/\partial \dot q$; that cancellation is the point of the Legendre transformation, and it leaves

<Formula label="The change in H, from the definition" tex="dH = \dot q\,dp - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\,dq - \frac{\partial L}{\partial t}\,dt" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.28), p. 8" />

Since $dq$, $dp$ and $dt$ can be varied independently, their coefficients must agree: $\partial H/\partial p = \dot q$, $\partial H/\partial q = -\partial L/\partial q = -\dot p$ (using the Euler–Lagrange equation), and $\partial H/\partial t = -\partial L/\partial t$.

The two Hamilton equations play different roles at first sight. The first, $\dot q_i = \partial H/\partial p_i$, just undoes the definition $p_i = \partial L/\partial \dot q_i$, giving the velocities back. The second, $\dot p_i = -\partial H/\partial q_i$, carries the dynamics, Newton's law. Yet the Hamiltonian view gives positions and momenta the same status, so neither equation is more basic than the other. In practice the definition of $p_i$ is needed only once, to construct $H$; after that the Lagrangian plays no further part.

## Constraints first, then the Hamiltonian

For the particle in a cone from the previous page, the notes sketch the possible routes (notes p. 9). From the Lagrangian in $(r, \theta, z)$ one can impose $z = r\cot\alpha$ to get a Lagrangian in $(r, \theta)$, and then either find the Euler–Lagrange equations or pass to $H(r, \theta, p_r, p_\theta)$ and Hamilton's equations. One could also pass first to a Hamiltonian in all three coordinates and their momenta, but that route is never taken here: **in 8.09 constraints are always applied to the Lagrangian**, before any Hamiltonian is formed. (Stewart points the curious to Dirac's short book *Lectures on Quantum Mechanics*, which handles constraints in Hamiltonians, including more general ones.)

The two formalisms are equivalent, but each has its strengths. For the Hamiltonian one they include the geometry of phase space with Liouville's theorem, Poisson brackets and the link to quantum mechanics, and the Hamilton–Jacobi theory of transformations, all promised for later in the course. (Poisson brackets and Hamilton–Jacobi theory follow in Chapter 4; the posted notes have no section on Liouville's theorem.)

## When H is the energy

Suppose the kinetic energy is quadratic in the velocities, $T = \frac12 T_{jk}(q)\,\dot q_j\dot q_k$ with a symmetric matrix $T_{jk}$, and $V = V(q)$, with $L = T - V$ (notes p. 9). Then

<Formula label="Velocities times momenta" tex="\dot q_i\,p_i = \dot q_i\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_i} = \frac12\dot q_i T_{ik}\dot q_k + \frac12\dot q_j T_{ji}\dot q_i = 2T" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.30), p. 9" />

(a special case of Euler's theorem for functions that are homogeneous of degree 2), and so

<Formula label="H is the energy" tex="H = \dot q_i p_i - L = T + V = E" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.31), p. 9" />

## A general recipe for H

A wider class of Lagrangians has terms of degree 0, 1 and 2 in the velocities (notes pp. 9–10):

<Formula label="Lagrangian up to quadratic order in the velocities" tex="L(q, \dot q, t) = L_0 + a_j\dot q_j + \frac12\dot q_j T_{jk}\dot q_k" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.32), p. 9" />

where $L_0$, $a_j$ and the symmetric $T_{jk}$ may all depend on $q$ and $t$. (The notes also write this with arrows, treating $q$, $a$ and $p$ as columns of numbers, even though their entries may have different units, such as a length and an angle.) The momenta are

<Formula label="Momenta for this Lagrangian" tex="p_j = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_j} = a_j + T_{jk}\dot q_k" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.34), p. 10" />

Kinetic energy is positive for any motion, so the matrix $T_{jk}$ is positive definite and has an inverse; then $\dot q_j = (T^{-1})_{jk}(p_k - a_k)$, and $H = \dot q_j p_j - L$ works out to

<Formula label="Hamiltonian for this Lagrangian" tex="H = \frac12\,(p_j - a_j)\,(T^{-1})_{jk}\,(p_k - a_k) - L_0(q, t)" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.35), p. 10" />

So for any Lagrangian of this form, inverting one matrix gives the Hamiltonian at once.

**A charge in electromagnetic fields.** For a charge $e$ in a scalar potential $\phi$ and vector potential $\vec A$ (SI units), $L = \frac12 m\vec v^2 - e\phi + e\vec A\cdot\vec v$. In Cartesian coordinates $a_j = eA_j$, $T_{jk} = m\,\delta_{jk}$ and $L_0 = -e\phi$, so

<Formula label="Hamiltonian of a charge" tex="H = \frac{1}{2m}\left(\vec p - e\vec A\right)^2 + e\phi" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.36), p. 10" />

which, as shown in earlier courses, gives the Lorentz force $m\dot{\vec v} = e(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B)$.

## A pendulum hanging from a sliding block

The notes' main example (notes pp. 10–12) is the system mentioned on the previous page: a block of mass $m_1$ slides without friction along a horizontal rail (the $x$ axis), and from it hangs a pendulum, a bob of mass $m_2$ on a rigid massless rod of length $\ell$. Two constraints, $y_1 = 0$ and a fixed rod length,

<Formula label="Constraints" tex="y_1 = 0, \qquad (y_1 - y_2)^2 + (x_1 - x_2)^2 = \ell^2" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.37), p. 10" />

reduce four coordinates to two. Before the constraints,

<Formula label="Lagrangian before the constraints" tex="L = \frac{m_1}{2}\dot x_1^2 + \frac{m_2}{2}\left(\dot x_2^2 + \dot y_2^2\right) - m_2 g y_2 - m_1 g y_1" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.38), p. 10" />

Take the block's position $x \equiv x_1$ and the rod's angle $\theta$ from the downward vertical. Then $x_2 = x + \ell\sin\theta$, $y_2 = -\ell\cos\theta$, with velocities $\dot x_2 = \dot x + \ell\cos\theta\,\dot\theta$ and $\dot y_2 = \ell\sin\theta\,\dot\theta$, and, simplifying with $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$,

<Formula label="Lagrangian with the constraints imposed" tex="L = \frac{m_1 + m_2}{2}\dot x^2 + m_2\ell\cos\theta\,\dot x\dot\theta + \frac{m_2\ell^2}{2}\dot\theta^2 + m_2 g\ell\cos\theta" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.39), p. 10" />

The momenta are

<Formula label="Momenta" tex="p_x = (m_1 + m_2)\dot x + m_2\ell\cos\theta\,\dot\theta, \qquad p_\theta = m_2\ell\cos\theta\,\dot x + m_2\ell^2\dot\theta" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.40), p. 10" />

$p_x$ is not just a mass times $\dot x$; it is the total momentum of block and bob. This Lagrangian is of the general form with $a_j = 0$, $L_0 = m_2 g\ell\cos\theta$ and the velocity matrix

<Formula label="The velocity matrix and its inverse" tex="T = \begin{pmatrix} m_1 + m_2 & m_2\ell\cos\theta \\ m_2\ell\cos\theta & m_2\ell^2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad T^{-1} = \frac{1}{m_2\ell^2\left(m_1 + m_2\sin^2\theta\right)}\begin{pmatrix} m_2\ell^2 & -m_2\ell\cos\theta \\ -m_2\ell\cos\theta & m_1 + m_2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eqs. (1.41)–(1.42), p. 11" />

(the determinant is $m_2\ell^2(m_1 + m_2) - m_2^2\ell^2\cos^2\theta = m_2\ell^2(m_1 + m_2\sin^2\theta)$). The recipe then gives

<Formula label="Hamiltonian of the pendulum on a sliding block" tex="H = \frac{m_2\ell^2 p_x^2 + (m_1 + m_2)p_\theta^2 - 2m_2\ell\cos\theta\,p_x p_\theta}{2m_2\ell^2\left(m_1 + m_2\sin^2\theta\right)} - m_2 g\ell\cos\theta" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.43), p. 11" />

Hamilton's equations for the pair $(x, p_x)$ are

<Formula label="Equations for x" tex="\dot x = \frac{p_x}{m_1 + m_2\sin^2\theta} - \frac{\cos\theta\,p_\theta}{\ell\left(m_1 + m_2\sin^2\theta\right)}, \qquad \dot p_x = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial x} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.44), p. 11" />

The total momentum is conserved, as it must be with no horizontal external force. The pair $(\theta, p_\theta)$ gives a longer pair of coupled non-linear equations, which a computer can integrate:

<Formula label="Equations for theta" tex="\dot\theta = \frac{(m_1 + m_2)p_\theta - m_2\ell\cos\theta\,p_x}{m_2\ell^2\left(m_1 + m_2\sin^2\theta\right)}, \qquad \dot p_\theta = \frac{\sin\theta\cos\theta\left[m_2\ell^2 p_x^2 + (m_1 + m_2)p_\theta^2 - 2m_2\ell\cos\theta\,p_x p_\theta\right]}{\ell^2\left(m_1 + m_2\sin^2\theta\right)^2} - m_2 g\ell\sin\theta - \frac{\sin\theta\,p_x p_\theta}{\ell\left(m_1 + m_2\sin^2\theta\right)}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.45), p. 11" />

(In the last term of $\dot p_\theta$ the notes print the denominator as $\ell(m_1 + m_2\sin\theta)$; differentiating $H$ shows that the square on $\sin\theta$ belongs there, as written here.)

To check them by hand, take small angles, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ and $\cos\theta \approx 1$, and work in the frame where the total momentum is zero, $p_x = 0$. For small swings $p_\theta$ is small too, so the term $\theta p_\theta^2$ is of higher order and can be dropped. What remains is

<Formula label="Small oscillations" tex="\dot x = -\frac{p_\theta}{\ell m_1}, \qquad \dot\theta = \frac{p_\theta}{\mu\ell^2}, \qquad \dot p_\theta = -m_2 g\ell\,\theta" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.47), p. 12" />

where $\mu = m_1 m_2/(m_1 + m_2)$ is the **reduced mass** of block and bob. Combining the last two, $\ddot\theta = \dot p_\theta/(\mu\ell^2) = -(m_2 g/\mu\ell)\,\theta$: simple harmonic motion with

<Formula label="Frequency of the small oscillations" tex="\omega^2 = \frac{m_2\,g}{\mu\,\ell} = \frac{g}{\ell}\left(1 + \frac{m_2}{m_1}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2, p. 12" />

(the second form is ours). A heavy block, $m_1 \gg m_2$, is a fixed pivot and gives the familiar $g/\ell$. A light block lets the pivot swing back and forth against the bob, both moving about their common centre of mass, and the oscillation is faster.

<WorkedExample title="A spherical pendulum, the quick way">
<Problem>
A bob of mass $m$ on a light rod of length $\ell$ can swing in any direction about a fixed pivot. Use the recipe above to write down its Hamiltonian, with $\theta$ the angle from the downward vertical and $\phi$ the azimuth. Then find how fast it must go round to circle steadily at $\theta = 60^\circ$ when $\ell = 0.50$ m.
</Problem>
<Step title="The Lagrangian">
The bob's speed squared is $\ell^2\dot\theta^2 + \ell^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2$ and its height is $-\ell\cos\theta$, so $L = \frac{m\ell^2}{2}\left(\dot\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2\right) + mg\ell\cos\theta$. This has $a_j = 0$, $L_0 = mg\ell\cos\theta$, and a diagonal velocity matrix with entries $m\ell^2$ and $m\ell^2\sin^2\theta$.
</Step>
<Step title="The Hamiltonian">
Inverting a diagonal matrix inverts its entries, so $H = \frac{p_\theta^2}{2m\ell^2} + \frac{p_\phi^2}{2m\ell^2\sin^2\theta} - mg\ell\cos\theta$. It is quadratic in the momenta with $V$ depending only on $\theta$, so $H$ is the energy.
</Step>
<Step title="Hamilton's equations">
$\phi$ does not appear, so $\dot p_\phi = 0$: $p_\phi = m\ell^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi$ is constant. For $\theta$, $\dot p_\theta = -\partial H/\partial\theta = \frac{p_\phi^2\cos\theta}{m\ell^2\sin^3\theta} - mg\ell\sin\theta$.
</Step>
<Step title="Steady circling">
A steady circle needs $\dot p_\theta = 0$. Putting $p_\phi = m\ell^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi$ in, $m\ell^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2 = mg\ell\sin\theta$, so $\dot\phi^2 = g/(\ell\cos\theta)$. At $60^\circ$, $\cos\theta = 0.5$ and $\dot\phi^2 = 9.81/0.25 = 39.2\ \text{s}^{-2}$, so $\dot\phi = 6.26$ rad/s: one turn every $2\pi/6.26 = 1.00$ s. This is the conical pendulum of first-year mechanics, found here without drawing a force diagram.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The canonical momenta $p_i = \partial L/\partial \dot q_i$ join the coordinates $q_i$ as phase-space variables; they need not equal $m_i\dot q_i$.
- The Legendre transformation $H = \dot q_i p_i - L$ gives Hamilton's equations $\dot q_i = \partial H/\partial p_i$, $\dot p_i = -\partial H/\partial q_i$, and $\partial H/\partial t = -\partial L/\partial t$.
- This course imposes constraints on the Lagrangian first and only then passes to $H$.
- If $T$ is quadratic in the velocities and $V = V(q)$, then $H = T + V = E$.
- For $L = L_0 + a_j\dot q_j + \frac12\dot q_j T_{jk}\dot q_k$, $H = \frac12(p - a)\cdot T^{-1}\cdot(p - a) - L_0$; a charge in fields has $H = (\vec p - e\vec A)^2/2m + e\phi$.
- A pendulum hanging from a freely sliding block conserves the total momentum $p_x$ and oscillates with $\omega^2 = m_2 g/(\mu\ell)$, $\mu$ the reduced mass.
