As an extended example, Section 4.5 of the notes (pp. 79–83) solves the Kepler problem twice: first with the Lagrangian and conservation laws, then with the Hamilton-Jacobi equation of [the previous section](/courses/8-09/hamilton-jacobi-equation), in plane polar and then in spherical coordinates. The point of the second pass is to see the H-J machinery produce the same integrals almost automatically, with its constants turning out to be the energy and the angular momentum. This page follows the notes in order, with its own example of a satellite orbit.

## Reduction to one body

Two masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ interact through a potential $V(r)$ that depends only on their separation. With the centre of mass at rest at the origin, $\vec R = 0$, the problem is that of one particle with the **reduced mass** $m$ (notes p. 79):

<Formula label="Lagrangian of the two-body problem" tex="L = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{\vec r}^{\,2} - V(r), \qquad \frac{1}{m} \equiv \frac{1}{m_1} + \frac{1}{m_2}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.5, eq. (4.148), p. 79" />

A central potential exerts no torque, so **any** $V(r)$ conserves the angular momentum $\vec L = \vec r \times \vec p$, and $\vec r$ and $\vec p$ stay in the plane perpendicular to $\vec L$. In polar coordinates $(r, \psi)$ in that plane,

<Formula label="Lagrangian in the plane of the orbit" tex="L = \frac{m}{2}\left(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\psi^2\right) - V(r)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.5, eq. (4.149), p. 79" />

The angle $\psi$ does not appear, so it is cyclic and its momentum is conserved:

<Formula label="Conserved angular momentum" tex="p_\psi = mr^2\dot\psi \text{ is a constant}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.5, eq. (4.150), p. 79" />

It is the magnitude of the angular momentum, $p_\psi = |\vec L| \equiv \ell$; the notes write $\ell$ to keep it apart from the Lagrangian $L$.

## The effective potential

Eliminating $\dot\psi$ in favour of $\ell$, the conserved energy is (notes pp. 79–80)

<Formula label="Energy with the effective potential" tex="E = \frac{m}{2}\dot r^2 + V_{\text{eff}}(r), \qquad V_{\text{eff}}(r) = V(r) + \frac{\ell^2}{2mr^2}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.5, eqs. (4.151)–(4.152), pp. 79–80" />

The radial motion is one-dimensional motion in the **effective potential** $V_{\text{eff}}$. The extra term $\ell^2/(2mr^2)$ is the angular part of the kinetic energy: with $\ell$ fixed it grows as the body comes in and acts as a centrifugal barrier. For gravity, $V_{\text{eff}}$ rises steeply at small $r$, dips to a minimum, and climbs back towards zero from below at large $r$. A horizontal line at height $E$ meets the curve at the **turning points**, where $\dot r = 0$: one point for $E \geq 0$ (the body comes in once and leaves), two for $E < 0$ (the radius oscillates between them), and the minimum itself for a circular orbit.

Solving the energy equation for $\dot r = dr/dt$, writing it as $dt = dr/(\ldots)$ and integrating, gives the radial motion implicitly (notes p. 80; they say the Euler-Lagrange equation is solved for $\dot r$, but it is the energy equation that is used):

<Formula label="Radial motion" tex="t = t(r) = \int_{r_0}^{r}\frac{dr'}{\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{m}\left(E - V(r') - \dfrac{\ell^2}{2mr'^2}\right)}}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.5, eq. (4.153), p. 80" />

For the shape of the orbit, use $\dot\psi = d\psi/dt = \ell/(mr^2)$ from eq. (4.150) to replace $dt$ by $d\psi$:

<Formula label="Orbit equation" tex="\psi - \psi_0 = \ell\int_{r_0}^{r}\frac{dr'}{r'^2\sqrt{2m\left(E - V(r') - \dfrac{\ell^2}{2mr'^2}\right)}}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.5, eq. (4.154), p. 80" />

## The Kepler orbits

For the inverse-square force, $V(r) = -k/r$, the orbit integral can be done, with the result (notes p. 81)

<Formula label="Kepler orbit" tex="\frac{1}{r(\psi)} = \frac{mk}{\ell^2}\left(1 + \varepsilon\cos(\psi - \psi')\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.5, eq. (4.155), p. 81" />

a conic section with the attracting centre at a focus, $\psi'$ the direction of closest approach, and **eccentricity**

<Formula label="Eccentricity" tex="\varepsilon \equiv \sqrt{1 + \frac{2E\ell^2}{mk^2}}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.5, eq. (4.156), p. 81" />

The energy decides the shape: $\varepsilon = 0$, a circle, at the lowest energy allowed for a given $\ell$ (the bottom of $V_{\text{eff}}$); $0 < \varepsilon < 1$, an ellipse, for $E < 0$; $\varepsilon = 1$, a parabola, for $E = 0$; and $\varepsilon > 1$, a hyperbola, for $E > 0$. The last two are unbound.

<WorkedExample title="A satellite between 7000 and 14000 km">
<Problem>
A 1000 kg satellite orbits the Earth ($GM = 3.986 \times 10^{14}$ m³/s², so $k = GMm = 3.986 \times 10^{17}$ J m; the satellite is so light that the reduced mass is its own mass) between a perigee of $r_p = 7000$ km and an apogee of $r_a = 14000$ km from the Earth's centre. Find $\varepsilon$, $\ell$ and $E$, and check that the eccentricity formula and the turning points of $V_{\text{eff}}$ agree.
</Problem>
<Step title="Eccentricity and the orbit's size">
From the orbit equation the extremes are $r_p = p/(1 + \varepsilon)$ and $r_a = p/(1 - \varepsilon)$, with $p = \ell^2/(mk)$. Dividing, $\varepsilon = (r_a - r_p)/(r_a + r_p) = 7000/21000 = 1/3$, and $p = r_p(1 + \varepsilon) = 9333$ km.
</Step>
<Step title="Angular momentum and energy">
$\ell^2 = mkp = 1000 \times 3.986 \times 10^{17} \times 9.333 \times 10^{6} = 3.720 \times 10^{27}$, so $\ell = 6.10 \times 10^{13}$ kg m²/s. At the turning points $E = V_{\text{eff}}$: at perigee, $-k/r_p + \ell^2/(2mr_p^2) = -5.694 \times 10^{10} + 3.796 \times 10^{10} = -1.898 \times 10^{10}$ J. At apogee, $-2.847 \times 10^{10} + 0.949 \times 10^{10}$ gives the same $-1.898 \times 10^{10}$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with the eccentricity formula">
$2E\ell^2/(mk^2) = 2 \times (-1.898 \times 10^{10}) \times 3.720 \times 10^{27}/(1000 \times (3.986 \times 10^{17})^2) = -0.889$, so $\varepsilon = \sqrt{1 - 0.889} = \sqrt{0.111} = 1/3$, as it should be. The energy is also $-k/(r_p + r_a)$, minus $k$ over the major axis: $-3.986 \times 10^{17}/(2.1 \times 10^{7}) = -1.898 \times 10^{10}$ J.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Orbits as a measuring instrument">
The same physics, applied to stars in the gravitational field of a galaxy, gives the rotation curves of [Ferreras's dark-matter course](/courses/fundamentals-of-dark-matter/rotation-curves): a circular orbit sits at the bottom of $V_{\text{eff}}$, where the condition $dV_{\text{eff}}/dr = 0$ is the force balance $v^2 = GM(r)/r$ used there, so its speed measures the mass enclosed. Speeds that stay flat far outside the visible stars were one of the first signs of dark matter. In general relativity the effective potential gains an extra term, and orbits no longer close: the [perihelion of Mercury](/courses/8-033/schwarzschild-orbits-perihelion) precesses.
</Callout>

## The same problem by Hamilton-Jacobi, in the plane

Now take the H-J route (notes pp. 81–82). Assume the plane of the orbit is known and use $(r, \psi)$. The Hamiltonian, time-independent and equal to the energy, is

<Formula label="Hamiltonian in the plane" tex="H = \frac{1}{2m}\left(p_r^2 + \frac{p_\psi^2}{r^2}\right) + V(r) = \alpha_1 = E" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.5, eq. (4.157), p. 81" />

Since $\psi$ is cyclic, $p_\psi \equiv \alpha_\psi$ is a constant, and by Section 4.4 a cyclic coordinate separates: $W = W_1(r) + \alpha_\psi\psi$ (eq. (4.158)). The time-independent H-J equation becomes an ordinary equation in $r$ alone (eq. (4.159)), whose solution is

<Formula label="The radial part of W" tex="\frac{\partial W_1}{\partial r} = \sqrt{2m(\alpha_1 - V(r)) - \frac{\alpha_\psi^2}{r^2}}, \qquad W = \alpha_\psi\psi + \int\sqrt{2m(\alpha_1 - V(r)) - \frac{\alpha_\psi^2}{r^2}}\,dr" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.5, eqs. (4.160)–(4.161), p. 82" />

The transformation equations of Section 4.4, $\partial W/\partial\alpha_1 = t + \beta_1$ and $\partial W/\partial\alpha_\psi = \beta_2$, then read

<Formula label="The transformation equations" tex="t + \beta_1 = \frac{\partial W}{\partial\alpha_1} = m\int\frac{dr}{\sqrt{2m(\alpha_1 - V(r)) - \dfrac{\alpha_\psi^2}{r^2}}}, \qquad \beta_2 = \frac{\partial W}{\partial\alpha_\psi} = \psi - \alpha_\psi\int\frac{dr}{r^2\sqrt{2m(\alpha_1 - V(r)) - \dfrac{\alpha_\psi^2}{r^2}}}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.5, eq. (4.162), p. 82" />

These are the radial equation $t(r)$ and the orbit equation $\psi(r)$ of eqs. (4.153)–(4.154), obtained in one step, with $\alpha_\psi = \ell$ and $\alpha_1 = E$: the constants of the method are the physical constants of the orbit.

## In three dimensions

Suppose instead that we did not know the plane of the orbit, and use spherical coordinates $(r, \theta, \varphi)$ (notes pp. 82–83):

<Formula label="Hamiltonian in spherical coordinates" tex="H = \frac{1}{2m}\left(p_r^2 + \frac{p_\theta^2}{r^2} + \frac{p_\varphi^2}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\right) + V(r) = \alpha_1" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.5, eq. (4.163), p. 82" />

Try a fully separated form, $W = W_r(r) + W_\theta(\theta) + W_\varphi(\varphi)$ (eq. (4.164)). The angle $\varphi$ is cyclic, so $W_\varphi = \alpha_\varphi\varphi$ (eq. (4.165)), and multiplying the H-J equation by $2m$ leaves

<Formula label="H-J equation after removing φ" tex="\left(\frac{\partial W_r}{\partial r}\right)^2 + \frac{1}{r^2}\left(\left(\frac{\partial W_\theta}{\partial\theta}\right)^2 + \frac{\alpha_\varphi^2}{\sin^2\theta}\right) + 2mV(r) = 2m\alpha_1" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.5, eq. (4.166), p. 82" />

The bracket depends only on $\theta$ (eq. (4.167)), everything else only on $r$, so the bracket is a constant, written $\alpha_\theta^2$:

<Formula label="The angular separation" tex="\left(\frac{\partial W_\theta}{\partial\theta}\right)^2 + \frac{\alpha_\varphi^2}{\sin^2\theta} \equiv \alpha_\theta^2" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.5, eq. (4.168), p. 83" />

and the radial equation that remains,

<Formula label="The radial equation" tex="\left(\frac{\partial W_r}{\partial r}\right)^2 + \frac{\alpha_\theta^2}{r^2} = 2m(\alpha_1 - V(r))" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.5, eq. (4.169), p. 83" />

is exactly the planar one, with $\alpha_1 = E$ and $\alpha_\theta = \ell$. The angular equation, read with $p_\theta = \partial W_\theta/\partial\theta$ and $p_\varphi = \alpha_\varphi$, says

<Formula label="Total angular momentum" tex="p_\theta^2 + \frac{p_\varphi^2}{\sin^2\theta} = \ell^2" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.5, eq. (4.170), p. 83" />

the square of the total angular momentum, while $p_\varphi$ is its component along $\hat z$. So the spherical H-J solution carries three constants of the motion, $E$, $\ell$ and $\ell_z$, as its separation constants. Section 4.6 turns them into action variables.

## Summary

- The two-body problem with a central potential reduces to one body of reduced mass $m$; angular momentum $\ell = mr^2\dot\psi$ is conserved and the orbit is planar.
- The radial motion is one-dimensional in $V_{\text{eff}} = V + \ell^2/(2mr^2)$; turning points satisfy $V_{\text{eff}} = E$, and $t(r)$ and $\psi(r)$ are integrals.
- For $V = -k/r$: $1/r = (mk/\ell^2)(1 + \varepsilon\cos(\psi - \psi'))$ with $\varepsilon = \sqrt{1 + 2E\ell^2/(mk^2)}$: circle, ellipse, parabola, hyperbola as $E$ rises.
- By Hamilton-Jacobi, $\psi$ cyclic gives $W = W_1(r) + \alpha_\psi\psi$, and $\partial W/\partial\alpha_1 = t + \beta_1$, $\partial W/\partial\alpha_\psi = \beta_2$ reproduce $t(r)$ and $\psi(r)$ with $\alpha_1 = E$, $\alpha_\psi = \ell$.
- In spherical coordinates the equation separates fully; the separation constants are $E$, $\alpha_\theta = \ell$ and $\alpha_\varphi = \ell_z$.
