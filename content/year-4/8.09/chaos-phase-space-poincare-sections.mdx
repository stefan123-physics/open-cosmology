This chapter of Stewart's notes leaves the exactly solvable systems of earlier chapters and asks what non-linear equations do when they cannot be solved. The first section defines chaos, sets up the language of flows in phase space (fixed points, attractors, limit cycles) and introduces the tools used to picture chaotic motion, all built around one example: a damped pendulum pushed by a periodic force. The phase-portrait method itself (critical points, linearization, the undamped and damped pendulum) is the subject of the 18.03 page on [nonlinear autonomous systems](/courses/18-03/nonlinear-autonomous-systems); this page uses it. (Citations give the printed page numbers 142 to 152 of the Chapter 7 notes and their equation numbers.)

## What chaos means

A **deterministic** system is one whose equations, given initial conditions, have exactly one solution, as in classical mechanics. Stewart defines **chaos** in such a system as long-term behaviour that is aperiodic and depends sensitively on the initial conditions (notes p. 142):

- **Aperiodic** means trajectories in phase space neither settle onto a point nor onto a periodic orbit; they wander irregularly. Stewart counts **topological mixing** as part of this: take any open region of starting points, and in time its image overlaps every other region, so a chaotic trajectory roams over much more of phase space than a regular one (p. 143). (For a dissipative system, footnote 1 adds, the regions must be chosen with care, since some regions are left for good.)
- **Sensitive dependence on initial conditions** means that trajectories which start close together move apart exponentially. If $\delta(t)$ is the difference between points on two such trajectories at time $t$, then for some $\lambda > 0$ (p. 142; the notes write it as a proportionality, which we state as an approximate equality so that $\delta_0$ is the starting separation itself)

<Formula label="Exponential separation of nearby trajectories" tex="|\delta(t)| \approx |\delta_0|\,e^{\lambda t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 142 (Fig. 7.1 and text)" />

The consequence is that a chaotic system, though deterministic, is usually not predictable. Every measurement of the starting point has some error $\delta_0$, statistical or systematic, and that error is amplified exponentially until predictions far enough ahead are worthless (p. 142). Exponential growth on its own is not chaos (an inverted pendulum falling over grows exponentially too); it is the combination with aperiodic, mixing motion that matters (p. 143).

<WorkedExample title="A forecast horizon">
<Problem>
A chaotic model has $\lambda = 0.5$ per unit time. Its initial state is known to $\delta_0 = 10^{-6}$, and a prediction is useful while the error stays below $10^{-2}$. (a) How long is the prediction useful? (b) How much longer if the initial state were known a thousand times better?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The horizon">
Set $\delta_0 e^{\lambda t} = 10^{-2}$: $t = \ln(10^{-2}/10^{-6})/\lambda = \ln(10^{4})/0.5 = 9.21/0.5 \approx 18.4$ time units.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A thousand times better data">
With $\delta_0 = 10^{-9}$, $t = \ln(10^{7})/0.5 = 16.1/0.5 \approx 32.2$: only $\ln(1000)/\lambda \approx 13.8$ units more. Each factor of ten in accuracy buys the same fixed extra time, $\ln 10/\lambda \approx 4.6$ units.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Flows: first-order equations and uniqueness

Any dynamical system of interest can be written as $n$ first-order equations (notes p. 143, 7.1.1):

<Formula label="A dynamical system as first-order equations" tex="\dot x_1 = f_1(x_1, \ldots, x_n), \quad \dot x_2 = f_2(x_1, \ldots, x_n), \quad \ldots, \quad \dot x_n = f_n(x_1, \ldots, x_n)" cite="reading1" at="p. 143, eq. (7.1)" />

The $x_i$ may be coordinates, velocities, momenta or anything else that evolves; in this chapter they are usually made dimensionless. Hamilton's equations are of this form, with an even number of variables (p. 143). In short (p. 143):

<Formula label="Shorthand for the flow" tex="\dot{\vec x} = \vec f(\vec x)" cite="reading1" at="p. 143, eq. (7.2)" />

**Existence and uniqueness.** If the $f_i$ and their partial derivatives $\partial f_i/\partial x_j$ are continuous in a connected region $D$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$, then for any initial point $\vec x_0$ in $D$ there is one and only one solution $\vec x(t)$ through it, on some time interval around $t = 0$ (p. 143). This theorem is what makes the evolution deterministic.

## The damped driven pendulum

The example for the whole chapter is a pendulum of mass $m$ and length $l$ with damping and a periodic driving torque (notes p. 144):

<Formula label="Damped driven pendulum" tex="ml^2\ddot\theta + ml^2\gamma\dot\theta + mgl\sin\theta = A\cos(\omega_D t)" cite="reading1" at="p. 144, eq. (7.3)" />

where $\theta$ is the angle from the vertical, $\gamma$ the damping coefficient and $A\cos(\omega_D t)$ the drive. Three rescalings make it dimensionless (p. 144). Dividing by $mgl$ turns the drive strength into the number $a = A/(mgl)$ (eq. 7.4), leaving $(l/g)\ddot\theta + (\gamma l/g)\dot\theta + \sin\theta = a\cos(\omega_D t)$ (eq. 7.5). Measuring time in units of $\sqrt{l/g}$, that is, $t' = \sqrt{g/l}\,t$ and $\omega_D' = \sqrt{l/g}\,\omega_D$ (eq. 7.6), removes the first factor, and the damping becomes a **quality factor** $q$ with $1/q = \sqrt{l/g}\,\gamma$ (eq. 7.7). Dropping the primes (p. 144):

<Formula label="Dimensionless damped driven pendulum" tex="\ddot\theta + \frac{1}{q}\dot\theta + \sin\theta = a\cos(\omega_D t)" cite="reading1" at="p. 144, eq. (7.8)" />

with three dimensionless constants $a$, $q$ and $\omega_D$. Time appears explicitly in the drive; to remove it, treat the drive's phase $\varphi = \omega_D t$ as a variable of its own, and set $\omega = \dot\theta$. The result is three first-order equations of the form (7.1), with $\vec x = (\theta, \omega, \varphi)$ (p. 144):

<Formula label="The pendulum as a three-variable flow" tex="\dot\theta = \omega, \qquad \dot\omega = -\frac{1}{q}\,\omega - \sin\theta + a\cos\varphi, \qquad \dot\varphi = \omega_D" cite="reading1" at="p. 144, eq. (7.9)" />

<WorkedExample title="Dimensionless numbers for a real pendulum">
<Problem>
A pendulum of length $l = 0.50$ m has damping coefficient $\gamma = 0.80\ \text{s}^{-1}$ and is driven at $\omega_D = 2.0$ rad/s, with $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find the unit of time, the quality factor $q$ and the dimensionless drive frequency.
</Problem>
<Step title="Unit of time">
$\sqrt{l/g} = \sqrt{0.50/9.81} = 0.226$ s. One dimensionless time unit is $0.226$ s; a small free swing (natural angular frequency 1 in these units) has period $2\pi \times 0.226 = 1.42$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Quality factor and drive frequency">
$1/q = 0.226 \times 0.80 = 0.181$, so $q = 5.5$: lightly damped. The drive frequency becomes $\omega_D' = 0.226 \times 2.0 = 0.45$, well below the natural frequency 1.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Phase space: no crossings, and volumes

**Trajectories never cross** (notes p. 145, 7.1.2). Any point on a trajectory can serve as the initial condition of a new one; by uniqueness, only one trajectory passes through it. Two trajectories can come arbitrarily close but never meet or cross.

**Phase-space volume.** A region $\mathcal V$ of phase space has volume (p. 145)

<Formula label="Phase-space volume" tex="\mathcal V = \int_{\text{region}}\,\prod_{j=1}^{n} dx_j" cite="reading1" at="p. 145, eq. (7.10)" />

For Hamiltonian motion this volume is constant, $d\mathcal V/dt = 0$: time evolution is a canonical transformation (generated through the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, Chapter 4 of the notes), and canonical transformations preserve volume elements. Damping falls outside the Hamiltonian framework, and the notes answer the general case by analogy with a fluid (Chapter 6): a fluid volume moving with velocity field $\vec v(\vec r)$ changes at the rate $\int dV\,\nabla\cdot\vec v$. Phase space flows with "velocity" $\vec f$, so (p. 145)

<Formula label="Rate of change of a phase-space volume" tex="\dot{\vec x} = \vec f(\vec x) \quad\Rightarrow\quad d\mathcal V/dt = \int d\mathcal V\,\nabla\cdot\vec f" cite="reading1" at="p. 145, eq. (7.11)" />

where $\nabla$ now differentiates with respect to the $x_j$. This defines the two classes used from now on: $\nabla\cdot\vec f = 0$ is a **conservative** system, whether or not a Hamiltonian exists, and $\nabla\cdot\vec f < 0$ is a **dissipative** system, whose phase-space volumes shrink (p. 145). For the pendulum (7.9), only the middle equation depends on its own variable (p. 146):

<Formula label="Divergence of the pendulum flow" tex="\nabla\cdot\vec f = \frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\theta} + \frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}\!\left(-\frac{\omega}{q} - \sin\theta + a\cos\varphi\right) + \frac{\partial\omega_D}{\partial\varphi} = -\frac{1}{q} < 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 146, eq. (7.12)" />

so it is dissipative whenever $q$ is finite, and every volume shrinks by the factor $e^{-t/q}$ (our integration of (7.11) with a constant divergence). With $q \approx 5.5$ from the example above, a volume falls to $e^{-1}$ of its size in $q$ time units, which is $1/\gamma = 1.25$ s.

## Fixed points and attractors

Without damping or drive ($q \to \infty$, $a = 0$) the pendulum is $\dot\theta = \omega$, $\dot\omega = -\sin\theta$ (notes p. 146, eq. 7.13). It conserves the energy $E = \frac12\omega^2 - \cos\theta$ (in units of $mgl$; our label for the curves), so its trajectories are the curves of constant $E$:

Points where the flow stops are of special interest. A **fixed point** $\vec x_*$ satisfies (p. 146)

<Formula label="Fixed point" tex="\vec f(\vec x_*) = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 146, eq. (7.14)" />

and a system placed there stays there forever. The behaviour of nearby trajectories sorts fixed points into three kinds (pp. 146–147): **stable**, if nearby trajectories approach it; **unstable**, if they move away; and **saddle points**, if they approach along some directions and leave along others. For the undamped pendulum the fixed points are $\omega = 0$, $\theta = n\pi$: the hanging positions $\theta = 2n\pi$ are stable, the upright ones $\theta = (2n+1)\pi$ are saddles (p. 147). The trajectories that seem to meet at the top do not cross: a pendulum with exactly $E = 1$ creeps towards the upright position and stops there, and any small perturbation decides which way it falls (p. 147; the notes' "$E = 0$" measures energy from the top, where our $E$ is 1).

With dissipation the picture changes qualitatively. All trajectories near a stable fixed point now converge onto it as energy is lost, so the fixed point is an **attractor**, and the set of starting points that flow to it is its **basin of attraction** (p. 147). For the damped pendulum ($q$ finite, $a = 0$) the trajectories spiral into the hanging positions, and the basins of the different hanging positions $2n\pi$ are diagonal strips across the $\theta$–$\omega$ plane: a pendulum started with enough speed goes over the top before it is caught by one of them (p. 147, Fig. 7.4).

**Two necessary conditions for chaos** (pp. 147–148):

1. The equations must be **nonlinear**. A linear system is solved completely by exponentials and sinusoids, and nothing of that kind is chaotic.
2. There must be **at least three variables**, $n \ge 3$. The notes defer the reason to Section 7.4; in short, trajectories that cannot cross have too little room in a plane.

The undriven pendulum fails the second condition. With a drive, the phase $\varphi$ is the third variable, and for some values of $(a, q, \omega_D)$ the motion is chaotic (p. 148).

## The linear driven oscillator and limit cycles

For comparison, replace $\sin\theta$ by $\theta$ in (7.9). The middle equation becomes (notes p. 148)

<Formula label="Linearized driven oscillator" tex="\dot\omega = -\frac{1}{q}\,\omega - \theta + a\cos\varphi" cite="reading1" at="p. 148, eq. (7.15)" />

the forced damped oscillator of [8.03](/courses/8-03/driven-oscillators-resonance), solved exactly. It is underdamped for $q > \frac12$, critically damped for $q = \frac12$ and overdamped for $q < \frac12$ (p. 148). In the underdamped case (p. 148)

<Formula label="Underdamped driven solution" tex="\theta(t) = Be^{-t/(2q)}\cos\!\left(t\sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{4q^2}} + \varphi_0\right) + \frac{a\cos(\omega_D t - \psi)}{\omega_D\sqrt{q^{-2} + (\omega_D^{-1} - \omega_D)^2}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 148, eq. (7.16)" />

with $\tan\psi = \omega_D/(q - q\omega_D^2)$, and $B$, $\varphi_0$ fixed by the initial conditions. (The notes call the phase lag $\delta$; we write $\psi$ to keep $\delta$ for the separation of trajectories.) The first term is a transient that decays exponentially; the second is the steady forced motion, whose amplitude has the familiar resonance near $\omega_D = 1$ (p. 148; the notes say "at" $\omega_D = 1$, which holds exactly only for very weak damping, large $q$).

Projected onto the $\theta$–$\omega$ plane, every trajectory approaches the same ellipse, the steady motion. The projection may cross itself, but in the full space $(\theta, \omega, \varphi)$ it does not, since $\varphi = \omega_D t$ keeps increasing; if $\varphi$ is taken modulo $2\pi$, the motion closes into an orbit (p. 148). Stewart notes that writing the drive as $\cos\varphi$, a nonlinear function of the variable $\varphi$, is what makes this closed orbit an isolated end point of the motion. Such an attracting closed orbit, as opposed to an attracting point, is called a **limit cycle** (p. 149).

## Picturing chaotic trajectories

The nonlinear pendulum (7.9) has to be solved by computer. That is no obstacle to studying chaos: the exponential separation caused by a chosen difference in initial conditions shows up long before rounding errors at machine precision do (notes p. 149). Stewart describes three ways to look at the results.

**Projections** (p. 149). Project the trajectory onto two of its variables, for the pendulum the $\theta$–$\omega$ plane (dropping $\varphi$). For chaotic motion the projection is a tangle that fills a large part of the plane.

**Poincaré sections** (pp. 149–150). Sample the state only once per period of the drive, at $\varphi = 2\pi n$, and plot the points $(\theta_n, \omega_n)$. This is what a stroboscope flashing at the drive frequency would show. The transients are allowed to die out first. Stewart's examples take $\omega_D = \frac23$ and $q = 2$ and wait 30 drive cycles; as $a$ grows, the long-term motion goes from singly periodic ($a = 0.9$, one point in the section) to doubly periodic ($a = 1.07$, two points), to chaotic ($a = 1.19$, a scattered but structured set) and back to periodic within surrounding chaos ($a = 1.35$) (p. 150). (The notes' Figures 7.6 and 7.7 were made with a Mathematica demonstration by Nasser Abbasi; they are not reproduced here.)

**Bifurcation diagrams** (p. 152). Plot one variable from the Poincaré sections, say $\omega$, against a parameter of the system, say $a$, with the others fixed. Each value of $a$ then shows a vertical set of points: one for period-one motion, two after a **period doubling**, a smear where the motion is chaotic. For $q = 2$, $\omega_D = \frac23$ the notes read off the following:

| $a$ | Long-term behaviour |
|---|---|
| 1.0 | a single value of $\omega$ |
| 1.07 | two values of $\omega$ (period doubling) |
| 1.15 to 1.28 | mostly chaos, with some periodic windows |
| 1.35 | periodic again |

Changing $q$ or $\omega_D$ moves the periodic and chaotic windows but leaves the overall look of the diagram the same, and the sampled $\omega$ can just as well be plotted against $\omega_D$ instead of $a$ (p. 152). The change from one point to two is a first example of a **bifurcation**, a qualitative change in the long-term behaviour as a parameter is varied; the next section studies the simplest ones.

## Summary

- **Chaos**: aperiodic, mixing long-term behaviour of a deterministic system with sensitive dependence on initial conditions, $|\delta(t)| \approx |\delta_0|e^{\lambda t}$; prediction fails after a time that grows only like $\ln(1/\delta_0)$.
- Any dynamical system can be written as $\dot{\vec x} = \vec f(\vec x)$; existence and uniqueness make it deterministic and forbid trajectories from crossing.
- Phase-space volumes change at the rate $\int d\mathcal V\,\nabla\cdot\vec f$: conservative if $\nabla\cdot\vec f = 0$, dissipative if negative. The damped driven pendulum, $\dot\theta = \omega$, $\dot\omega = -\omega/q - \sin\theta + a\cos\varphi$, $\dot\varphi = \omega_D$, has $\nabla\cdot\vec f = -1/q$.
- Fixed points ($\vec f = 0$) are stable, unstable or saddles; with dissipation stable ones are attractors with basins of attraction. An attracting closed orbit is a limit cycle, as in the linear driven oscillator.
- Chaos needs nonlinearity and at least three variables.
- Chaotic motion is pictured by projections, Poincaré sections (one point per drive period) and bifurcation diagrams; for the pendulum with $q = 2$, $\omega_D = \frac23$, increasing $a$ gives period doubling near 1.07 and chaos between about 1.15 and 1.28.
