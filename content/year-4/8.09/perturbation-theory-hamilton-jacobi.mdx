Chapter 5 of Stewart's notes is about what to do when the equations of motion cannot be solved exactly, but the problem is close to one that can. The method, perturbation theory, will be familiar from quantum mechanics, where 8.06 develops it at length ([nondegenerate perturbation theory](/courses/8-06/nondegenerate-perturbation-theory)); the classical version is in some ways simpler, because it works directly with trajectories. This page covers the chapter's introduction and Section 5.1 (notes pp. 92–95). The method leans on Chapter 4 of the notes, the Hamilton-Jacobi equation and its principal function $S$ (Section 4.4 there), which is briefly recalled below where it is needed. (The source is the course's typed lecture notes, without videos; this page follows them in order, in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Close to a solvable problem

Suppose the Hamiltonian we care about, $H(q, p, t)$, differs only a little from one, $H_0(q, p, t)$, whose motion we can find exactly. Write the difference as a separate term (p. 92):

<Formula label="Splitting the Hamiltonian" tex="H(q, p, t) = H_0(q, p, t) + \Delta H(q, p, t)" cite="reading1" at="Chapter 5 introduction, eq. (5.1), p. 92" />

where $\Delta H$ is small. To keep track of how small, imagine it carries a factor $\epsilon$, and expand each coordinate and momentum in powers of $\epsilon$ (p. 92):

<Formula label="Expansion in the small parameter" tex="z(t) = z_0(t) + \epsilon\, z_1(t) + \epsilon^2 z_2(t) + \ldots, \qquad z \in \{q, p\}" cite="reading1" at="Chapter 5 introduction, eq. (5.2), p. 92" />

The goal is a sequence of better and better approximations, the partial sums up to order $k$ (p. 92):

<Formula label="Approximation of order k" tex="z^{(k)}(t) = \sum_{j=0}^{k} \epsilon^j z_j(t)" cite="reading1" at="Chapter 5 introduction, eq. (5.3), p. 92" />

Any formulation of mechanics can be expanded like this: the Euler-Lagrange equations, Hamilton's equations, Poisson brackets or the Hamilton-Jacobi equation. The notes choose the last one for its practical advantages, but start with Hamilton's equations to show the idea in its plainest form.

## A first example with Hamilton's equations

Take a free particle, $H_0 = p^2/2m$, and as the perturbation a harmonic potential, $\Delta H = \tfrac12 m\omega^2x^2$. The full problem is the harmonic oscillator, whose answer we know, so we can watch the perturbative answer being built up. Hamilton's equations are, exactly (p. 92),

<Formula label="Hamilton's equations of the example" tex="\dot x = \frac{p}{m}, \qquad \dot p = -m\omega^2 x" cite="reading1" at="Chapter 5 introduction, eq. (5.4), p. 92" />

The bookkeeping rule: $\omega^2$ counts as one power of $\epsilon$, and on each side of an equation the powers must match. (Twice on these pages the notes write a Latin w for the omega.) At zeroth order there is no force, so $p_0$ is a constant, set by the initial condition, and $x_0 = p_0t/m$ if the particle starts at $x = 0$ (p. 93). At first order the right-hand side of $\dot p$ uses the zeroth-order position (p. 93):

<Formula label="First-order momentum" tex="\dot p^{(1)} = -m\omega^2 x^{(0)} = -\omega^2 t\, p_0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad p^{(1)}(t) = p_0 - \tfrac12\, p_0\,\omega^2 t^2" cite="reading1" at="Chapter 5 introduction, eq. (5.5), p. 93" />

and the other equation then gives the first-order position (p. 93):

<Formula label="First-order position" tex="\dot x^{(1)} = \frac{p^{(1)}}{m} = \frac{p_0}{m} - \frac{p_0\,\omega^2 t^2}{2m} \quad\Rightarrow\quad x^{(1)}(t) = \frac{p_0}{m}\,t - \frac{p_0\,\omega^2 t^3}{6m}" cite="reading1" at="Chapter 5 introduction, eq. (5.6), p. 93" />

These are exactly the first two terms of the Taylor series of the true solution (p. 93):

<Formula label="Exact solution" tex="p(t) = p_0 \cos(\omega t), \qquad x(t) = \frac{p_0}{m\omega}\sin(\omega t)" cite="reading1" at="Chapter 5 introduction, eq. (5.7), p. 93" />

The example also shows the main weakness of a straightforward expansion. Each order adds a higher power of $\omega t$, so the approximation is good only while $\omega t$ is small, however small $\omega^2$ itself is. The graph makes this concrete; Section 5.2 shows how averaging over a period separates the parts that stay small from the secular parts that grow, and reads the latter as a shift of the frequency.

## Using the Hamilton-Jacobi solution of H₀

From here on the notes use Hamilton-Jacobi methods (p. 93). Suppose we have solved the unperturbed problem completely: Hamilton's principal function $S(q, \alpha, t)$ satisfies the Hamilton-Jacobi equation of $H_0$ (p. 93),

<Formula label="Hamilton-Jacobi equation of the unperturbed problem" tex="H_0\Big(q, \frac{\partial S}{\partial q}, t\Big) + \frac{\partial S}{\partial t} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.1, eq. (5.8), p. 93" />

and, used as a generating function, it takes the old variables $(q, p)$ to new ones $(\alpha, \beta)$ that do not move at all under $H_0$. The key observation is that a canonical transformation is just a change of variables on phase space: it does not care which Hamiltonian is then used. The variables $(\alpha, \beta)$ are particularly simple for $H_0$, but they are perfectly good variables for $H$ too. Under the full Hamiltonian they are simply no longer constant (pp. 93–94):

<Formula label="New variables from the unperturbed problem" tex="P_i = \alpha_i = \alpha_i(p, q), \qquad Q_i = \beta_i = \beta_i(p, q)" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.1, eq. (5.9), p. 93" />

The new Hamiltonian is the old one plus the time derivative of the generating function, and two of its three pieces cancel by eq. (5.8) (p. 94):

<Formula label="New Hamiltonian" tex="K = H_0 + \Delta H + \frac{\partial S}{\partial t} = \Delta H = \Delta H(\alpha, \beta, t)" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.1, eq. (5.10), p. 94" />

Hamilton's equations in the new variables, $\dot Q_i = \partial K/\partial P_i$ and $\dot P_i = -\partial K/\partial Q_i$, are then exact equations of motion in which only the perturbation appears (p. 94):

<Formula label="Exact equations for the slowly changing constants" tex="\dot\alpha_i = -\frac{\partial \Delta H}{\partial \beta_i}, \qquad \dot\beta_i = \frac{\partial \Delta H}{\partial \alpha_i}" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.1, eq. (5.11), p. 94" />

(A remark of this page, not of the notes:) this is the classical cousin of the [interaction picture](/courses/8-06/interaction-picture-time-dependent-perturbations) of quantum mechanics, and of the "variation of constants" of celestial mechanics: the quantities that would be constants of the motion drift slowly, and only $\Delta H$ makes them drift.

## Solving order by order

Since $\Delta H$ is small, the right-hand sides of eq. (5.11) are small, and they need the variables only to one order lower than the left-hand sides. At first order, put the constant zeroth-order values $\alpha^{(0)}$, $\beta^{(0)}$ on the right, with a subscript 0 meaning "evaluated there" (p. 94):

<Formula label="First order" tex="\dot\alpha_i^{(1)} = -\Big(\frac{\partial \Delta H}{\partial \beta_i}\Big)_{0}, \qquad \dot\beta_i^{(1)} = \Big(\frac{\partial \Delta H}{\partial \alpha_i}\Big)_{0}" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.1, eq. (5.12), p. 94" />

Integrating in time gives $\alpha^{(1)}(t)$ and $\beta^{(1)}(t)$; these go on the right to give the second order, and so on. At order $n$ (p. 94):

<Formula label="Order n" tex="\dot\alpha_i^{(n)} = -\Big(\frac{\partial \Delta H}{\partial \beta_i}\Big)_{n-1}, \qquad \dot\beta_i^{(n)} = \Big(\frac{\partial \Delta H}{\partial \alpha_i}\Big)_{n-1}" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.1, eq. (5.13), p. 94" />

where the subscript $n - 1$ means evaluated at $\alpha^{(n-1)}$, $\beta^{(n-1)}$. Each step is only a time integral of a known function. At any stage the old variables can be recovered from the inverse transformation $q(\alpha, \beta, t)$, $p(\alpha, \beta, t)$.

## The example again, the Hamilton-Jacobi way

Return to $H_0 = p^2/2m$ and $\Delta H = \tfrac12 m\omega^2x^2$ (pp. 94–95). The Hamilton-Jacobi equation of the free particle is $\frac{1}{2m}(\partial S/\partial x)^2 + \partial S/\partial t = 0$, and since $x$ is cyclic it is solved by $S = \alpha x - \alpha^2t/(2m)$. The new variables are (p. 94)

<Formula label="Free-particle variables" tex="P = \alpha, \qquad Q = \beta = \frac{\partial S}{\partial \alpha} = x - \frac{\alpha}{m}\,t" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.1, eq. (5.14), p. 94" />

so $\alpha$ is the momentum and $\beta$ the position the particle would have had at $t = 0$, and inversely $x = \alpha t/m + \beta$, $p = \partial S/\partial x = \alpha$. Take $\alpha^{(0)} = \alpha_0$ and $\beta^{(0)} = 0$. In the new variables the perturbation is $\Delta H = \tfrac12 m\omega^2(\alpha t/m + \beta)^2$, which depends on time explicitly, and the exact equations are (p. 95)

<Formula label="Equations for the free-particle constants" tex="\dot\alpha = -\frac{\partial \Delta H}{\partial \beta} = -m\omega^2\Big(\frac{\alpha}{m}t + \beta\Big), \qquad \dot\beta = \frac{\partial \Delta H}{\partial \alpha} = \omega^2 t\,\Big(\frac{\alpha}{m}t + \beta\Big)" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.1, eq. (5.15), p. 95" />

With the zeroth-order values on the right (p. 95):

<Formula label="First-order constants" tex="\alpha^{(1)}(t) = \alpha_0 - \tfrac12\,\omega^2 \alpha_0 t^2, \qquad \beta^{(1)}(t) = \frac{\omega^2 \alpha_0\, t^3}{3m}" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.1, eq. (5.16), p. 95" />

Transforming back, $p^{(1)} = \alpha^{(1)}$ is eq. (5.5) again (eq. 5.17), and for the position the two pieces combine (p. 95):

<Formula label="First-order position from the Hamilton-Jacobi route" tex="x^{(1)}(t) = \frac{\alpha^{(1)}(t)}{m}\,t + \beta^{(1)}(t) = \frac{\alpha_0}{m}\,t - \frac{\omega^2\alpha_0}{m}\,\frac{t^3}{2} + \frac{\omega^2\alpha_0}{m}\,\frac{t^3}{3} = \frac{\alpha_0}{m}\,t - \frac{\omega^2\alpha_0}{m}\,\frac{t^3}{3!}" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.1, eq. (5.18), p. 95" />

the same as from Hamilton's equations, as it must be. Notice that the drift of $\beta$ matters: the change of momentum alone would have given $-t^3/2$ instead of $-t^3/6$.

## A worked example

<WorkedExample title="A free particle pushed by a constant force">
<Problem>
A particle of mass $m = 2.0$ kg moves freely with momentum $p_0 = 3.0$ kg m/s, starting at $x = 0$, when a constant force $F = 0.50$ N is switched on at $t = 0$. Treat $\Delta H = -Fx$ as a perturbation of $H_0 = p^2/2m$ with the Hamilton-Jacobi variables above. Find $\alpha(t)$, $\beta(t)$ and $x(t)$ to first order, show that second order adds nothing, and evaluate at $t = 4.0$ s.
</Problem>
<Step title="The perturbation in the new variables">
With $x = \alpha t/m + \beta$, the perturbation is $\Delta H = -F(\alpha t/m + \beta)$. Its derivatives are $\partial\Delta H/\partial\beta = -F$ and $\partial\Delta H/\partial\alpha = -Ft/m$: neither depends on $\alpha$ or $\beta$.
</Step>
<Step title="First order">
$\dot\alpha^{(1)} = F$, so $\alpha^{(1)} = \alpha_0 + Ft$: the momentum grows at the rate $F$, Newton's second law. $\dot\beta^{(1)} = -Ft/m$, so $\beta^{(1)} = -Ft^2/(2m)$. Then $x^{(1)} = \alpha^{(1)}t/m + \beta^{(1)} = \alpha_0 t/m + Ft^2/m - Ft^2/(2m) = \alpha_0 t/m + Ft^2/(2m)$, the familiar uniformly accelerated motion.
</Step>
<Step title="Higher orders">
Because the right-hand sides do not depend on $\alpha$ and $\beta$, putting the first-order solution on the right gives the same equations again: every higher correction vanishes. A perturbation linear in $x$ is solved exactly at first order. For the harmonic $\Delta H$ of the notes, by contrast, the right-hand sides do depend on $\alpha$ and $\beta$, and the series continues forever, building up the sine and cosine.
</Step>
<Step title="Numbers">
At $t = 4.0$ s: $p = \alpha = 3.0 + 0.50 \times 4.0 = 5.0$ kg m/s, $\beta = -0.50 \times 16/(2 \times 2.0) = -2.0$ m, and $x = 3.0 \times 4.0/2.0 + 0.50 \times 16/(2 \times 2.0) = 6.0 + 2.0 = 8.0$ m. The "starting position" $\beta$ has drifted to $-2.0$ m: a free particle with the final momentum 5.0 kg m/s would have to have started 2.0 m behind the origin to be at 8.0 m now ($5.0 \times 4.0/2.0 - 2.0 = 8.0$).
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Perturbation theory treats $H = H_0 + \Delta H$ with $H_0$ solvable and $\Delta H$ small, expanding $q$ and $p$ in powers of a small parameter and solving order by order, always with lower-order results on the right-hand side.
- For a free particle perturbed by $\tfrac12 m\omega^2x^2$, Hamilton's equations give $p \approx p_0(1 - \omega^2t^2/2)$ and $x \approx (p_0/m)(t - \omega^2t^3/6)$: the start of the exact $p_0\cos\omega t$ and $(p_0/m\omega)\sin\omega t$, accurate only while $\omega t$ is small.
- In the Hamilton-Jacobi method, the unperturbed principal function $S(q, \alpha, t)$ defines variables $(\alpha, \beta)$ that are constant under $H_0$. Under $H$ the new Hamiltonian is $K = \Delta H(\alpha, \beta, t)$, and $\dot\alpha_i = -\partial\Delta H/\partial\beta_i$, $\dot\beta_i = \partial\Delta H/\partial\alpha_i$ exactly.
- At order $n$ the right-hand sides are evaluated on the $(n-1)$-th order solution; each step is a time integral. The same example reproduces $x \approx \alpha_0t/m - \omega^2\alpha_0t^3/(6m)$.
- A perturbation linear in the coordinate (a constant force) is solved exactly at first order.
