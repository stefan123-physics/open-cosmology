Section 6.2 turns the fluid fields of [Section 6.1](/courses/8-09/continuum-limit-fluid-variables) into equations of motion. This first page covers the continuity equation and the vorticity (Section 6.2.1, pp. 109–112); the [second page](/courses/8-09/euler-equation-fluid-conservation) covers the Euler equation and the conservation laws. The notes' figures are not reproduced, and the worked examples are our own.

## How a fluid element changes its volume

The tool is the **divergence theorem** of vector calculus, true for any vector field (p. 109). For a volume $V$ with closed boundary surface $\partial V$, outward unit normal $\hat n$ and $d\vec S = \hat n\,dS$:

<Formula label="The divergence theorem for the velocity" tex="\int_V dV\,\nabla\cdot\vec v = \oint_{\partial V} dS\,\hat n\cdot\vec v = \oint_{\partial V} d\vec S\cdot\vec v" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.1, eq. (6.20), p. 109" />

Now let the volume move with the fluid. A patch $dS$ of its surface moves outward at speed $\hat n\cdot\vec v$, so in a time $dt$ it sweeps out the extra volume $\hat n\cdot\vec v\,dS\,dt$. Adding these over the whole surface gives the rate at which the volume grows (p. 110):

<Formula label="Rate of change of a moving volume" tex="\frac{dV}{dt} = \oint_{\partial V}\vec v\cdot d\vec S = \int_V\nabla\cdot\vec v\,dV" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.1, eq. (6.21), p. 110" />

For a tiny element $\delta V$, small enough that $\nabla\cdot\vec v$ is the same throughout, the integral is just $\nabla\cdot\vec v$ times the volume (p. 110):

<Formula label="Volume of a small fluid element" tex="\frac{d\,\delta V}{dt} = \delta V\,\nabla\cdot\vec v" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.1, eq. (6.22), p. 110" />

So the divergence of the velocity is the fractional rate at which fluid elements swell. Where $\nabla\cdot\vec v = 0$ everywhere, no element changes its volume, and the fluid is called **incompressible** (p. 110).

## The continuity equation

An element may change its volume, but never its mass (p. 110):

<Formula label="The mass of an element is fixed" tex="\frac{d\,\delta m}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\rho\,\delta V\right) = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.1, eq. (6.23), p. 110" />

Expanding with the product rule and using (6.22) (p. 110):

<Formula label="Expanding the mass condition" tex="0 = \delta V\,\frac{d\rho}{dt} + \rho\,\frac{d\,\delta V}{dt} = \delta V\left(\frac{d\rho}{dt} + \rho\,\nabla\cdot\vec v\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.1, eq. (6.24), p. 110" />

so $d\rho/dt + \rho\,\nabla\cdot\vec v = 0$ (eq. 6.25): the density following an element falls exactly as fast as its volume grows. Writing the total derivative as $\partial/\partial t + \vec v\cdot\nabla$ gives $\partial\rho/\partial t + \vec v\cdot\nabla\rho + \rho\,\nabla\cdot\vec v = 0$ (eq. 6.26), and the last two terms are one divergence (p. 110):

<Formula label="The continuity equation" tex="\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\left(\rho\vec v\right) = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.1, eq. (6.27), p. 110" />

This is the **continuity equation**, a partial differential equation for the fluid fields. The vector $\rho\vec v$ is the **mass flux density**, how much mass crosses unit area per unit time, and the equation says that no mass is ever created or lost: any change of density at a point has to be paid for by mass carried in or out (p. 110). The integral form makes this explicit. Integrate over a fixed volume and use the divergence theorem on $\rho\vec v$ (p. 111):

<Formula label="Continuity in integral form" tex="\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_V dV\,\rho = -\oint_{\partial V} dS\,\hat n\cdot\left(\rho\vec v\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.1, eq. (6.28), p. 111" />

The left side is the gain of mass inside $V$; the surface integral on the right is the mass flowing out, and with the minus sign the right side counts what enters (p. 111).

<WorkedExample title="A uniformly expanding gas">
<Problem>
A cloud of gas, uniform in density, expands with the velocity field $\vec v = \alpha\vec r$ about its centre, with $\alpha = 0.2$ s⁻¹ held constant. Find $\nabla\cdot\vec v$, how the density changes with time, and the density after 2 s as a fraction of the starting value.
</Problem>
<Step title="The divergence">
$\nabla\cdot\vec v = \alpha\left(\partial x/\partial x + \partial y/\partial y + \partial z/\partial z\right) = 3\alpha = 0.6$ s⁻¹, the same everywhere. Every element swells at the fractional rate 0.6 per second.
</Step>
<Step title="The density">
The density stays uniform, so $\nabla\rho = 0$ and the continuity equation reduces to $d\rho/dt = -\rho\,\nabla\cdot\vec v = -3\alpha\rho$, with solution $\rho(t) = \rho_0e^{-3\alpha t}$. After 2 s, $\rho/\rho_0 = e^{-1.2} \approx 0.30$. Equivalently, every length grows as $e^{\alpha t}$ and every volume as $e^{3\alpha t}$, while the mass stays the same.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The same equation for the universe">
A uniform expansion $\vec v = \alpha\vec r$ is the form of **Hubble's law**, $\vec v = H\vec r$. In the [expanding sphere](/courses/8-286/expanding-sphere) of 8.286 the mass inside a comoving shell is fixed while the volume grows as $a^3$, so the matter density falls as $1/a^3$; that is what $d\rho/dt = -\rho\,\nabla\cdot\vec v = -3H\rho$ gives when $H = \dot a/a$ changes with time. In 8.902 the continuity equation, with the Euler and Poisson equations, is the starting point of [linear perturbation theory](/courses/8-902/linear-perturbations) for the growth of structure.
</Callout>

## Vorticity

The divergence of $\vec v$ has a clear meaning; so does its curl (p. 111). The **vorticity** is

<Formula label="Vorticity" tex="\vec\Omega = \nabla\times\vec v" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.1, unnumbered, p. 111" />

If the velocity is uniform, or points in one direction and changes only along that direction, $\vec v = v_x(x)\,\hat x$, the vorticity is zero (p. 111). To see what it measures, use **Stokes' theorem** for an open surface $S$ with boundary curve $\partial S$ (p. 111):

<Formula label="Stokes' theorem for the velocity" tex="\int_S\left(\nabla\times\vec v\right)\cdot\hat n\,dS = \oint_{\partial S}\vec v\cdot d\vec l" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.1, eq. (6.29), p. 111" />

The right side is the **circulation** of the flow round the curve. In a rotating fluid every piece of a circle about the axis has $\vec v$ along $d\vec l$, the contributions all add, and $\vec\Omega \neq 0$: the vorticity measures how fast the fluid turns (p. 111). A flow in a single direction can also have vorticity, if its speed changes across the flow: round a small circle, the fast side contributes more than the slow side takes away (p. 111). A flow with $\nabla\times\vec v = 0$ everywhere is called **irrotational** (p. 111).

A rotating frame makes the meaning exact (pp. 111–112). In a frame turning with constant angular velocity $\vec\omega$, the velocity is $\vec v = \vec v' + \vec\omega\times\vec r$, with $\vec v'$ measured in the rotating frame. Since $\nabla\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r) = \vec\omega(\nabla\cdot\vec r) - (\vec\omega\cdot\nabla)\vec r = 3\vec\omega - \vec\omega$ (p. 112),

<Formula label="Vorticity in a rotating frame" tex="\nabla\times\vec v = \nabla\times\vec v' + \nabla\times\left(\vec\omega\times\vec r\right) = \nabla\times\vec v' + 2\vec\omega" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.2.1, unnumbered, p. 112" />

We are free to choose $\vec\omega$. Taking $\vec\omega = \tfrac12\nabla\times\vec v$ at a point makes $\nabla\times\vec v' = 0$ there: in that frame the fluid near the point does not rotate. Seen from the original frame, the fluid near the point therefore turns with angular velocity $\vec\Omega/2$, and the argument works at every point (p. 112).

<WorkedExample title="Circulation in a turning tank">
<Problem>
Water in a tank turns rigidly about the vertical axis at 3 rad/s. Find its vorticity, and check Stokes' theorem on a horizontal circle of radius 0.1 m centred on the axis.
</Problem>
<Step title="The vorticity">
Rigid rotation is $\vec v = \vec\omega\times\vec r$ with $\omega = 3$ rad/s, so by the identity above (with $\vec v' = 0$) $\vec\Omega = 2\vec\omega$: 6 rad/s, pointing along the axis. Its half, 3 rad/s, is the rotation rate, as it should be.
</Step>
<Step title="Both sides of Stokes' theorem">
On the circle the speed is $\omega r = 0.3$ m/s along the path, so the circulation is $2\pi r \times 0.3 = 2\pi \times 0.1 \times 0.3 \approx 0.188$ m²/s. The flux of $\vec\Omega$ through the disc is $\Omega\,\pi r^2 = 6 \times \pi \times 0.01 \approx 0.188$ m²/s. The two agree.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- By the divergence theorem, a moving volume grows at $dV/dt = \oint\vec v\cdot d\vec S = \int\nabla\cdot\vec v\,dV$; a small element obeys $d\,\delta V/dt = \delta V\,\nabla\cdot\vec v$. If $\nabla\cdot\vec v = 0$ everywhere, the flow is **incompressible**.
- Each element keeps its mass, $d(\rho\,\delta V)/dt = 0$, so $d\rho/dt + \rho\nabla\cdot\vec v = 0$, the **continuity equation** $\partial\rho/\partial t + \nabla\cdot(\rho\vec v) = 0$. In integral form the mass in a region changes only by the flux $\rho\vec v$ through its surface.
- The **vorticity** $\vec\Omega = \nabla\times\vec v$ is, by Stokes' theorem, the circulation per unit area; it is non-zero in rotating fluids and in shear flows. A flow with $\vec\Omega = 0$ is **irrotational**.
- In a frame rotating at $\vec\omega$, $\nabla\times\vec v = \nabla\times\vec v' + 2\vec\omega$, so the fluid near each point turns with angular velocity $\vec\Omega/2$.
