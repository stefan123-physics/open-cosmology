Section 4.3 ended with a suggestion: the motion itself is a canonical transformation, from present to initial values, so look directly for a transformation to variables that never change. This page follows Section 4.4 of the notes (pp. 73–79), which turns that idea into an equation, and tests it on the harmonic oscillator.

## New variables that never move

Ask for new canonical variables that are all constant (notes p. 73):

<Formula label="All new variables constant" tex="\dot Q_i = \dot P_i = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad (Q, P) \text{ are all constants}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.101), p. 73" />

Hamilton's equations in the new variables, $\dot Q_i = \partial K/\partial P_i$ and $\dot P_i = -\partial K/\partial Q_i$, then demand that $K$ not depend on $Q$ or $P$ (eq. (4.102)). A constant $K$ would do, but $K = 0$ is the simplest choice. With a type-2 generating function $F_2(q, P, t)$, Section 4.1 gives $K = H + \partial F_2/\partial t$, so the requirement is

<Formula label="Zero new Hamiltonian" tex="K = H(q, p, t) + \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial t} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.103), p. 73" />

## The Hamilton-Jacobi equation

The old momenta are not independent variables here: for a type-2 function, $p_i = \partial F_2/\partial q_i$. Substituting turns the condition into an equation for $F_2$ alone (notes p. 73):

<Formula label="Time-dependent Hamilton-Jacobi equation" tex="H\left(q_1, \ldots, q_n, \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial q_1}, \ldots, \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial q_n}, t\right) + \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial t} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.104), p. 73" />

This is the time-dependent **Hamilton-Jacobi equation** (the H-J equation): a first-order partial differential equation for $F_2$ in the $n + 1$ variables $q_1, \ldots, q_n, t$. A complete solution contains $n + 1$ independent constants of integration. One of them only adds a constant to $F_2$, which changes nothing, so drop it. Call the solution, with its $n$ remaining constants $\alpha_i$,

<Formula label="Hamilton's principal function" tex="F_2 \equiv S = S(q_1, \ldots, q_n, \alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n, t)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.105), p. 73" />

**Hamilton's principal function**, $S$.

## From S to the motion

The constants $\alpha_i$ are the natural choice for the new momenta, $P_i = \alpha_i$, since new momenta are supposed to be constant anyway. That makes $S$ a function of $(q, P, t)$, as a type-2 generating function should be. Its other derivative then gives the new coordinates, which the notes call $\beta_i$ to stress that they too are constants:

<Formula label="The new constant variables" tex="P_i \equiv \alpha_i \quad\text{and}\quad Q_i \equiv \beta_i = \frac{\partial S(q, \alpha, t)}{\partial\alpha_i}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.106), p. 73" />

Solving these equations gives the motion (notes pp. 73–74): the $n$ equations $\beta_i = \partial S/\partial\alpha_i$ can be inverted for $q_i(\alpha, \beta, t)$, and substituting into $p_i = \partial S/\partial q_i$ gives $p_i(\alpha, \beta, t)$:

<Formula label="Solution for coordinates and momenta" tex="\beta_i = \frac{\partial S}{\partial\alpha_i} \;\Rightarrow\; q_i = q_i(\alpha, \beta, t), \qquad p_i = \frac{\partial S}{\partial q_i} \;\Rightarrow\; p_i = p_i(\alpha, \beta, t)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.107), p. 73" />

(The notes point out that $p_i(q, \alpha, t)$ and $p_i(\alpha, \beta, t)$ are, strictly, different functions; the arguments make clear which is meant.) To solve an initial-value problem, set $t = t_0$ in these results, $q_{i0} = q_i(\alpha, \beta, t_0)$ and $p_{i0} = p_i(\alpha, \beta, t_0)$ (eq. (4.108)): $2n$ equations that trade the $2n$ constants $\alpha_i, \beta_i$ for the initial data. So $S$ generates the canonical transformation to constant variables, and finding it solves the mechanics problem.

## What S is

The notes add three remarks (pp. 74–75).

**The constants are ours to choose.** Any $n$ independent functions $\gamma_i(\alpha)$ of the constants would serve equally well, so when solving the H-J equation we introduce constants in whatever form is convenient.

**$S$ is the action.** Along the motion, its total time derivative is

<Formula label="Total time derivative of S" tex="\dot S = \frac{\partial S}{\partial q_i}\dot q_i + \frac{\partial S}{\partial P_i}\dot P_i + \frac{\partial S}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.109), p. 74" />

and the three pieces are known: $\partial S/\partial q_i = p_i$, $\dot P_i = 0$, and $\partial S/\partial t = -H$ by the H-J equation (eq. (4.110)). Hence

<Formula label="S is the action" tex="\dot S = p_i\dot q_i - H = L \quad\Rightarrow\quad S = \int L\,dt" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.111), p. 74" />

Hamilton's principal function is the action, written as an indefinite time integral of the Lagrangian; that is why the two share the letter $S$.

**The H-J equation is the classical limit of quantum mechanics.** Start from the Schrödinger equation for a particle in a potential,

<Formula label="Schrödinger equation" tex="i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = H\psi = \left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial q^2} + V(q)\right)\psi" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.112), p. 74" />

and write the wavefunction as $\psi = \exp(iS/\hbar)$. This is only a change of variable, with $S(q, t)$ now complex, and loses nothing. Taking the derivatives and dividing out the common exponential gives an equation for $S$ that is equivalent to the Schrödinger equation (notes p. 75):

<Formula label="Schrödinger equation for the phase" tex="-\frac{\partial S}{\partial t} = -\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial q^2} + \frac{1}{2m}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial q}\right)^2 + V(q)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.113), p. 75" />

As $\hbar \to 0$ the only term with $\hbar$ disappears, and what remains is the H-J equation for $H = p^2/2m + V$:

<Formula label="The classical limit" tex="0 = \frac{\partial S}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2m}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial q}\right)^2 + V(q) = \frac{\partial S}{\partial t} + H\left(q, \frac{\partial S}{\partial q}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.114), p. 75" />

This is the leading term of the WKB approximation, the subject of the 8.06 pages on [the semiclassical approximation](/courses/8-06/semiclassical-local-de-broglie-wavelength) and [the WKB approximation](/courses/8-06/wkb-approximation-connection-formula): there the phase of the wavefunction is $\int p\,dx/\hbar$, which is $W/\hbar$ in the notation below.

## Test case: the harmonic oscillator

A new method should first be tried where the answer is known (notes pp. 75–76). Take the oscillator of Section 4.1,

<Formula label="Harmonic oscillator" tex="H = \frac{1}{2m}\left(p^2 + (m\omega q)^2\right) = E" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.115), p. 75" />

with one constant momentum $P = \alpha$ and one constant coordinate $Q = \beta$. The H-J equation reads

<Formula label="H-J equation of the oscillator" tex="\frac{1}{2m}\left(\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial q}\right)^2 + (m\omega q)^2\right) + \frac{\partial S}{\partial t} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.116), p. 75" />

It **separates**: try $S(q, \alpha, t) = W(q, \alpha) + g(\alpha, t)$ (eq. (4.117)). Then the $q$-dependent terms and the $t$-dependent term stand on opposite sides,

<Formula label="Separating q from t" tex="\frac{1}{2m}\left(\left(\frac{\partial W}{\partial q}\right)^2 + (m\omega q)^2\right) = -\frac{\partial g}{\partial t} = \alpha" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.118), p. 75" />

and since one side does not depend on $t$ and the other not on $q$, both equal a constant, which is the natural choice for the new momentum $\alpha$. The time part integrates at once to $g = -\alpha t$ (dropping an additive constant, eq. (4.119)). Since $H = -\partial S/\partial t = -\partial g/\partial t = \alpha$ (eq. (4.120)), the constant is the energy, $\alpha = E$ (eq. (4.121)). The space part, eq. (4.122), is solved for $\partial W/\partial q$ and left as an integral for now:

<Formula label="Principal function of the oscillator" tex="S = -\alpha t \pm \int\sqrt{2m\alpha - (m\omega q)^2}\,dq" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eqs. (4.123)–(4.124), p. 76" />

Now differentiate with respect to $\alpha$ first, and only then integrate:

<Formula label="The new coordinate of the oscillator" tex="\beta = \frac{\partial S}{\partial\alpha} = -t \pm m\int\frac{dq}{\sqrt{2m\alpha - (m\omega q)^2}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad t + \beta = \pm\frac{1}{\omega}\arcsin\left(\sqrt{\frac{m\omega^2}{2\alpha}}\,q\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.125), p. 76" />

Inverting, and then using $p = \partial S/\partial q$:

<Formula label="The oscillator solved by H-J" tex="q = \pm\sqrt{\frac{2\alpha}{m\omega^2}}\,\sin(\omega(t + \beta)), \qquad p = \pm\sqrt{2m\alpha}\,\cos(\omega(t + \beta))" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eqs. (4.126)–(4.127), p. 76" />

the familiar solution, with $\beta$ fixing the phase. Shifting $\omega\beta$ by $\pi$ flips both signs, so the positive roots can be kept, and $(\alpha, \beta)$ can be traded for initial conditions $(q_0, p_0)$ at $t = 0$.

## Time-independent Hamiltonians: the characteristic function W

Separation of variables is the main way to solve the H-J equation. One separation is always available: if $H$ has no explicit time dependence, $\dot H = \partial H/\partial t = 0$ (eq. (4.128)), and time separates as in the example (notes pp. 76–77):

<Formula label="Separating time" tex="S(q, \alpha, t) = W(q, \alpha) - \alpha_1 t" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.129), p. 76" />

with $\alpha_1$ the separation constant. Substituting into the H-J equation leaves

<Formula label="Time-independent Hamilton-Jacobi equation" tex="H\left(q_1, \ldots, q_n, \frac{\partial W}{\partial q_1}, \ldots, \frac{\partial W}{\partial q_n}\right) = \alpha_1" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.130), p. 77" />

the **time-independent Hamilton-Jacobi equation**. Here $\alpha_1$ is the conserved value of $H$, the energy $E$ when $H$ is the energy. The function $W$ is **Hamilton's characteristic function**. Following the time-dependent recipe, the momenta and the constant coordinates are

<Formula label="Variables from W" tex="p_i = \frac{\partial W}{\partial q_i}, \qquad Q_1 = \beta_1 = \frac{\partial S}{\partial\alpha_1} = \frac{\partial W}{\partial\alpha_1} - t, \qquad Q_j = \beta_j = \frac{\partial W}{\partial\alpha_j} \;\; (j = 2, \ldots, n)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.131), p. 77" />

## W as a generating function in its own right

There is a second way to read the same equations, which never mentions $S$ (notes p. 77). Use $W(q, P)$ itself as a type-2 generating function, and ask only that the new **momenta** be constant, with $P_1 = \alpha_1 = H$. The opening of this chapter showed that this weaker demand lets the new coordinates grow linearly in time, which is still simple. Three relations are unchanged, $p_i = \partial W/\partial q_i$, $P_i = \alpha_i$ and $H(q, \partial W/\partial q) = \alpha_1$ (eq. (4.132)), but now $W$ has no explicit time dependence, so $K = H = \alpha_1$, not zero, and every new coordinate is $Q_i = \partial W/\partial\alpha_i$ (eq. (4.133)). Hamilton's equations then give

<Formula label="New coordinates from W" tex="\dot Q_1 = \frac{\partial K}{\partial\alpha_1} = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; Q_1 = t + \beta_1 = \frac{\partial W}{\partial\alpha_1}, \qquad \dot Q_j = \frac{\partial K}{\partial\alpha_j} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; Q_j = \beta_j = \frac{\partial W}{\partial\alpha_j}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eqs. (4.134)–(4.135), p. 77" />

which is eq. (4.131) rearranged. Three more remarks (notes p. 78):

- If the constants are re-chosen as functions $\gamma_i$ of the $\alpha$'s, then $\dot Q_i = \partial K/\partial\gamma_i = v_i$ are constants, and potentially every new coordinate is linear in time, $Q_i = v_it + \beta_i$ (eq. (4.136)).
- $W$ is also an action of a kind:

<Formula label="W as a kind of action" tex="\dot W = \frac{\partial W}{\partial q_i}\dot q_i = p_i\dot q_i \quad\Rightarrow\quad W = \int p_i\dot q_i\,dt = \int p_i\,dq_i" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.137), p. 78" />

- The time-independent H-J equation resembles the time-independent Schrödinger equation: both involve $H$ and a constant energy. But the H-J equation is non-linear in $W$, while the Schrödinger equation is linear in $\psi$.

<WorkedExample title="A ball thrown upwards, by Hamilton-Jacobi">
<Problem>
A ball of mass $m = 0.2$ kg moves vertically, height $q$, in uniform gravity $g = 9.8$ m/s², so $H = p^2/2m + mgq$. Solve the time-independent H-J equation, find $q(t)$ from $\partial W/\partial\alpha = t + \beta$, and fix the constants for a throw from $q = 0$ at $9.8$ m/s upwards at $t = 0$.
</Problem>
<Step title="The characteristic function">
The H-J equation is $\frac{1}{2m}(\partial W/\partial q)^2 + mgq = \alpha$, so $\partial W/\partial q = \sqrt{2m(\alpha - mgq)}$, and integrating, $W = -\dfrac{\left[2m(\alpha - mgq)\right]^{3/2}}{3m^2g}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The motion">
$\partial W/\partial\alpha = -\dfrac{\sqrt{2m(\alpha - mgq)}}{mg} = t + \beta$. Squaring, $m^2g^2(t + \beta)^2 = 2m(\alpha - mgq)$, so $q = \dfrac{\alpha}{mg} - \dfrac{1}{2}g(t + \beta)^2$: a parabola whose top, at height $\alpha/(mg)$, is reached at $t = -\beta$. The square root is never negative, so this branch has $t + \beta \leq 0$: it is the way up; the other sign of $\partial W/\partial q$ gives the way down.
</Step>
<Step title="The constants">
$\alpha$ is the energy: $\tfrac{1}{2}(0.2)(9.8)^2 = 9.604$ J. The top is at $9.604/(0.2 \times 9.8) = 4.9$ m, reached after $9.8/9.8 = 1$ s, so $\beta = -1$ s. Check at $t = 0$: $q = 4.9 - 4.9 \times 1 = 0$, and $p = \partial W/\partial q = \sqrt{2 \times 0.2 \times 9.604} = 1.96$ kg m/s $= mv_0$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Separation of variables

The H-J method pays off when $H$ lets the variables separate (notes pp. 78–79). Suppose the dependence on one pair $(q_3, p_3)$ enters only through a function $f$:

<Formula label="A Hamiltonian in which q_3 separates" tex="H = h_1(q_1, q_2, p_1, p_2) + h_2(q_1, q_2, p_1, p_2)\,f(q_3, p_3) = \alpha_1" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.138), p. 78" />

Then $f(q_3, p_3) = (\alpha_1 - h_1)/h_2$ (eq. (4.139)). The left side involves only $q_3, p_3$, the right side does not, so both are constant; call it $f = \alpha_2$ (eq. (4.140)). Writing $W = W'(q_1, q_2, \alpha) + W_3(q_3, \alpha)$, the H-J equation splits in two:

<Formula label="The two separated equations" tex="f\left(q_3, \frac{\partial W_3}{\partial q_3}\right) = \alpha_2, \qquad h_1\left(q_1, q_2, \frac{\partial W'}{\partial q_1}, \frac{\partial W'}{\partial q_2}\right) + \alpha_2\,h_2\left(q_1, q_2, \frac{\partial W'}{\partial q_1}, \frac{\partial W'}{\partial q_2}\right) = \alpha_1" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eqs. (4.141)–(4.142), p. 78" />

and $q_1, q_2$ may or may not separate further. If every variable separates, $W = \sum_i W_i(q_i, \alpha)$ (eq. (4.143)); in practice one tries this form and sees whether it works.

**Cyclic coordinates always separate.** If $q_1$ is cyclic, its momentum is a constant $\gamma$ and $H$ does not contain $q_1$ (eq. (4.144)). Writing $W = W_1(q_1, \alpha) + W'(q_2, \ldots, q_n, \alpha)$ (eq. (4.145)), the condition $p_1 = \partial W_1/\partial q_1 = \gamma$ integrates at once:

<Formula label="A cyclic coordinate separates" tex="W(q, \alpha) = \gamma q_1 + W'(q_2, \ldots, q_n, \alpha)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eqs. (4.146)–(4.147), p. 79" />

and the same step repeats for every other cyclic coordinate. Finally, separation is a property of the coordinates as much as of the problem. With spherical symmetry, spherical coordinates are the natural choice; Cartesian ones may fail. Section 4.5 separates the Kepler problem in plane polar and then in spherical coordinates.

## Summary

- Asking for a canonical transformation to **constant** variables, generated by $S(q, P, t)$ with $K = 0$, gives the **Hamilton-Jacobi equation** $H(q, \partial S/\partial q, t) + \partial S/\partial t = 0$.
- Its $n$ constants are the new momenta $\alpha_i$; the new coordinates $\beta_i = \partial S/\partial\alpha_i$ are also constant, and solving for $q_i(\alpha, \beta, t)$ gives the motion.
- Along the motion $\dot S = L$: $S$ is the action. With $\psi = e^{iS/\hbar}$ the Schrödinger equation becomes the H-J equation as $\hbar \to 0$.
- For time-independent $H$, $S = W - \alpha_1 t$ and $H(q, \partial W/\partial q) = \alpha_1$; $W = \int p_i\,dq_i$ is Hamilton's characteristic function, and $\partial W/\partial\alpha_1 = t + \beta_1$.
- The method works when the variables separate, $W = \sum_i W_i(q_i, \alpha)$; cyclic coordinates always separate, and the choice of coordinates matters.
