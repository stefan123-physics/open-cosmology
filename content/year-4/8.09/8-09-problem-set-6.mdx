This set practises [the Hamilton-Jacobi equation](/courses/8-09/hamilton-jacobi-equation), its separation in polar coordinates as for [the Kepler problem](/courses/8-09/kepler-problem-hamilton-jacobi), and [action-angle variables](/courses/8-09/action-angle-variables), in one dimension and [in several](/courses/8-09/action-angle-variables-kepler). The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 6, in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

A few things to know about the source. The sheet has printed page numbers; its problems are on "p. 2" and "p. 3". Its reading list (sections of a mechanics textbook) and its course notices are left out. MIT says that all five problems come from, or are related to, problems in that textbook, but the sheet states each of them in full, so they are written here from the sheet alone. The hint of Problem 4(c) pointed to integrals in the textbook; here it points to the same integral in the course notes instead. The sheet has no figures.

Our additions, each marked where it appears: every answer box; the split of Problems 2 and 3, which MIT gives as single questions, into two parts each; and the graph in the solution of Problem 1(b). Some choices are ours as well, each marked "our choice" where it appears: SI units, the charge $e$ and the sign of $B$ in Problem 1; the new momentum $\alpha$ and the additive constant of $S$ in Problem 2; the cell $0 < x < \pi x_0$ in Problem 4; and, in Problem 5, the letter $\nu_i$ for the frequencies.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `sqrt(2*m*E)/F`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. It treats its letters as independent, so each question says which letters to type: `al` for $\alpha$, `x0` for $x_0$, `v0` for $v_0$, `w1` for $w_1$, and so on.

MIT gives the points of each problem (12, 10, 10, 18 and 10, together 60), and the points of the parts of Problems 1, 4 and 5. The split of Problems 2 and 3 between their parts is ours. Several parts ask for more than their boxes check, such as a derivation or a proof, and Problem 5(b) asks you to show something: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The conventions are those of the lessons. An overdot is $d/dt$. Hamilton's principal function is $S(q, \alpha, t)$; for a Hamiltonian without explicit time it is $S = W - \alpha_1 t$, with $W$ Hamilton's characteristic function. The action variable is $J = \oint p\,dq$ over one cycle, and $\nu = \partial H/\partial J$ is the frequency in cycles per unit time. The notes write the angle variable as $\omega$; the sheet writes it as $w$, and so do we, so that $\omega$ stays free for angular frequencies. With either letter the angle variable grows by 1 in each cycle.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A charged particle in a plane" points={12} mit="Problem 1">

A particle of mass $m$ and charge $e$ is held to a plane. A force that is not electromagnetic pulls it towards a fixed point of the plane, with potential

<Formula label="Problem 1: the central potential" tex="V = \frac{1}{2}k r^2, \qquad k > 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 2" />

where $r$ is the distance from that point. A constant magnetic field $\vec B$ stands perpendicular to the plane; it comes from the vector potential

<Formula label="Problem 1: the vector potential" tex="\vec A = \frac{1}{2}\vec B \times \vec r" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, eq. (1), p. 2" />

Our choice: SI units, as in the notes; plane polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ about the fixed point, with $\theta$ counterclockwise seen from the side the unit vector $\hat z$ of the normal points to, and $\vec B = B\hat z$, where $B$ and $e$ may each have either sign.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Set up the Hamilton-Jacobi equation for Hamilton's characteristic function $W$ in plane polar coordinates. Separate it, and reduce it to an integral.

The box is our addition. With $E$ the energy and $\alpha_\theta$ the constant value of $p_\theta$, give $(\partial W_r/\partial r)^2$, where $W = \alpha_\theta\theta + W_r(r)$; type `m`, `E`, `k`, `e`, `B`, `r` and `al` for $\alpha_\theta$.

<Hint>

Start from the Hamiltonian of a charge in the notes. In polar coordinates $\vec A$ has only a $\theta$ component; find it, and write $(\vec p - e\vec A)^2$ with $p_r$ and $p_\theta/r$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\theta$ does not appear in $H$. What does that do to the $\theta$ part of $W$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1a-wr" label="$(\partial W_r/\partial r)^2 =$" expr="2*m*E + e*B*al - al^2/r^2 - (m*k + e^2*B^2/4)*r^2"

  mistakes={[

    { expr: "2*m*E - al^2/r^2 - (m*k + e^2*B^2/4)*r^2", hint: "Expanding (α_θ/r − eBr/2)² gives a cross term, −eBα_θ, which is a constant: it shifts the energy and must not be dropped." },
    { expr: "2*m*E + e*B*al - al^2/r^2 - m*k*r^2", hint: "The square of eA_θ, e²B²r²/4, adds to the spring term: the magnetic field stiffens the radial motion." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write $H$ in polar coordinates. The angle $\theta$ is cyclic, so $W$ is $\alpha_\theta\theta$ plus a function of $r$ alone, and the Hamilton-Jacobi equation becomes an ordinary equation for that function.

<Step title="The Hamiltonian in polar coordinates">

The notes give, for a charge $e$ in a vector potential $\vec A$ and a scalar potential $\phi$,

<Formula label="Hamiltonian of a charge" tex="H = \frac{1}{2m}\left(\vec p - e\vec A\right)^2 + e\phi" cite="reading2" at="Section 1.2, eq. (1.36), p. 10" />

Here there is no $\phi$, and the spring potential $V$ is added. With $\vec B = B\hat z$ and $\vec r = r\hat r$, $\vec A = \frac12 Br\,\hat z \times \hat r = \frac12 Br\,\hat\theta$. The canonical momentum has polar components $p_r$ and $p_\theta/r$, so

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the Hamiltonian in polar coordinates" tex="H = \frac{1}{2m}\left[p_r^2 + \left(\frac{p_\theta}{r} - \frac{eBr}{2}\right)^2\right] + \frac{1}{2}kr^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The Hamilton-Jacobi equation and its separation">

$H$ has no explicit time, so $S = W - Et$ and $W$ obeys the time-independent equation of the notes,

<Formula label="Time-independent Hamilton-Jacobi equation" tex="H\left(q_1, \ldots, q_n, \frac{\partial W}{\partial q_1}, \ldots, \frac{\partial W}{\partial q_n}\right) = \alpha_1" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.130), p. 77" />

with $\alpha_1 = E$. Since $\theta$ is cyclic, $W = \alpha_\theta\theta + W_r(r)$, as the notes do for the Kepler problem, and

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the Hamilton-Jacobi equation" tex="\frac{1}{2m}\left[\left(\frac{\partial W_r}{\partial r}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\alpha_\theta}{r} - \frac{eBr}{2}\right)^2\right] + \frac{1}{2}kr^2 = E" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The integral">

Expanding the square,

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the characteristic function" tex="W = \alpha_\theta\theta + \int\sqrt{2mE + eB\alpha_\theta - \frac{\alpha_\theta^2}{r^2} - \left(mk + \frac{e^2B^2}{4}\right)r^2}\;dr" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Write $\omega_L = eB/(2m)$ and $\Omega^2 = k/m + \omega_L^2$. Then the integrand is $\sqrt{2m(E + \omega_L\alpha_\theta) - \alpha_\theta^2/r^2 - m^2\Omega^2r^2}$: the same integral as for a plane oscillator of angular frequency $\Omega$, with the energy shifted by $\omega_L\alpha_\theta$. The motion follows from $t + \beta_1 = \partial W/\partial E$ and $\beta_2 = \partial W/\partial\alpha_\theta$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $B = 0$ the integrand is that of the notes' central-force problem, $\sqrt{2m(E - V) - \alpha_\theta^2/r^2}$, with $V = \frac12kr^2$. The units: $evB$ is a force, so $eB$ is a mass per unit time and $eBr$ a momentum; then $eB\alpha_\theta$ and $e^2B^2r^2$ are momenta squared, like $2mE$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Solve for the motion when the canonical momentum is $p_\theta = 0$ at time $t = 0$.

The three boxes are our additions. In the first, give the rate $\dot\theta$ at which the particle's direction from the centre turns; type `e`, `B` and `m`. In the second, give the angular frequency of its radial oscillation; type `k`, `m`, `e` and `B`. In the third, choose the shape of the path.

<Hint>

$p_\theta$ is conserved, so $\alpha_\theta = 0$ at all times. Put $\alpha_\theta = 0$ into the derivatives of $W$ only after taking them.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\partial W/\partial E$ gives the integral of an oscillator. For $\partial W/\partial\alpha_\theta$ at $\alpha_\theta = 0$, only the $eB\alpha_\theta$ term under the root contributes.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1b-rate" label="$\dot\theta =$" expr="-e*B/(2*m)"

<Answer id="p1b-freq" label="Angular frequency of the radial oscillation:" expr="sqrt(k/m + e^2*B^2/(4*m^2))"
  vars={{ k: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], e: [0.5, 2], B: [0.5, 2] }}

<Answer id="p1b-path" label="The path in the plane is:" choices={["a circle about the centre, run at the cyclotron frequency", "a fixed ellipse centred on the centre", "a rosette: swings through the centre along a turning line", "a straight segment through the centre, the same at all times"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With $\alpha_\theta = 0$ the radial integral is that of a plain oscillator of angular frequency $\Omega$, and the angle equation says the direction turns at a steady rate. The particle swings back and forth through the centre along a line that itself turns.

<Step title="The two transformation equations">

From $S = W - Et$, the new coordinates are $\beta_i = \partial S/\partial\alpha_i$; as in the notes,

<Formula label="Variables from W" tex="p_i = \frac{\partial W}{\partial q_i}, \qquad Q_1 = \beta_1 = \frac{\partial S}{\partial\alpha_1} = \frac{\partial W}{\partial\alpha_1} - t, \qquad Q_j = \beta_j = \frac{\partial W}{\partial\alpha_j} \;\; (j = 2, \ldots, n)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.131), p. 77" />

With $\alpha_\theta = 0$ after differentiating:

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): the time equation" tex="t + \beta_1 = \frac{\partial W}{\partial E} = \int\frac{m\,dr}{\sqrt{2mE - m^2\Omega^2r^2}} = \frac{1}{\Omega}\arcsin\left(\sqrt{\frac{m\Omega^2}{2E}}\;r\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): the angle equation" tex="\beta_2 = \frac{\partial W}{\partial\alpha_\theta} = \theta + \int\frac{(eB/2)\,dr}{\sqrt{2mE - m^2\Omega^2r^2}} = \theta + \frac{eB}{2m}\,(t + \beta_1)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The motion">

Inverting both,

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): the motion for zero canonical angular momentum" tex="r = \sqrt{\frac{2E}{m\Omega^2}}\,\sin\big(\Omega(t + \beta_1)\big), \qquad \theta = \theta_0 - \frac{eB}{2m}\,t, \qquad \Omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m} + \frac{e^2B^2}{4m^2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

with $\theta_0$ and $\beta_1$ set by the start. Read $r$ as a signed distance along the line at angle $\theta$: when $r$ changes sign, the particle passes through the centre to the other side. So the particle swings through the centre along a line with angular frequency $\Omega$, while the line turns at $\dot\theta = -eB/(2m)$, half the cyclotron frequency. Its path is a rosette of loops through the centre; it closes only when $\Omega$ and $eB/(2m)$ are in a rational ratio.

    { x: "sin(s)*cos(-0.15*s)", y: "sin(s)*sin(-0.15*s)", range: [0, 20.944] }
  ]}
  caption="The path of Problem 1(b), computed from the formulas above with our own ratio of rates, eB/(2m) = 0.15 Ω and eB > 0, until the line has turned through half a turn; lengths in units of the amplitude. Each swing through the centre comes back along a line turned a little clockwise." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The momentum $p_\theta = mr^2\dot\theta + eBr^2/2$ is zero exactly when $\dot\theta = -eB/(2m)$, as found. For $B = 0$ the line stands still and the particle swings along it at $\sqrt{k/m}$: a spring with no angular momentum. In a frame turning with the line, at $-eB/(2m)$, the magnetic force due to the motion along the line is cancelled by the Coriolis force, and what remains is the spring plus an extra stiffness $m(eB/2m)^2$ (magnetic force less centrifugal force), which is where $\Omega^2 = k/m + (eB/2m)^2$ comes from. A direct numerical solution of Newton's equation with the Lorentz force agrees with these formulas (our check, in the calcs file).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A time-dependent Hamiltonian" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

A system with one degree of freedom has a potential that grows linearly with time, so that its Hamiltonian is

<Formula label="Problem 2: the Hamiltonian" tex="H = \frac{p^2}{2m} - mA\,t\,x" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, eq. (2), p. 2" />

where $m$ is the mass and $A$ a constant. At $t = 0$ the particle is at $x = 0$ with momentum $p = mv_0$. Solve the problem with Hamilton's principal function $S$. If you get stuck, solve it another way first, and let that solution suggest the form of $S$; then solve it as asked.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find Hamilton's principal function.

The box is our addition. Our choice: take as the new momentum $\alpha$ the constant to which $\partial S/\partial x$ reduces at $t = 0$, and fix the additive constant of $S$ so that $S = 0$ at $x = 0$, $t = 0$. Give $S(x, \alpha, t)$; type `x`, `al` for $\alpha$, `t`, `m` and `A`.

<Hint>

$H$ depends on time, so $S = W - Et$ does not work. The force $mAt$ does not depend on $x$, so try $S$ linear in $x$, with coefficients that depend on $t$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put $S = x\,f(t) + g(t)$ into the Hamilton-Jacobi equation and collect the terms with $x$ and those without.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2a-s" label="$S =$" expr="x*(al + m*A*t^2/2) - (al^2*t + al*m*A*t^3/3 + m^2*A^2*t^5/20)/(2*m)"
  vars={{ x: [1, 2], al: [0.5, 1], t: [0.2, 0.6], m: [1, 2], A: [0.5, 1] }} names={{ al: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "x*(al + m*A*t^2/2) - al^2*t/(2*m)", hint: "∂S/∂t must cancel the whole of p²/2m, with p = α + mAt²/2: the cross term and the square of mAt²/2 need their integrals too." },
    { expr: "x*(al + m*A*t) - (al^2*t + al*m*A*t^2 + m^2*A^2*t^3/3)/(2*m)", hint: "The x terms of the equation give df/dt = mAt, so f grows as t²/2, not as t." },
    { expr: "x*al - al^2*t/(2*m)", hint: "This is the free particle. The term −mAtx of H must be cancelled by an x-dependent part of ∂S/∂t." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The Hamilton-Jacobi equation with explicit time is a partial differential equation in $x$ and $t$. Because the force does not depend on $x$, a principal function linear in $x$ solves it, and the two coefficients follow from ordinary integrals.

<Step title="The Hamilton-Jacobi equation">

The notes' equation for $S(q, \alpha, t)$,

<Formula label="Time-dependent Hamilton-Jacobi equation" tex="H\left(q_1, \ldots, q_n, \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial q_1}, \ldots, \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial q_n}, t\right) + \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial t} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.104), p. 73" />

with $F_2 = S$, reads here

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the Hamilton-Jacobi equation" tex="\frac{1}{2m}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial x}\right)^2 - mA\,t\,x + \frac{\partial S}{\partial t} = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="A principal function linear in x">

With $S = x f(t) + g(t)$: $\frac{f^2}{2m} - mAtx + x\,\frac{df}{dt} + \frac{dg}{dt} = 0$ for every $x$, so $df/dt = mAt$ and $dg/dt = -f^2/(2m)$. Then $f = \alpha + \frac12mAt^2$, with $\alpha$ the constant of integration, our new momentum. Integrating $-f^2/(2m)$, with $g(0) = 0$:

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): Hamilton's principal function" tex="S(x, \alpha, t) = x\left(\alpha + \frac{1}{2}mAt^2\right) - \frac{1}{2m}\left(\alpha^2 t + \frac{1}{3}\alpha mAt^3 + \frac{1}{20}m^2A^2t^5\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\partial S/\partial x = \alpha + \frac12mAt^2$, and $\partial S/\partial t = mAtx - (\alpha + \frac12mAt^2)^2/(2m)$: together they make the equation vanish. For $A = 0$, $S = \alpha x - \alpha^2t/(2m)$, the free particle's principal function with $\alpha$ its momentum.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the motion $x(t)$ and $p(t)$ for the given start.

The box is our addition. Give $x(t)$; type `v0` for $v_0$, `A` and `t`.

<Hint>

The new coordinate $\beta = \partial S/\partial\alpha$ is a constant. Solve that equation for $x$, then use the start to fix $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2b-x" label="$x(t) =$" expr="v0*t + A*t^3/6"
  vars={{ v0: [0.5, 2], A: [0.5, 2], t: [0.5, 2] }} names={{ v0: "v_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "v0*t + A*t^2/2", hint: "That is a constant acceleration A. Here the force mAt grows with time, so the acceleration is At and x picks up At³/6." },
    { expr: "v0*t + A*t^3/3", hint: "Integrate twice: the velocity is v0 + At²/2, and its integral is v0 t + At³/6." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The constants $\alpha$ and $\beta = \partial S/\partial\alpha$ are the new momentum and coordinate; inverting $\beta = \partial S/\partial\alpha$ gives $x(t)$, and $p = \partial S/\partial x$ gives the momentum.

<Step title="The transformation equations">

As in the notes,

<Formula label="Solution for coordinates and momenta" tex="\beta_i = \frac{\partial S}{\partial\alpha_i} \;\Rightarrow\; q_i = q_i(\alpha, \beta, t), \qquad p_i = \frac{\partial S}{\partial q_i} \;\Rightarrow\; p_i = p_i(\alpha, \beta, t)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.4, eq. (4.107), p. 73" />

Here $\beta = x - \alpha t/m - At^3/6$ and $p = \alpha + \frac12mAt^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The start">

At $t = 0$: $x = \beta = 0$ and $p = \alpha = mv_0$. So

<Formula label="Problem 2(b): the motion" tex="x(t) = v_0 t + \frac{1}{6}At^3, \qquad p(t) = mv_0 + \frac{1}{2}mAt^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Hamilton's equations give the same: $\dot x = \partial H/\partial p = p/m = v_0 + \frac12At^2$ and $\dot p = -\partial H/\partial x = mAt$. This is the other route the sheet suggests: it shows that $p$ is independent of $x$ at every time, which is why $S$ linear in $x$ works. The units: $A$ is an acceleration per time, so $At^3$ is a length.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="The |x| potential" points={10} mit="Problem 3">

A particle of mass $m$ moves back and forth along a line in the potential

<Formula label="Problem 3: the potential" tex="V(x) = F\,|x|, \qquad F > 0 \text{ a constant}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2" />

Using action-angle variables, find the period of the motion as a function of the energy $E$, and check that it has the right dimensions.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the action variable $J$ as a function of $E$.

The box is our addition. Give $J(E)$; type `m`, `E` and `F`.

<Hint>

$J = \oint p\,dx$ over one full cycle. Where are the turning points, and how many equal pieces does the cycle split into?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3a-j" label="$J =$" expr="8*sqrt(2*m)*E^(3/2)/(3*F)"
  vars={{ m: [0.5, 2], E: [0.5, 2], F: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "4*sqrt(2*m)*E^(3/2)/(3*F)", hint: "The cycle runs from −E/F to E/F and back: four equal quarters, not two." },
    { expr: "2*sqrt(2*m)*E^(3/2)/(3*F)", hint: "This is one quarter of the loop. ∮ p dx covers both sides of x = 0, out and back: four quarters." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The action is the area of the closed orbit in phase space. The orbit is symmetric in $x$ and in $p$, so it is four times the area of one quarter.

<Step title="The action">

The notes define

<Formula label="Action variable" tex="J = \oint p\,dq" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.173), p. 85" />

Here $p = \pm\sqrt{2m(E - F|x|)}$ and the turning points are $x = \pm E/F$, so

<Answer id="p5c-two" label="When $k_1 = k_2 = k_3$, the two conserved new angle variables are:" choices={["$w_a$ and $w_b$", "$w_b$ and $w_c$", "$w_a$ and $w_c$", "none: degeneracy does not stop any angle"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A linear change of the actions comes with the transposed inverse change of the angles. Keeping $\sum w J$ the same is the simplest way to find it.

<Step title="The new angles">

Invert the actions: $J_1 = J_c$, $J_2 = J_b - J_c$, $J_3 = J_a - J_b$. With $F_2 = w_1J_c + w_2(J_b - J_c) + w_3(J_a - J_b)$, $w_a = \partial F_2/\partial J_a$, $w_b = \partial F_2/\partial J_b$ and $w_c = \partial F_2/\partial J_c$ give

<Formula label="Problem 5(c): the new angle variables" tex="w_a = w_3, \qquad w_b = w_2 - w_3, \qquad w_c = w_1 - w_2" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

Check with the brackets of (b): $\{w_b, J_b\} = \{w_2 - w_3, J_1 + J_2\} = 1$, $\{w_b, J_a\} = 1 - 1 = 0$, $\{w_b, J_c\} = 0$; the others go the same way, and the new angles have zero brackets among themselves, as the new actions do.

</Step>

<Step title="Their rates">

Each $w_i$ grows at $\nu_i$, so

<Formula label="Problem 5(c): the rates of the new angles" tex="\frac{dw_a}{dt} = \nu_3, \qquad \frac{dw_b}{dt} = \nu_2 - \nu_3, \qquad \frac{dw_c}{dt} = \nu_1 - \nu_2" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The same follows from $H = \nu_3J_a + (\nu_2 - \nu_3)J_b + (\nu_1 - \nu_2)J_c$ and Hamilton's equation $dw_a/dt = \partial H/\partial J_a$, and the same for $b$ and $c$.

</Step>

<Step title="Degeneracy">

For $k_1 = k_2$, $\nu_1 = \nu_2$ and $w_c$ is conserved. For $k_1 = k_2 = k_3$, also $\nu_2 = \nu_3$, and $w_b$ and $w_c$ are both conserved; $w_a$ still grows at $\nu_3$. $H$ then depends on $J_a$ alone.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$w_aJ_a + w_bJ_b + w_cJ_c = w_3(J_1 + J_2 + J_3) + (w_2 - w_3)(J_1 + J_2) + (w_1 - w_2)J_1 = w_1J_1 + w_2J_2 + w_3J_3$. The pattern is that of the Kepler problem in the notes, where $J_1 = J_\varphi$, $J_2 = J_\theta + J_\varphi$, $J_3 = J_r + J_\theta + J_\varphi$ and two of the new angles are constants of the orbit.

</Step>

</Solution>
