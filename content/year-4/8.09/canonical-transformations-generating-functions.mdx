Chapter 4 of Stewart's 8.09 notes turns from the Lagrangian to the Hamiltonian way of doing mechanics, with one question in mind: how much simpler can a problem become if we choose the right variables? The Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formalisms themselves were reviewed in Chapter 1 (Section 1.2 there covers Hamilton's equations). This page follows the notes' short introduction and Section 4.1 (notes pp. 60–65), in order, in its own words and with its own worked example.

## The ideal: every coordinate cyclic

Suppose we were lucky enough to find coordinates $q_i$ that are **all cyclic**: none of them appears in the Hamiltonian. Then $\dot p_i = -\partial H/\partial q_i = 0$, so every momentum is a constant, $p_i = \alpha_i$. If $H$ itself is conserved, it is a function of these constants alone (notes p. 60):

<Formula label="A Hamiltonian of constant momenta only" tex="H = H(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n)" cite="reading1" at="Introduction, eq. (4.1), p. 60" />

and Hamilton's other equation gives each coordinate a constant rate of change:

<Formula label="Every coordinate grows linearly" tex="\dot q_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial \alpha_i} = \omega_i(\alpha) \quad\Rightarrow\quad q_i = \omega_i t + \delta_i" cite="reading1" at="Introduction, eq. (4.2), p. 60" />

with constants $\delta_i$. Nothing could be simpler. The plan of the chapter is to hunt for changes of variables that make as many coordinates cyclic as possible. First we need to know which changes of variables are allowed.

## Transformations in phase space

In Lagrangian mechanics two kinds of change leave the Euler-Lagrange equations intact: a **point transformation** $Q = Q(q, t)$ of the coordinates, with the Lagrangian rewritten in the new variables, and adding a total time derivative $dF[q, t]/dt$ to the Lagrangian (notes pp. 60–61). The Hamiltonian formalism treats coordinates and momenta as independent, so it can afford more freedom: point transformations of the whole **phase space**,

<Formula label="A change of phase-space variables" tex="Q_i = Q_i(q, p, t) \quad\text{and}\quad P_i = P_i(q, p, t)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eq. (4.3), p. 61" />

where the old variables obey Hamilton's equations,

<Formula label="Hamilton's equations" tex="\dot q_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} \quad\text{and}\quad \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eq. (4.4), p. 61" />

Most such transformations scramble the equations of motion. The transformation is called **canonical** if the new variables obey equations of the same form, with some new Hamiltonian $K(Q, P, t)$ (eqs. (4.5)–(4.6)):

<Formula label="Hamilton's equations in the new variables" tex="\dot Q_i = \frac{\partial K}{\partial P_i} \quad\text{and}\quad \dot P_i = -\frac{\partial K}{\partial Q_i}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eq. (4.6), p. 61" />

From here on a repeated index is summed over.

## What a canonical transformation must satisfy

Hamilton's equations follow from Hamilton's principle written in phase space: the integral of $p_i\dot q_i - H$ between fixed end times is stationary. For the new variables to obey the new equations, the same must hold for $P_i\dot Q_i - K$ (eqs. (4.7)–(4.8)):

<Formula label="Hamilton's principle in the old and new variables" tex="\delta\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\left(p_i\dot q_i - H\right)dt = 0, \qquad \delta\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\left(P_i\dot Q_i - K\right)dt = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eqs. (4.7)–(4.8), p. 61" />

Both statements hold together if the two integrands agree up to a constant factor $\lambda$ and the time derivative of some function $F$:

<Formula label="The condition linking the two integrands" tex="\lambda\,(p_i\dot q_i - H) = P_i\dot Q_i - K + \dot F" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eq. (4.9), p. 61" />

The extra term does no harm: its integral is $F(t_2) - F(t_1)$, and since the paths are varied with their end points held fixed, $\delta F$ vanishes at both ends (eq. (4.10)). The factor $\lambda$ sorts transformations into kinds (notes pp. 61–62):

- $\lambda = 1$: a **canonical** transformation, the case studied from now on.
- $\lambda \neq 1$: an **extended canonical** transformation. It adds nothing new, because rescaling $q$ and $p$ brings it back to $\lambda = 1$.
- If in addition $Q$ and $P$ do not depend on $t$ explicitly, the transformation is called **restricted canonical**.

All transformations here are assumed invertible, so that any two of the four sets $q, p, Q, P$ can serve as independent variables.

## Generating functions of the first type

When $F$ is written as a function of a mixture of old and new variables, it is called a **generating function**: it encodes the whole transformation. There are four basic choices. The first takes the old and new coordinates as the independent variables (eq. (4.11)), $F = F_1(q, Q, t)$. Its total time derivative, by the chain rule, goes into the condition with $\lambda = 1$:

<Formula label="The condition for a type-1 generating function" tex="p_i\dot q_i - H = P_i\dot Q_i - K + \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial q_i}\dot q_i + \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial Q_i}\dot Q_i" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eq. (4.12), p. 62" />

Since $q$ and $Q$ are independent, their velocities can be varied independently, and the coefficients of $\dot q_i$, of $\dot Q_i$ and the remaining terms must match one by one:

<Formula label="Type-1 transformation" tex="p_i = \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial q_i}, \qquad P_i = -\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial Q_i}, \qquad K = H + \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eq. (4.13), p. 62" />

The first two relations give $p$ and $P$ as functions of $(q, Q, t)$ (eq. (4.14)); solving them gives the new variables in terms of the old. The third tells us the new Hamiltonian. (Eq. (4.13) in the notes prints the first relation with $\partial Q_i$ in the denominator, a typo; the table on p. 64 has $\partial q_i$.)

The notes give two quick examples (notes pp. 62–63). The function $F_1 = -Q/q$ generates $p = Q/q^2$ and $P = 1/q$, that is $Q = pq^2$ and $P = 1/q$ (eqs. (4.15)–(4.17)). In the other direction, given a transformation one can test whether it is canonical by trying to build its $F_1$. For $Q = \ln(p/q)$ and $P = -(q^2/2 + 1)\,p/q$ (eq. (4.18)), write $p = qe^Q$, integrate $\partial F_1/\partial q = qe^Q$ over $q$ to get $\tfrac{1}{2}q^2e^Q + g(Q)$, and fix the unknown function $g$ by demanding $P = -\partial F_1/\partial Q$. It works with $g = e^Q$:

<Formula label="Generating function of the second example" tex="F_1 = \left(\frac{q^2}{2} + 1\right)e^{Q}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eq. (4.22), p. 63" />

so that transformation is canonical.

## The other three types

The remaining choices pair old and new variables differently. To use the old coordinates and new momenta, take $F = F_2(q, P, t) - Q_iP_i$ (eq. (4.23)). The subtracted product is there so that, after the chain rule, the terms in $\dot Q_i$ cancel and only $\dot q_i$ and $\dot P_i$ remain to be matched (eq. (4.24)). The result:

<Formula label="Type-2 transformation" tex="p_i = \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial q_i}, \qquad Q_i = \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial P_i}, \qquad K = H + \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eq. (4.25), p. 63" />

In the same way $F = F_3(p, Q, t) + q_ip_i$ uses the old momenta and new coordinates (eq. (4.26)), and $F = F_4(p, P, t) + q_ip_i - Q_iP_i$ the old and new momenta (eq. (4.27)). The notes collect all four in a table (p. 64), each with its simplest example:

| Generating function | Transformation | Simplest case | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| $F_1(q, Q, t)$ | $p_i = \partial F_1/\partial q_i$, $P_i = -\partial F_1/\partial Q_i$ | $F_1 = q_iQ_i$ | $Q_i = p_i$, $P_i = -q_i$ |
| $F_2(q, P, t)$ | $p_i = \partial F_2/\partial q_i$, $Q_i = \partial F_2/\partial P_i$ | $F_2 = q_iP_i$ | $Q_i = q_i$, $P_i = p_i$ |
| $F_3(p, Q, t)$ | $q_i = -\partial F_3/\partial p_i$, $P_i = -\partial F_3/\partial Q_i$ | $F_3 = p_iQ_i$ | $Q_i = -q_i$, $P_i = -p_i$ |
| $F_4(p, P, t)$ | $q_i = -\partial F_4/\partial p_i$, $Q_i = \partial F_4/\partial P_i$ | $F_4 = p_iP_i$ | $Q_i = p_i$, $P_i = -q_i$ |

Two lessons hide in the last column. $F_2 = q_iP_i$ is the identity, which makes type 2 the natural starting point for transformations close to the identity (Section 4.3 uses this). And $F_1 = q_iQ_i$ swaps coordinates and momenta, up to a sign: in Hamiltonian mechanics the labels "coordinate" and "momentum" are a matter of convention.

For every type the new Hamiltonian is

<Formula label="The new Hamiltonian" tex="K = H + \frac{\partial F_i}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eq. (4.28), p. 64" />

so a generating function without explicit time dependence leaves the Hamiltonian's value unchanged, $K = H$ (eq. (4.29)), only rewritten in the new variables. With several degrees of freedom one may also mix types, using, say, type 1 for one pair of variables and type 2 for another.

## Point transformations are canonical

A type-2 function linear in the new momenta (notes p. 64),

<Formula label="Generating a point transformation" tex="F_2 = f_i(q, t)\,P_i" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eq. (4.30), p. 64" />

gives new coordinates $Q_i = \partial F_2/\partial P_i = f_i(q, t)$ that depend on the old coordinates only (eq. (4.31)): an ordinary change of coordinates, of the kind familiar from Lagrangian mechanics. Its other half fixes how the momenta must change along with it:

<Formula label="How the momenta follow a change of coordinates" tex="p_i = \frac{\partial f_j}{\partial q_i}\,P_j" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eq. (4.32), p. 64" />

which can be solved for the $P_j$. So every coordinate change, completed by the right momenta, is canonical. (The notes write the derivative as $\partial f_i/\partial q_j$ with $P_j$, which is the transpose of what $\partial F_2/\partial q_i$ gives; for a single coordinate the two agree.)

<WorkedExample title="A free particle in squared coordinates">
<Problem>
A particle of mass $m = 2$ kg moves freely along a line at $v = 3$ m/s, so $H = p^2/(2m)$ and $q = vt$ (taking $q = 0$ at $t = 0$). Use $F_2 = q^2P$ to define new variables. Find $Q$, $P$ and the new Hamiltonian $K$, check that Hamilton's equations hold in the new variables, and evaluate everything at $t = 1$ s.
</Problem>
<Step title="The transformation">
$Q = \partial F_2/\partial P = q^2$ and $p = \partial F_2/\partial q = 2qP$, so $P = p/(2q)$. This is the point transformation $f = q^2$, valid for $q > 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="The new Hamiltonian">
$F_2$ has no explicit time dependence, so $K = H$. With $p = 2qP$ and $q^2 = Q$: $K = (2qP)^2/(2m) = 2QP^2/m$. The free particle now looks like a particle whose "kinetic energy" depends on its position: $Q$ is no longer cyclic. A poor choice of variables can make a problem harder, not easier.
</Step>
<Step title="Check Hamilton's equations">
Along the motion $Q = v^2t^2$ and $P = mv/(2vt) = m/(2t)$. Then $\dot Q = 2v^2t$, and $\partial K/\partial P = 4QP/m = 4v^2t^2\cdot m/(2t)/m = 2v^2t$, which agrees. Also $\dot P = -m/(2t^2)$, and $-\partial K/\partial Q = -2P^2/m = -m/(2t^2)$, which agrees too.
</Step>
<Step title="Numbers at t = 1 s">
$q = 3$ m and $p = mv = 6$ kg m/s, so $Q = 9$ m² and $P = 6/(2 \times 3) = 1$ kg/s. The energy is $H = 6^2/4 = 9$ J, and $K = 2 \times 9 \times 1^2/2 = 9$ J, the same value.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The harmonic oscillator, made cyclic

Now a transformation that helps (notes pp. 64–65). Take the oscillator

<Formula label="Harmonic oscillator" tex="H = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{kq^2}{2}, \qquad k = m\omega^2" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eq. (4.33), p. 64" />

Its energy is a sum of two squares, which suggests writing $p$ as a cosine and $q$ as a sine of a new variable $Q$, with an amplitude set by a new momentum $P$. With a constant $\alpha$ still to be chosen:

<Formula label="A trial transformation" tex="p = \alpha\sqrt{2mP}\,\cos Q, \qquad q = \frac{\alpha}{m\omega}\sqrt{2mP}\,\sin Q" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eq. (4.34), p. 65" />

Substituting, $H = P\alpha^2(\cos^2 Q + \sin^2 Q) = P\alpha^2$ (eq. (4.35)). The new Hamiltonian does not contain $Q$ at all, and the new momentum is the energy divided by $\alpha^2$ (eq. (4.36)).

Is it canonical? Look for a type-1 generating function. Dividing the two relations gives $p/q = m\omega\cot Q$ (eq. (4.37)), so $p$ is known as a function of $q$ and $Q$, and

<Formula label="Building the generating function" tex="F_1 = \int p(q, Q)\,dq + g(Q) = \frac{1}{2}m\omega q^2\cot Q + g(Q), \qquad P = -\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial Q} = \frac{m\omega q^2}{2\sin^2 Q} - \frac{dg}{dQ}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eq. (4.38), p. 65" />

Choosing $dg/dQ = 0$ gives $q^2 = 2P\sin^2 Q/(m\omega)$ (eq. (4.39)). This matches the trial transformation only if $\alpha^2 = \omega$, that is $\alpha = \sqrt{\omega}$. With that choice the transformation is canonical, and

<Formula label="The new momentum" tex="P = \frac{E}{\omega}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eq. (4.40), p. 65" />

Hamilton's equation for the new coordinate is now trivial, because $K = H = \omega P$:

<Formula label="The new coordinate is a phase" tex="\dot Q = \frac{\partial K}{\partial P} = \omega \quad\Rightarrow\quad Q = \omega t + \delta" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eq. (4.41), p. 65" />

and substituting back gives the familiar solution:

<Formula label="The oscillator solved" tex="q = \sqrt{\frac{2E}{m\omega^2}}\,\sin(\omega t + \delta), \qquad p = \sqrt{2mE}\,\cos(\omega t + \delta)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, eq. (4.42), p. 65" />

The notes record the final transformation for later use:

<Formula label="The oscillator's canonical transformation" tex="q = \sqrt{\frac{2P}{m\omega}}\,\sin Q, \qquad p = \sqrt{2m\omega P}\,\cos Q" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.1, p. 65 (unnumbered)" />

In the old variables the motion runs round an ellipse in the $(q, p)$ plane; in the new ones it runs along a straight line, $P$ fixed and $Q$ increasing at the steady rate $\omega$. The graph below draws the ellipse from this transformation: $P$ sets its size, and $Q$ is the position around it. Section 4.6 returns to this picture: $P$ is the oscillator's action divided by $2\pi$, and $Q$ its angle.

That is the whole strategy of the chapter in miniature. The remaining difficulty is that we had to guess the transformation, and checking it meant an integration. Section 4.2 gives a test that needs only derivatives.

## Summary

- A **canonical transformation** $(q, p) \to (Q, P)$ keeps Hamilton's equations in force, with a new Hamiltonian $K$. Hamilton's principle in both sets of variables requires $p_i\dot q_i - H = P_i\dot Q_i - K + \dot F$.
- A **generating function** $F$ of half old and half new variables encodes the transformation. The four types: $F_1(q, Q, t)$, $F_2(q, P, t)$, $F_3(p, Q, t)$, $F_4(p, P, t)$, each with $K = H + \partial F/\partial t$.
- $F_2 = q_iP_i$ is the identity; $F_1 = q_iQ_i$ exchanges coordinates and momenta. Every point transformation is canonical, through $F_2 = f_i(q, t)P_i$.
- For the harmonic oscillator, $q = \sqrt{2P/(m\omega)}\sin Q$, $p = \sqrt{2m\omega P}\cos Q$ is canonical, gives $K = \omega P$ with $P = E/\omega$ constant, and makes $Q = \omega t + \delta$ a phase.
- The aim of the chapter: find variables in which all coordinates are cyclic, so that the motion is trivial.
