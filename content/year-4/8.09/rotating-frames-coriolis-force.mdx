Section 2.1 of the notes ended with the angular velocity $\vec\omega(t)$ of a rotating body. Section 2.2 (notes pp. 33–37) uses it to answer a general question: if we describe motion using axes that rotate, how do the laws of motion look? The answer is a single rule for time derivatives, and from it the familiar **fictitious forces** of a rotating frame: centrifugal, Coriolis, and a third one for a rotation that speeds up or slows down. The notes then apply them to the rotating Earth. Circular motion in an inertial frame was the subject of [Newton's second law and circular motion](/courses/8-01/newtons-second-law-circular-motion); here the frame itself turns.

## Derivatives in a rotating frame

Use unprimed axes $(x, y, z)$ that are fixed (inertial), with basis vectors $\vec e_i$, and primed axes $(x', y', z')$ that turn with the body, with basis vectors $\vec e{}'_i$. Any vector can be written in either basis:

<Formula label="One vector, two bases" tex="\vec b = \sum_i b_i\,\vec e_i = \sum_i b'_i\,\vec e{}'_i" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.2, eq. (2.19), p. 34" />

The fixed basis vectors do not change, $\dot{\vec e}_i = 0$. The body basis vectors keep their length and are carried round by the rotation. In a short time $dt$ the tip of $\vec e{}'_i$ moves sideways by $dt\,\vec\omega \times \vec e{}'_i$, perpendicular both to the axis and to the vector itself, so

<Formula label="Body basis vectors turn with the body" tex="\dot{\vec e}{}'_i = \vec\omega(t) \times \vec e{}'_i" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.2, eq. (2.20), p. 34" />

Differentiate $\vec b = b'_i\,\vec e{}'_i$ (sum over $i$): one part comes from the changing components, the other from the turning basis vectors, $\dot b'_i\,\vec e{}'_i + \vec\omega \times (b'_i\,\vec e{}'_i)$. The first part is what an observer riding with the frame sees, who regards the $\vec e{}'_i$ as fixed. Write $d/dt$ for the rate of change seen in the fixed frame (F) and $d_R/dt$ for that seen in the rotating frame (R). Then for every vector

<Formula label="Time derivatives in fixed and rotating frames" tex="\frac{d\vec b}{dt} = \frac{d_R\vec b}{dt} + \vec\omega \times \vec b" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.2, eq. (2.21), p. 34" />

The notes suggest remembering it as an operator identity, "$d/dt = d_R/dt + \vec\omega\,\times$", which can be applied to any vector. Notice what it says about $\vec\omega$ itself: since $\vec\omega \times \vec\omega = 0$, its rate of change is the same in both frames.

## Fictitious forces

Apply the rule to the position $\vec r$ of a particle of mass $m$, measured from a point on the rotation axis:

<Formula label="Velocities in the two frames" tex="\frac{d\vec r}{dt} = \frac{d_R\vec r}{dt} + \vec\omega \times \vec r \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \vec v_F = \vec v_R + \vec\omega \times \vec r" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.2, eq. (2.22), p. 34" />

Newton's law holds in the fixed frame, $\vec F = m\,d\vec v_F/dt$. Apply the rule a second time, now to $\vec v_F$, and insert (2.22):

<Formula label="Acceleration, expanded" tex="\frac{\vec F}{m} = \frac{d_R\vec v_R}{dt} + \frac{d_R\vec\omega}{dt} \times \vec r + \vec\omega \times \frac{d_R\vec r}{dt} + \vec\omega \times \vec v_R + \vec\omega \times (\vec\omega \times \vec r)" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.2, eq. (2.23), p. 34" />

Here $d_R\vec r/dt = \vec v_R$ is the velocity measured in the rotating frame and $d_R\vec v_R/dt = \vec a_R$ the acceleration measured there. The third and fourth terms on the right are then equal and combine into $2\vec\omega \times \vec v_R$. Moving everything except $\vec a_R$ to the right:

<Formula label="Newton's law in a rotating frame" tex="m\vec a_R = \vec F - m\vec\omega \times (\vec\omega \times \vec r) - 2m\vec\omega \times \vec v_R - m\frac{d_R\vec\omega}{dt} \times \vec r" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.2, eq. (2.24), p. 34" />

An observer in the rotating frame who insists on using $m\vec a_R = $ force sees three extra forces besides the real one:

- the **centrifugal** force, $-m\vec\omega \times (\vec\omega \times \vec r)$;
- the **Coriolis** force, $-2m\vec\omega \times \vec v_R$;
- a force $-m\,(d_R\vec\omega/dt) \times \vec r$ that appears only when the rotation is not uniform, so that the frame has an angular acceleration. (The notes do not name it; it is often called the Euler force.)

The same equation follows from the Euler-Lagrange equations, writing the Lagrangian in the rotating coordinates, where the inertial velocity is $\dot{\vec r} + \vec\omega \times \vec r$ (the notes leave this as a problem-set exercise):

<Formula label="Lagrangian in rotating coordinates" tex="L = \frac{m}{2}\,(\dot{\vec r} + \vec\omega \times \vec r)^2 - V" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.2, eq. (2.25), p. 35" />

**Which way they point.** Split $\vec r$ into a part along the axis and a part perpendicular to it, $\vec r = \vec r_\parallel + \vec r_\perp$. The part along the axis drops out of $\vec\omega \times \vec r$, and for the perpendicular part the double cross product unwinds simply:

<Formula label="The centrifugal force points away from the axis" tex="-\vec\omega \times (\vec\omega \times \vec r) = -\vec\omega \times (\vec\omega \times \vec r_\perp) = \omega^2\,\vec r_\perp" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.2, p. 35" />

So the centrifugal force pushes straight away from the axis, with strength $m\omega^2 r_\perp$, and it acts on everything in the rotating frame. (The notes misprint ω² here as w².) The Coriolis force is different: it acts only on something that **moves** relative to the frame, $\vec v_R \ne 0$, and always at right angles to that motion, so it changes the direction of motion but does no work.

## The rotating Earth

The Earth turns once a day, so (notes p. 35)

<Formula label="The Earth's angular velocity" tex="\omega_\text{Earth} = \frac{2\pi}{24 \times 3600\ \text{s}} \approx 7.3 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{s}^{-1}" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.2, p. 35" />

(Strictly, one turn relative to the stars takes a sidereal day, about 4 minutes shorter, which gives $7.29 \times 10^{-5}$ s⁻¹; the difference does not matter here.) The centrifugal force is small. At the equator, $\omega^2 R_\text{Earth} \approx 0.034$ m/s², about 0.3% of $g$, and it is absorbed into the measured, effective $g$. The Coriolis force is smaller still for everyday speeds, but it acts sideways and steadily, so its effects build up.

**Projectiles turn right.** In the northern hemisphere $\vec\omega$ points towards the north pole, out of the ground. Working out $-\vec\omega \times \vec v_R$ for horizontal motion shows that it always points to the right of the velocity in the northern hemisphere, and to the left in the southern one, where the vertical component of $\vec\omega$ points into the ground. This is why large-scale winds circle low-pressure regions anticlockwise in the north and clockwise in the south.

**The Foucault pendulum.** A long pendulum that is free to swing in any vertical plane is a natural Coriolis detector. For small swings, and to first order in $\omega$, the plane of the swing turns at the rate (notes p. 35, proved on a problem set)

<Formula label="Foucault pendulum precession" tex="\dot\phi \approx \omega_\text{Earth}\sin\lambda" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.2, p. 35" />

where $\lambda$ is the latitude, measured from the equator, and $\phi$ the direction of the swing plane. The turning is clockwise seen from above in the northern hemisphere. It is fastest at the north pole, $\lambda = 90^\circ$, where the plane stays fixed in space while the Earth turns underneath it once a day; at the equator it vanishes. At latitude $40^\circ$ the plane makes a full turn in 24 hours divided by $\sin 40^\circ$, about 37.3 hours. (The notes refer the reader to Fig. 2.2 for the pendulum; the drawing they mean is the unnumbered one on p. 36.)

## Falling bodies drift east

The notes' last example works out the Coriolis deflection of a body moving vertically in the northern hemisphere (pp. 36–37). Take local axes with $z$ pointing straight up, $y$ horizontal towards the north and $x$ horizontal towards the east, and let $\theta$ be the angle between $\vec\omega$ and the vertical $z$ axis (so $\theta = 90^\circ - \lambda$). To first order in the small $\omega$, dropping the centrifugal terms of order $\omega^2$,

<Formula label="Equation of motion near the ground" tex="m\vec a_R = m\dot{\vec v}_R = -mg\,\hat z - 2m\vec\omega \times \vec v" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.2, eq. (2.26), p. 36" />

Start with a vertical velocity $\vec v(0) = v_0\hat z$. For vertical motion, $-\vec\omega \times \vec v$ points along $\pm\hat x$, east or west, so the vertical motion is untouched at this order:

<Formula label="Vertical motion" tex="\ddot z = -g + O(\omega^2) \quad\Rightarrow\quad v_z = v_0 - gt" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.2, eq. (2.27), p. 36" />

while the east-west motion is driven by the vertical velocity. With $\dot x(0) = 0$,

<Formula label="East-west velocity" tex="\ddot x = -2(\vec\omega \times \vec v)_x = -2\omega\sin\theta\,v_z(t) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \dot x = -2\omega\sin\theta\left(v_0 t - \frac{g}{2}t^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.2, eq. (2.28), p. 36" />

and with $x(0) = 0$ one more integration gives the drift:

<Formula label="East-west drift" tex="x(t) = -2\omega\sin\theta\left(\frac{v_0}{2}t^2 - \frac{g}{6}t^3\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.2, eq. (2.29), p. 36" />

Two cases show the sign.

**Dropped from rest.** With $v_0 = 0$ from height $h_\text{max}$, the body falls as

<Formula label="Free fall from rest" tex="z = h_\text{max} - \frac{g}{2}t^2" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.2, eq. (2.30), p. 37" />

and reaches the ground at

<Formula label="Time to fall" tex="t_1 = \sqrt{\frac{2h_\text{max}}{g}}" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.2, eq. (2.31), p. 37" />

Now $\dot x = \omega\sin\theta\,g t^2 > 0$ throughout: the body is pushed east all the way down, and lands at

<Formula label="Eastward drift of a dropped body" tex="x(t_1) = \frac{\omega\sin\theta}{3}\,g\,t_1^3 = \frac{\omega\sin\theta}{3}\sqrt{\frac{8h_\text{max}^3}{g}} > 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.2, p. 37 (unnumbered; corrected)" />

(The notes print this as $8\omega\sin\theta\,h_\text{max}^3/(3g^2)$, which has lost a square root: it does not even have the units of a length. Inserting $t_1$ from (2.31) into (2.29) gives the form above.)

**Thrown straight up.** Now start from the ground with $v_0 > 0$:

<Formula label="Thrown upwards" tex="z(t) = v_0 t - \frac{g}{2}t^2 > 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.2, eq. (2.32), p. 37" />

The body rises to $z = v_0^2/(2g)$ at $t = v_0/g$ and comes back down at $t_1 = 2v_0/g$. By (2.28), $\dot x = -2\omega\sin\theta\,z(t)$, which is negative the whole time the body is in the air, so it drifts west both going up and coming down, and lands at

<Formula label="Westward drift of a body thrown up" tex="x(t_1) = -\frac{4\omega\sin\theta\,v_0^3}{3g^2} < 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.2, eq. (2.33), p. 37" />

It does not land where it started. Seen from outside, the ball keeps its angular momentum about the Earth's axis (gravity points at the centre, so it exerts no torque about the axis). As it rises, its distance from the axis grows, so its eastward speed drops, while keeping pace with the turning ground at that height would need more eastward speed, not less: the ball falls behind, towards the west. The dropped stone is the opposite case: it starts with the larger eastward speed of the tower top, and its eastward speed grows further as it falls closer to the axis, so it gets ahead of the ground below.

<WorkedExample title="A stone dropped from a tower, a ball thrown up from its foot">
<Problem>
At latitude $45^\circ$, a stone is dropped from rest from the top of an 80 m tower, and a ball is thrown straight up from the ground at 20 m/s. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s² and $\omega = 2\pi/(86\,400\ \text{s}) = 7.27 \times 10^{-5}$ s⁻¹. How far east or west of the starting vertical does each land?
</Problem>
<Step title="The angle">
At latitude $45^\circ$, the angle between $\vec\omega$ and the vertical is $\theta = 90^\circ - 45^\circ = 45^\circ$, so $\omega\sin\theta = 7.27 \times 10^{-5} \times 0.707 = 5.14 \times 10^{-5}$ s⁻¹.
</Step>
<Step title="The stone">
It falls for $t_1 = \sqrt{2 \times 80/9.8} = 4.04$ s. Its drift is $x = \tfrac{1}{3} \times 5.14 \times 10^{-5} \times 9.8 \times 4.04^3 \approx 0.011$ m: about **1.1 cm east**.
</Step>
<Step title="The ball">
It rises to $20^2/(2 \times 9.8) = 20.4$ m and is in the air for $2 \times 20/9.8 = 4.08$ s, about as long as the stone. Its drift is $x = -4 \times 5.14 \times 10^{-5} \times 20^3/(3 \times 9.8^2) \approx -0.0057$ m: about **5.7 mm west**.
</Step>
<Step title="Comparing">
Both effects are tiny next to the heights involved (parts in ten thousand), which is why careful experiments are needed to see them. The two flights last almost the same time, yet the ball's drift is only about half the stone's.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Basis vectors fixed in a frame turning with $\vec\omega$ change as $\dot{\vec e}{}'_i = \vec\omega \times \vec e{}'_i$, so for any vector $d\vec b/dt = d_R\vec b/dt + \vec\omega \times \vec b$. The angular velocity has the same rate of change in both frames.
- Applied twice to a position, this gives Newton's law in the rotating frame, $m\vec a_R = \vec F - m\vec\omega \times (\vec\omega \times \vec r) - 2m\vec\omega \times \vec v_R - m\,(d_R\vec\omega/dt) \times \vec r$: centrifugal, Coriolis, and a force from a changing rotation. The Lagrangian $L = \tfrac{m}{2}(\dot{\vec r} + \vec\omega \times \vec r)^2 - V$ gives the same.
- The centrifugal force is $m\omega^2\vec r_\perp$, away from the axis; the Coriolis force acts only on moving bodies, at right angles to their motion.
- On the Earth, $\omega \approx 7.3 \times 10^{-5}$ s⁻¹. Moving bodies are turned to the right in the northern hemisphere; a Foucault pendulum's plane turns at $\omega\sin\lambda$; a dropped body lands east of the point below, one thrown straight up lands west of its starting point.
