With the equations of motion and conservation laws of [Section 6.2](/courses/8-09/euler-equation-fluid-conservation) in hand, the notes turn to two important special cases of ideal fluids: fluids at rest and flows that do not change in time (pp. 115–120). The notes' figures (a tank, a narrowing pipe, stream and flow lines, a Pitot tube) are not reproduced; the worked examples and numbers are our own.

## Static fluids

A **static** fluid has $\vec v = 0$ everywhere: it is at rest, in mechanical equilibrium (p. 115). Continuity then gives $\partial\rho/\partial t = 0$, so $\rho$ and $P$ do not change in time. With gravity, $\Phi = gz$, the Euler equation (6.34) keeps only the pressure and the potential (p. 115):

<Formula label="Hydrostatic equilibrium" tex="\frac{\nabla P}{\rho} = -g\hat z" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.3, eq. (6.52), p. 115" />

The $x$ and $y$ components say that $P$ cannot change horizontally, so $P$ depends only on $z$; the equation then makes $\rho$ a function of $z$ only, and the combination $(1/\rho)\,\partial P/\partial z = -g$ is the same at every height (p. 115).

**A liquid.** If the density is constant, integrating gives (p. 115)

<Formula label="Pressure in a liquid of constant density" tex="P(z) = P_0 - \rho gz" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.3, eq. (6.53), p. 115" />

so the pressure falls steadily as one goes up, and grows by $\rho g$ per unit depth going down.

**An isothermal atmosphere.** Treat the air as an ideal gas at one uniform temperature $T$, so $P = \rho R'T$ (p. 116). Then $\rho = P/R'T$ and

<Formula label="Hydrostatics of an isothermal gas" tex="\frac{\partial P}{\partial z} = -\frac{g}{R'T}\,P" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.3, eq. (6.54), p. 116" />

whose solution falls off exponentially (p. 116):

<Formula label="The isothermal atmosphere" tex="P(z) = P_0\,e^{-gz/R'T}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.3, eq. (6.55), p. 116" />

The pressure drops by a factor $e$ over the **scale height** $R'T/g$ (our name for it).

<Plot variable="z" domain={[0, 20]} range={[0, 1.05]} xLabel="z (km)" yLabel="P/P₀"

    { expr: "exp(-z*1000*9.81/(287*T))", label: "isothermal gas" },

  caption="Pressure against height, computed from (6.55) for an isothermal ideal gas with R′ = 287 J/(kg K) (air) and g = 9.81 m/s², and from (6.53) for a fluid that kept the ground-level density (dashed), which would end at one scale height R′T/g. The slider sets the temperature T in kelvin." />

**Archimedes' principle.** The upward push of a fluid on a floating or submerged body equals the weight of the fluid it displaces (p. 116). It follows from the constant-density result: for a block floating upright with its bottom at depth $z$ below the surface, the extra pressure on the bottom is $\rho gz$; times the bottom's area, that is $\rho g$ times the volume displaced, the weight of the displaced fluid.

The notes add a warning (p. 116): a fluid whose temperature is not uniform is not in stable mechanical equilibrium, because temperature differences drive **convection** currents that stir it. The isothermal example relied on the fluid being ideal and at one temperature.

<WorkedExample title="The scale height of a cold atmosphere">
<Problem>
Treat the air as an isothermal ideal gas at 250 K, with $R' = 287$ J/(kg K) and $g = 9.81$ m/s². Find the scale height, and the pressure at 8.8 km (roughly the height of Everest) as a fraction of the ground-level value.
</Problem>
<Step title="Scale height">
$R'T/g = 287 \times 250/9.81 \approx 7310$ m, about 7.3 km.
</Step>
<Step title="Pressure at 8.8 km">
$P/P_0 = e^{-8800/7314} = e^{-1.20} \approx 0.30$. In this model the air at the top of Everest is at under a third of sea-level pressure; the real atmosphere is not isothermal, so this is only a rough estimate.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Steady flows

A **steady** flow has $\partial\rho/\partial t = 0$, $\partial P/\partial t = 0$ and $\partial\vec v/\partial t = 0$ at every point $\vec r$ (p. 116): the fluid moves, but the pattern does not change. Continuity becomes $\nabla\cdot(\rho\vec v) = 0$, which says that whatever flows into a region flows out again. In integral form, for any closed surface $S$ (p. 116):

<Formula label="Steady flow: no net flux" tex="\oint_S d\vec S\cdot\left(\rho\vec v\right) = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.3, eq. (6.56), p. 116" />

With no explicit time dependence, Bernoulli's equation (6.51) says that the energy per unit mass of each element stays the same as it moves (p. 116):

<Formula label="Bernoulli's equation for a steady flow" tex="\frac{v^2}{2} + \Phi + \frac{P}{\rho} + U = B" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.3, eq. (6.57), p. 116" />

where $B$ is constant along the path of each element (it may differ from one path to another). Usually $\Phi = gz$.

For a **steady incompressible** flow, $\nabla\cdot\vec v = 0$ and $U$ is constant (p. 116). Continuity then gives $\vec v\cdot\nabla\rho = 0$, so the density does not change along the flow; the notes write $\nabla\rho = 0$ and take it constant throughout. Absorbing the constant $U$, Bernoulli's equation becomes (p. 116)

<Formula label="Bernoulli for steady incompressible flow" tex="\frac{v^2}{2} + \Phi + \frac{P}{\rho} = B'" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.3, eq. (6.58), p. 116" />

**A narrowing pipe.** Water at room temperature is very nearly incompressible (p. 117). Let it flow through a horizontal pipe that narrows from area $A_1$ to $A_2$; with both ends at nearly the same height the $gz$ term drops out. Across each cross-section the velocity can be taken uniform and along the pipe, and Bernoulli connects the two ends (p. 117):

<Formula label="Bernoulli between the ends of the pipe" tex="\frac{\rho v_1^2}{2} + P_1 = \frac{\rho v_2^2}{2} + P_2" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.3, eq. (6.59), p. 117" />

Continuity across a surface bounded by the pipe wall and the two sections says that the volume entering per second leaves per second, $A_1v_1 = A_2v_2$. Eliminating $v_1$ (p. 117):

<Formula label="Pressure drop in a narrowing pipe" tex="P_1 = P_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_2^2\left(1 - \left(\frac{A_2}{A_1}\right)^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.3, eq. (6.60), p. 117" />

With $A_1 > A_2$, the fluid speeds up in the narrow part, $v_2 > v_1$, and its pressure is lower there, $P_1 > P_2$ (p. 117). The pressure difference is what accelerates the fluid into the constriction.

**A jet from a tank.** A tank is filled to height $z_1$ and has a small hole at height $z_2 < z_1$ (p. 117). If the tank is much wider than the hole, the surface hardly moves, $v_1 \approx 0$, and Bernoulli between the surface and the jet reads (p. 117)

<Formula label="Bernoulli from the surface to the hole" tex="\frac{1}{2}\rho v_2^2 + P_2 + \rho gz_2 = P_1 + \rho gz_1" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.3, eq. (6.61), p. 117" />

Both the surface and the jet are open to the air, $P_1 = P_2 = P_{\text{atm}}$, so (p. 117)

<Formula label="Speed of the jet" tex="v_2 = \sqrt{2g\left(z_1 - z_2\right)}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.3, eq. (6.62), p. 117" />

the speed a body gains falling from rest through $z_1 - z_2$, but pointing sideways rather than down.

## Stream lines and flow lines

Two pictures of a flow are common (pp. 118–119). **Stream lines** are drawn at one instant, everywhere tangent to the velocity. With $s$ measuring distance along the line (p. 118),

<Formula label="Definition of a stream line" tex="\frac{d\vec x(s)}{ds}\times\vec v = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.3, eq. (6.63), p. 118" />

They never cross, because each point has only one velocity. Writing out the cross product, its $z$ component is $v_y\,dx/ds - v_x\,dy/ds = 0$, and similarly for the others, which gives (p. 118)

<Formula label="Stream lines as ratios" tex="\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{v_y}{v_x}, \qquad \frac{dz}{dx} = \frac{v_z}{v_x} \qquad \left(\text{or}\ \frac{dz}{dy} = \frac{v_z}{v_y}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.3, eq. (6.64), p. 118" />

valid where $x$ can replace $s$ as the parameter along the curve. **Flow lines** are the actual paths of fluid elements (p. 118):

<Formula label="Definition of a flow line" tex="\frac{d\vec x}{dt} = \vec v(\vec x, t)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.3, eq. (6.65), p. 118" />

They depend on time, and can cross: an element passing through a point later may follow a different path from one that passed earlier.

In a **steady** flow the two coincide (pp. 118–119). The flow-line equations $dx/dt = v_x$, $dy/dt = v_y$ have time-independent right sides, so dividing them removes $t$ and gives $dy/dx = v_y/v_x$, the stream-line equation; conversely, taking $s = t$ as the parameter on a stream line, $d\vec x/dt \times \vec v = \vec v\times\vec v = 0$. So in a steady flow Bernoulli's constant (6.57), constant along the paths of elements, is constant along stream lines; the notes restate the equation there as (6.66) (p. 119).

## Stagnation points and the Pitot tube

Let a nearly uniform steady flow of an ideal incompressible fluid run into a wall (p. 119). At the wall the flow must turn along it, and often part goes one way and part the other. Between them is a **stagnation point** $o$ where $v_o = 0$. On the stream line that ends there, Bernoulli (ignoring gravity) gives $P_o = P_\infty + \tfrac12\rho v_\infty^2$, with $v_\infty$ and $P_\infty$ the speed and pressure far upstream. The stagnation point has the **highest pressure** in the whole flow: all of the energy there is pressure, none kinetic (p. 119).

A **Pitot tube** uses this to measure speed, for example on aircraft (pp. 119–120). Air of density $\rho_a$ flows past an opening facing upstream; behind the opening the tube is bent down into a U holding a liquid of density $\rho_l$, whose other arm is open to the moving air through a side opening $s$, where the air passes at the free-stream speed. The air in the tube is at rest, so the opening $o$ is a stagnation point. The notes compare four points (p. 120): far upstream, the stagnation point $o$, the point $s$ near the liquid surface in the open arm, and the top $m$ of the liquid in the arm connected to $o$. Take $s$ a depth $z_o + z_s$ below $o$ and $m$ a further $\Delta h$ below $s$. Bernoulli on the stream line to $o$ gives $P_o$; Bernoulli from far upstream to $s$, where the speed is the same, leaves only a height term of hydrostatic form; and hydrostatics in the still air of the tube and in the liquid gives $P_m$ two ways (p. 120):

<Formula label="Pressures in the Pitot tube" tex="P_o = P_\infty + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho_av_\infty^2, \qquad P_s = P_\infty + \rho_ag\left(z_o + z_s\right), \qquad P_m = P_o + \rho_ag\left(z_o + z_s + \Delta h\right) = P_s + \rho_lg\,\Delta h" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.3, unnumbered, p. 120" />

[The notes print the second line as $P_\infty + \tfrac12\rho_ag(z_o + z_s)$. The half belongs only to the kinetic term: with equal speeds far upstream and at $s$, Bernoulli gives the full $\rho_ag(z_o + z_s)$, and only without the half do the air terms cancel to give (6.67).] Equating the two expressions for $P_o - P_s$ removes every pressure (p. 120):

<Formula label="Pitot tube: speed and column height" tex="\frac{1}{2}\rho_av_\infty^2 = g\left(\rho_l - \rho_a\right)\Delta h" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.3, eq. (6.67), p. 120" />

or, since the liquid is far denser than air (p. 120),

<Formula label="Airspeed from a Pitot tube" tex="v_\infty^2 = 2g\left(\frac{\rho_l}{\rho_a} - 1\right)\Delta h \approx 2g\,\frac{\rho_l}{\rho_a}\,\Delta h" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.3, eq. (6.68), p. 120" />

Knowing the density ratio, a single measured height difference $\Delta h$ gives the airspeed.

<WorkedExample title="A Pitot tube in a wind tunnel">
<Problem>
A Pitot tube filled with water ($\rho_l = 1000$ kg/m³) sits in the air flow of a wind tunnel ($\rho_a = 1.2$ kg/m³) and shows a height difference of 10 cm. Find the air speed, with and without the approximation in (6.68).
</Problem>
<Step title="Exact form">
$\rho_l/\rho_a = 833.3$, so $v_\infty^2 = 2 \times 9.81 \times (833.3 - 1) \times 0.10 \approx 1633$ m²/s² and $v_\infty \approx 40.4$ m/s, about 145 km/h.
</Step>
<Step title="Approximate form">
Dropping the 1: $v_\infty^2 \approx 2 \times 9.81 \times 833.3 \times 0.10 \approx 1635$ m²/s², $v_\infty \approx 40.4$ m/s. The correction is one part in about 830 in $v^2$ (half that in $v$), invisible at this precision.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Hydrostatic equilibrium in a galaxy cluster">
The same balance $\nabla P = -\rho\nabla\Phi$, with the gravitational potential of the whole cluster as $\Phi$, is assumed for the hot X-ray gas in [galaxy clusters](/courses/fundamentals-of-dark-matter/galaxy-clusters) in Ferreras's *Fundamentals of Dark Matter*. Measuring the gas density and temperature then gives the mass inside each radius, far more than the gas and galaxies supply: evidence for dark matter.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Static fluids** ($\vec v = 0$): $\nabla P/\rho = -g\hat z$. Constant density gives $P = P_0 - \rho gz$; an isothermal ideal gas gives $P = P_0e^{-gz/R'T}$. Archimedes' principle follows, and non-uniform temperature drives convection.
- **Steady flows** (no $\partial/\partial t$): $\oint d\vec S\cdot(\rho\vec v) = 0$, and $v^2/2 + \Phi + P/\rho + U = B$ along each path; for incompressible flow $v^2/2 + \Phi + P/\rho = B'$.
- A narrowing pipe: $A_1v_1 = A_2v_2$, and the faster fluid has lower pressure, $P_1 - P_2 = \tfrac12\rho v_2^2(1 - (A_2/A_1)^2)$. A jet from a tank leaves at $\sqrt{2g(z_1 - z_2)}$.
- **Stream lines** (tangent to $\vec v$ at one instant) and **flow lines** (paths of elements) coincide in steady flow.
- The **stagnation point** has the highest pressure, $P_\infty + \tfrac12\rho v_\infty^2$; the **Pitot tube** turns it into a speed, $v_\infty^2 \approx 2g(\rho_l/\rho_a)\Delta h$.
