The [previous section](/courses/8-09/one-dimensional-bifurcations) classified fixed points of a single variable, where the only question is whether the flow points towards or away from them. In two dimensions trajectories can also turn, so there are more kinds of fixed point. Stewart classifies them through the linearized equations, much as the 18.03 pages on [sketching linear systems](/courses/18-03/sketching-linear-systems) and [repeated and complex eigenvalues](/courses/18-03/repeated-and-complex-eigenvalues) do, and then turns to systems with a conserved quantity. (Citations give the printed page numbers 164 to 173 of the Chapter 7 notes. On this page $\tau$ is the trace and $\Delta$ the determinant of a matrix, not a torque or a difference.)

## Linear systems in the plane

Near a fixed point $\vec x_* = (x_*, y_*)$ the flow can be linearized, so the notes first study a general linear system with its fixed point at the origin (p. 164):

<Formula label="A general 2D linear system" tex="\dot x = ax + by, \qquad \dot y = cx + dy" cite="reading1" at="p. 164, eq. (7.37)" />

or, in matrix form, with no restriction on the four coefficients (p. 164),

<Formula label="Matrix form" tex="\dot{\vec x} = M\vec x, \qquad M = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 164, eq. (7.38)" />

**Two uncoupled flows** (pp. 164–165). The simplest case is $\dot x = ax$, $\dot y = -y$ (eq. 7.39), solved by $x = x_0e^{at}$, $y = y_0e^{-t}$ (eq. 7.40). For every $a < 0$ the origin attracts everything, but in three different ways: if $a < -1$, $x$ dies faster than $y$, so trajectories first collapse onto the $y$-axis and then slide in along it; if $a = -1$ both decay at the same rate and trajectories are straight rays; if $-1 < a < 0$, $y$ dies first and trajectories come in along the $x$-axis. The notes call all three stable nodes; strictly, the middle case, $a = -1$, has $M = -I$ and is the **star** defined below, while the other two are **stable nodes**. If $a = 0$, every point of the $x$-axis is a fixed point, so the origin is no longer isolated. If $a > 0$, the origin is a **saddle**: trajectories come in along the $y$-axis, the **stable manifold**, and leave along the $x$-axis, the **unstable manifold** (p. 165).

**What "stable" means** (pp. 165–166). With more room to move, the definitions need care. A fixed point $\vec x_*$ is

- **attracting** if every trajectory that starts close enough to it approaches it as $t \to \infty$;
- **Lyapunov stable** if every trajectory that starts close enough stays close for all time;
- **stable** if it is both.

The two properties are independent. On the line of fixed points at $a = 0$, each point is Lyapunov stable (nearby trajectories move straight towards the line and stop) but not attracting (they stop at a different point of the line). Conversely, a fixed point that lets a trajectory make a long excursion before returning can be attracting without being Lyapunov stable (p. 166).

## Eigenvalues from trace and determinant

For a general $M$, look for solutions along eigenvectors (p. 166):

<Formula label="Eigenvector solutions" tex="\dot{\vec a} = M\vec a = \lambda\vec a \quad\Rightarrow\quad \vec a(t) = \vec a_0 e^{\lambda t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 166, eq. (7.41)" />

so $\det(M - \lambda I) = 0$. With the determinant and trace of $M$ (p. 166)

<Formula label="Determinant and trace" tex="\Delta \equiv \det M = ad - bc, \qquad \tau \equiv \operatorname{tr} M = a + d" cite="reading1" at="p. 166, eq. (7.42)" />

(the notes print $\det M = ac - bd$, a slip: for the matrix (7.38) it is $ad - bc$), the characteristic equation is $\lambda^2 - \tau\lambda + \Delta = 0$, with roots (p. 166)

<Formula label="Eigenvalues of a 2 × 2 matrix" tex="\lambda_\pm = \frac{\tau \pm \sqrt{\tau^2 - 4\Delta}}{2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 166, eq. (7.43)" />

The eigenvectors $\vec a_\pm$ are in general not orthogonal. If they are distinct, linearity gives the general solution (p. 166)

<Formula label="General solution of the linear system" tex="\vec x(t) = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[C_+\vec a_+ e^{\lambda_+ t} + C_-\vec a_- e^{\lambda_- t}\bigr]" cite="reading1" at="p. 166, eq. (7.44)" />

with the real part taken at the end when the eigenvalues are complex. Three cases follow (pp. 166–168).

**1. Real, distinct eigenvalues.** The picture is that of the uncoupled example, with the axes replaced by the eigenvector directions. If $\lambda_- < 0 < \lambda_+$, trajectories grow along $\vec a_+$ and decay along $\vec a_-$: a saddle. If $\lambda_- < \lambda_+ < 0$, the component along $\vec a_-$ dies first, so trajectories arrive tangent to $\vec a_+$, the slow direction: a stable node. (The notes illustrate both with $\vec a_+ = (1, 1)$ and $\vec a_- = (1, -4)$, p. 166.)

**2. Equal real eigenvalues** $\lambda_+ = \lambda_- = \lambda$. Two independent eigenvectors exist only if $M$ is a multiple of the identity (p. 167),

<Formula label="Star" tex="M = \lambda I" cite="reading1" at="p. 167, eq. (7.45)" />

and then every direction is an eigenvector and trajectories are straight rays: a **star**. Otherwise there is a single eigenvector, as for $M = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda & b \\ 0 & \lambda \end{pmatrix}$, whose only eigenvector is $(1, 0)$: a **degenerate node**, where trajectories swing round to approach along that one direction (p. 167).

**3. Complex eigenvalues** $\lambda_\pm = \alpha \pm i\omega$ with $\omega \ne 0$. Trajectories turn about the fixed point. If $\alpha < 0$ they spiral in (**stable spiral**); if $\alpha = 0$ they are closed orbits around it (**centre**); if $\alpha > 0$ they spiral out (**unstable spiral**) (p. 168). (The captions of the notes' Figs. 7.36 and 7.38 have the signs of $\alpha$ the wrong way round; the text is right.)

## The trace–determinant diagram

Since $\tau = \lambda_+ + \lambda_-$ and $\Delta = \lambda_+\lambda_-$, the pair $(\Delta, \tau)$ settles the type (pp. 168–169):

- $\Delta < 0$: the eigenvalues are real with opposite signs: **saddle**.
- $\Delta = 0$: one eigenvalue vanishes: a line of **non-isolated** fixed points.
- $\Delta > 0$: **stable nodes** for $\tau < -2\sqrt\Delta$, **stable spirals** for $-2\sqrt\Delta < \tau < 0$, **centres** for $\tau = 0$, **unstable spirals** for $0 < \tau < 2\sqrt\Delta$, **unstable nodes** for $\tau > 2\sqrt\Delta$, and **stars or degenerate nodes** on $\tau = \pm 2\sqrt\Delta$.

Every unstable fixed point with $\Delta > 0$ has $\tau > 0$ and every stable one $\tau < 0$, stars and degenerate nodes included (p. 169).

**How far to trust linearization** (p. 169). Saddles, nodes and spirals fill open regions of the diagram, so a small nonlinear correction, which shifts $(\Delta, \tau)$ a little, cannot change them. The borderline cases (centres, stars, degenerate nodes, non-isolated fixed points) sit on lines, and nonlinear terms can tip them into a neighbouring region. Among the isolated ones ($\Delta > 0$), only for a centre is the stability itself at stake: a star or degenerate node can become a node or spiral of the same stability, but a centre can turn into a stable or an unstable spiral (p. 169). (For a non-isolated case, $\Delta = 0$, the nonlinear terms decide everything: $\dot x = x^2$, $\dot y = -y$ linearizes like the line of fixed points but its origin is unstable.)

## General two-dimensional systems

For a nonlinear system (p. 169)

<Formula label="A general 2D flow" tex="\dot{\vec x} = \vec f(\vec x) = \bigl(f_x(x, y),\, f_y(x, y)\bigr)" cite="reading1" at="p. 169, eq. (7.46)" />

with several fixed points, linearize about each one: set $u = x - x_*$, $v = y - y_*$, $\vec u = (u, v)$, and keep first order (p. 170),

<Formula label="Linearization of a 2D flow" tex="\dot{\vec u} = M\vec u, \qquad M_{ij} = \left.\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}\right|_{\vec x = \vec x_*}" cite="reading1" at="p. 170, eq. (7.47)" />

the first term of a Taylor series about the fixed point, where $\vec f(\vec x_*) = 0$.

**Rabbits and sheep** (pp. 170–171). Two species compete for the same grass. Alone, a population might follow $\dot x = x(1 - x)$, growing when small and shrinking once food runs short; together they inhibit each other (p. 170):

<Formula label="Competition model" tex="\dot x = x(3 - x - 2y), \qquad \dot y = y(2 - y - x)" cite="reading1" at="p. 170, eq. (7.48)" />

with rabbits $x$ and sheep $y$. The growth rates favour the rabbits (3 for $x$, 2 for $y$), but the cross terms favour the sheep: a sheep reduces the rabbits' growth twice as much as a rabbit reduces the sheep's. The notes call this the Lotka–Volterra model; it is the competition version, not the predator–prey one. The fixed points are (p. 170)

<Formula label="Fixed points of the competition model" tex="\vec x_* \in \{(0, 0),\ (0, 2),\ (3, 0),\ (1, 1)\}" cite="reading1" at="p. 170, eq. (7.49)" />

and the Jacobian is $M = \begin{pmatrix} 3 - 2x - 2y & -2x \\ -y & 2 - x - 2y \end{pmatrix}$. At $(0, 0)$, $M = \operatorname{diag}(3, 2)$: an unstable node. At $(0, 2)$, $\tau = -3$, $\Delta = 2$, $\lambda = -1, -2$: a stable node (sheep only). At $(3, 0)$, $\lambda = -1, -3$: another stable node (rabbits only). At $(1, 1)$, $\lambda = -1 \pm \sqrt2$, one positive and one negative: a saddle (p. 170; the notes label the rabbit fixed point $(2, 0)$ in this list, a slip for $(3, 0)$). Joining these local pictures: the stable manifold of the saddle, a curve through the unstable node and the saddle, divides the quadrant into the basin where the sheep win and the basin where the rabbits win. Coexistence is impossible, and which species survives depends on the starting populations (p. 170).

## Systems with a conserved quantity

Many mechanical systems conserve energy. Generalizing, call a system **conservative with $E$** if some function $E(\vec x)$ is constant along every trajectory, $\dot E = 0$, and is not constant on any closed region of phase space (which excludes the trivial choice of a constant) (p. 171). This is not the same as $\nabla\cdot\vec f = 0$ in general, which is why the notes keep the separate name. Three results follow (pp. 171–172).

- **No attracting fixed points** (p. 171). If a fixed point attracted a whole basin, every point of the basin would flow to it and so share its value of $E$, making $E$ constant on a region. Contradiction.
- **Minima of $E$ are centres** (p. 172). If the $\partial f_i/\partial x_j$ are continuous and $\vec x_*$ is an isolated fixed point at a minimum of $E$, all trajectories close enough to it are closed orbits around it. This is the small-oscillation result of the notes' chapter on vibrations (Chapter 3), seen in phase space.
- **In two dimensions, $\nabla\cdot\vec f = 0$ means there is such an $E$** (p. 172). Construct a function $H(x, y)$ with $\partial H/\partial y = f_x$ and $\partial H/\partial x = -f_y$. Integrability requires $\partial f_x/\partial x = -\partial f_y/\partial y$, which is exactly $\nabla\cdot\vec f = 0$. Explicitly, with a fixed reference point $(x_0, y_0)$,

<Formula label="The conserved function of a 2D divergence-free flow" tex="H(x, y) = \int_{y_0}^{y} f_x(x, y')\,dy' - \int_{x_0}^{x} f_y(x', y_0)\,dx', \qquad \dot x = \frac{\partial H}{\partial y}, \quad \dot y = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}" cite="reading1" at="p. 172, eq. (7.50), adapted (see below)" />

These are Hamilton's equations with $H$ as Hamiltonian, so $H$ is conserved and serves as $E$, and its stationary points, $\nabla H = 0$, are the fixed points of the flow (p. 172). (The notes integrate $f_y(x', y)$ at the running $y$ instead of at $y_0$, which leads them to the result that $\partial H/\partial y$ is $f_x$ or $2f_x$, Hamilton's equations up to a rescaling; evaluating the second integral at $y_0$, as here, gives them exactly.)

**Example** (pp. 172–173). The one-dimensional motion $\ddot x = ax - x^2 \equiv -U'(x)$ with $a > 0$ (eq. 7.51) becomes $\dot x = y = f_x$, $\dot y = ax - x^2 = f_y$ (eq. 7.52), with $\nabla\cdot\vec f = 0$ since $f_x$ does not depend on $x$ nor $f_y$ on $y$. The construction gives kinetic plus potential energy (p. 172):

<Formula label="Conserved function of the example" tex="H = \frac{y^2}{2} - \frac{ax^2}{2} + \frac{x^3}{3} = K(y) + U(x), \qquad \dot x = \frac{\partial K}{\partial y}, \quad \dot y = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}" cite="reading1" at="p. 172, eqs. (7.53), (7.54)" />

The fixed points $(0, 0)$ and $(a, 0)$ are the stationary points of $H$. At the origin $\partial^2H/\partial x^2 = -a < 0$ and $\partial^2H/\partial y^2 = 1 > 0$, with no mixed derivative: a saddle of $H$, so a saddle point of the flow. At $(a, 0)$ both second derivatives are positive ($a$ and 1): a minimum of $H$, so a centre (p. 173, eqs. 7.55, 7.56). Trajectories with $H < 0$ near the centre are bound and closed; those with $H > 0$ are unbound; the dividing trajectory $H = 0$ runs into the saddle (p. 173).

## Summary

- Near a fixed point, $\dot{\vec u} = M\vec u$ with $M_{ij} = \partial f_i/\partial x_j$. In two dimensions the eigenvalues are $\lambda_\pm = \frac12(\tau \pm \sqrt{\tau^2 - 4\Delta})$ with $\tau = \operatorname{tr}M$, $\Delta = \det M = ad - bc$.
- $\Delta < 0$: saddle. $\Delta = 0$: non-isolated. $\Delta > 0$: nodes outside the parabola $\tau^2 = 4\Delta$, spirals inside, stars and degenerate nodes on it, centres on $\tau = 0$; stable for $\tau < 0$, unstable for $\tau > 0$.
- A fixed point is stable if it is both attracting and Lyapunov stable; the two are independent.
- Linearization is reliable for saddles, nodes and spirals; borderline cases can be tipped by nonlinear terms, a centre can lose or gain stability, and non-isolated cases are decided by the nonlinear terms.
- In the rabbits-and-sheep competition model the coexistence point is a saddle whose stable manifold separates the basins where one or the other species wins.
- A system with a conserved $E(\vec x)$ has no attracting fixed points, and isolated minima of $E$ are centres. In two dimensions $\nabla\cdot\vec f = 0$ guarantees a Hamiltonian $H$, with the fixed points at its stationary points.
