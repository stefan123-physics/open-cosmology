The conventions are those of the notes and the lessons. The inertia tensor is $I_{ab} = \sum_i m_i\left(\delta_{ab}\,r_i^2 - r_{ia}r_{ib}\right)$, so its off-diagonal entries carry a minus sign. The Euler angles $\phi$, $\theta$, $\psi$ are the turns about $z$, then about the new $x$ axis (the line of nodes), then about the new $z$ axis, and $U = U_\psi U_\theta U_\phi$ takes the components of a vector in the fixed axes to its components in the body axes. An overdot is $d/dt$. A dagger $\dagger$ means complex conjugate and transpose.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Rotation angle in Euler's theorem" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

The lesson on Euler's theorem shows that every rotation $U$ (a real orthogonal matrix with determinant 1) leaves an axis fixed. This problem finishes the argument: it shows that the angle $\Phi$ in the eigenvalues really is the angle of a rotation about that axis.

The set-up, as the sheet recalls it: $\vec r\,' = U\vec r$, and the eigenvectors $\vec\xi_i$ of $U$ satisfy

<Formula label="Problem 1: eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a rotation" tex="U\vec\xi_i = \lambda_i\,\vec\xi_i, \qquad \lambda_1 = e^{i\Phi}, \quad \lambda_2 = e^{-i\Phi}, \quad \lambda_3 = 1, \qquad \vec\xi_i^{\,\dagger}\cdot\vec\xi_j = \delta_{ij}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 2" />

with $\Phi \neq 0$. The third eigenvector is real, $\vec\xi_3^{\,*} = \vec\xi_3$; the other two are complex and conjugate to each other, $\vec\xi_1^{\,*} = \vec\xi_2$. The matrix $X$ with columns $\vec\xi_1$, $\vec\xi_2$, $\vec\xi_3$ is unitary, $X^\dagger = X^{-1}$, and it diagonalizes $U$:

<Formula label="Problem 1: X diagonalizes U" tex="X^\dagger U X = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \lambda_3 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 2" />

(compare eq. (2.15) of the notes). Since $U\vec\xi_3 = \vec\xi_3$, the axis is along the real vector $\vec\xi_3$, and we take it as the new $z$ axis.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

The obvious choice for the other two axes would be $\vec\xi_1$ and $\vec\xi_2$: with $\vec r = X\vec s$, the vector $\vec s$ holds the components of $\vec r$ along $\vec\xi_1$, $\vec\xi_2$, $\vec\xi_3$. But $\vec\xi_1$ and $\vec\xi_2$ are complex, so they cannot be axes in real space. Find two real vectors $\vec\xi_a$ and $\vec\xi_b$, linear combinations of $\vec\xi_1$ and $\vec\xi_2$, such that $\{\vec\xi_a, \vec\xi_b, \vec\xi_3\}$ is an orthonormal set.

<Hint>

Use $\vec\xi_2 = \vec\xi_1^{\,*}$. Which combinations of a complex vector and its conjugate are real?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The sum $\vec\xi_1 + \vec\xi_2$ is real. The difference $\vec\xi_1 - \vec\xi_2$ is purely imaginary, so multiply it by $i$. Then fix the lengths with $\vec\xi_i^{\,\dagger}\cdot\vec\xi_j = \delta_{ij}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1a-pair" label="A real orthonormal pair is:" choices={["(ξ₁ + ξ₂)/2 and i(ξ₁ − ξ₂)/2", "i(ξ₁ + ξ₂)/√2 and (ξ₁ − ξ₂)/√2", "(ξ₁ + ξ₂)/√2 and i(ξ₁ − ξ₂)/√2", "(ξ₁ + ξ₂)/√2 and (ξ₁ − ξ₂)/√2"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A complex vector plus its conjugate is twice its real part; minus its conjugate is $2i$ times its imaginary part. Both are real once the second is multiplied by $i$.

<Step title="Two real vectors">

Take

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the real axes" tex="\vec\xi_a = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\left(\vec\xi_1 + \vec\xi_2\right), \qquad \vec\xi_b = \frac{i}{\sqrt 2}\left(\vec\xi_1 - \vec\xi_2\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

With $\vec\xi_2 = \vec\xi_1^{\,*}$: $\vec\xi_a^{\,*} = (\vec\xi_2 + \vec\xi_1)/\sqrt2 = \vec\xi_a$ and $\vec\xi_b^{\,*} = -i(\vec\xi_2 - \vec\xi_1)/\sqrt2 = \vec\xi_b$. Both are real: $\vec\xi_a = \sqrt2\,\mathrm{Re}\,\vec\xi_1$ and $\vec\xi_b = -\sqrt2\,\mathrm{Im}\,\vec\xi_1$. The overall sign of $\vec\xi_b$ is our choice; the other sign also works and turns the rotation of part (c) into one by $-\Phi$.

</Step>

<Step title="Orthonormal">

Using $\vec\xi_i^{\,\dagger}\cdot\vec\xi_j = \delta_{ij}$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the new axes are orthonormal" tex="\vec\xi_a^{\,\dagger}\cdot\vec\xi_a = \tfrac12(1 + 1) = 1, \qquad \vec\xi_b^{\,\dagger}\cdot\vec\xi_b = \tfrac{(-i)(i)}{2}(1 + 1) = 1, \qquad \vec\xi_a^{\,\dagger}\cdot\vec\xi_b = \tfrac{i}{2}(1 - 1) = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Both are combinations of $\vec\xi_1$ and $\vec\xi_2$, which are orthogonal to $\vec\xi_3$, so they are orthogonal to $\vec\xi_3$ too. For real vectors the dagger is just the transpose, so these are ordinary dot products.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Without the factor $i$ the second vector, $(\vec\xi_1 - \vec\xi_2)/\sqrt2 = \sqrt2\,i\,\mathrm{Im}\,\vec\xi_1$, would be purely imaginary. With $1/2$ in place of $1/\sqrt2$ the vectors would have length $1/\sqrt2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Write the relation between the components $\vec u$ of a vector in the basis $\{\vec\xi_a, \vec\xi_b, \vec\xi_3\}$ and its components $\vec s$ in the basis $\{\vec\xi_1, \vec\xi_2, \vec\xi_3\}$ as a matrix, $\vec u = W\vec s$, and show that $W$ is unitary.

<Hint>

Write $\vec r = s_1\vec\xi_1 + s_2\vec\xi_2 + s_3\vec\xi_3 = u_a\vec\xi_a + u_b\vec\xi_b + u_3\vec\xi_3$, put in part (a), and compare the coefficients of $\vec\xi_1$ and $\vec\xi_2$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Express the new basis in the old one, then solve for the new components.

<Step title="The matrix">

Comparing coefficients gives $s_1 = (u_a + i u_b)/\sqrt2$ and $s_2 = (u_a - i u_b)/\sqrt2$, $s_3 = u_3$. Solving for $\vec u$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): the change of components" tex="\vec u = W\vec s, \qquad W = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ -i & i & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \sqrt 2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Unitary">

Multiply $W$ by $W^\dagger$ (transpose, then conjugate, so the $-i$ and $i$ swap sign). The first row with itself gives $\frac12(1 + 1) = 1$, the second row with itself $\frac12\left((-i)(i) + (i)(-i)\right) = 1$, the first with the second $\frac12\left(1\cdot i + 1\cdot(-i)\right) = 0$, and the third row stands apart:

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): W is unitary" tex="W W^\dagger = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ -i & i & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \sqrt 2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & i & 0 \\ 1 & -i & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \sqrt 2 \end{pmatrix} = 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A unitary matrix takes one orthonormal basis to another, as it must here: both bases are orthonormal. Its determinant, $\frac12(i + i) = i$, has modulus 1.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

The new coordinates are $\vec u = W\vec s = WX^\dagger\vec r$. Write the rotation $\vec r\,' = U\vec r$ as $\vec u\,' = \tilde U\vec u$, and show that $\tilde U$ is a standard rotation matrix with rotation angle $\Phi$. The sheet ends with a remark: the trace is the same in every basis, $\mathrm{Tr}\,U = \mathrm{Tr}\,\tilde U = 1 + 2\cos\Phi$, so the angle of any rotation matrix can be read off its trace.

Then use that remark on a matrix of our own (our addition). The matrix below sends the components $(x, y, z)$ to $(z, x, y)$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): a matrix of our own" tex="P = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 2 (our own example)" />

Find its rotation angle between $0°$ and $180°$, and its axis.

<Hint>

$\vec r = X\vec s$ and $\vec s = W^\dagger\vec u$, so $\vec r = XW^\dagger\vec u$. Put that into $\vec u\,' = WX^\dagger\vec r\,'$ with $\vec r\,' = U\vec r$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The middle of the product, $X^\dagger U X$, is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues. Only the upper-left $2\times2$ block needs work: write $e^{\pm i\Phi} = \cos\Phi \pm i\sin\Phi$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $P$: its trace gives $\cos\Phi$. The axis is the vector that $P$ leaves unchanged.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1c-angle" label="Rotation angle of $P$:" value="120" unit="degrees" tolerance="1%" calc="p1c-angle" />

<Answer id="p1c-axis" label="Axis of $P$:" choices={["along (1, 1, 1)", "along (0, 0, 1)", "along (1, −1, 0)", "along (1, 0, −1)"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the new coordinates the rotation is $\tilde U = W(X^\dagger U X)W^\dagger$, and $W$ turns the two complex phases into a cosine and a sine.

<Step title="The rotation in the new coordinates">

With $\vec r = XW^\dagger\vec u$,

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): the rotation in the real coordinates" tex="\vec u\,' = WX^\dagger U\vec r = WX^\dagger U X W^\dagger\,\vec u \equiv \tilde U\vec u, \qquad \tilde U = W\,\mathrm{diag}\!\left(e^{i\Phi}, e^{-i\Phi}, 1\right)W^\dagger" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Multiply out">

The upper-left entry is $\frac12\left(e^{i\Phi} + e^{-i\Phi}\right) = \cos\Phi$. The entry in row 1, column 2 is $\frac12\left(e^{i\Phi}\cdot i + e^{-i\Phi}\cdot(-i)\right) = \frac{i}{2}\cdot 2i\sin\Phi = -\sin\Phi$; the entry in row 2, column 1 is $+\sin\Phi$, and the entry in row 2, column 2 is $\cos\Phi$. So

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): a standard rotation" tex="\tilde U = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\Phi & -\sin\Phi & 0 \\ \sin\Phi & \cos\Phi & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

a rotation by the angle $\Phi$ about the third axis, $\vec\xi_3$. It is real, as it must be: $\vec u$ holds the components of a real vector along real axes. With the other sign of $\vec\xi_b$ the sines change sign, a rotation by $-\Phi$, which is the same rotation seen from the other end of the axis.

</Step>

<Step title="The matrix P">

$\mathrm{Tr}\,P = 0 = 1 + 2\cos\Phi$, so $\cos\Phi = -\frac12$ and $\Phi = 120°$. $P$ leaves $(1, 1, 1)$ unchanged, so the axis is along $(1, 1, 1)$. Seen down that axis, the three coordinate axes are $120°$ apart, and $P$ moves each into the next.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The trace of $\tilde U$ is $2\cos\Phi + 1$, the trace of the diagonal form, as the sheet's remark says. Its determinant is $\cos^2\Phi + \sin^2\Phi = 1$. For $P$, three turns give the identity, $P^3 = 1$, and three turns of $120°$ make $360°$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Foucault pendulum and the Coriolis effect" points={13} mit="Problem 2">

A mass $m$ hangs from a long massless rod of length $\ell$ in a tower at latitude $\lambda$. Its motion is described in axes fixed to the Earth, which turns with angular velocity $\omega$. The sheet's figure sets up the axes: $\hat z$ is at right angles to the Earth's surface (up), $\hat y$ points north along the meridian, and so $\hat x = \hat y\times\hat z$ points east. The origin is at the pivot. The bob is at

<Formula label="Problem 2: the position of the bob" tex="\vec r = \ell\left(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\ \sin\theta\sin\phi,\ -\cos\theta\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 3 (our choice of origin and of the sense of φ)" />

The sheet's figure marks $\theta$ at the pivot, between the rod and the vertical below it, as we do; our choice is to put the origin of the axes at the pivot. We count $\phi$ from $\hat x$ (east) towards $\hat y$ (north), anticlockwise seen from above (our choice; the sheet's figure counts it from $\hat y$ towards the plane of swing, clockwise seen from above). The Earth's angular velocity has a northward and an upward part: $\vec\omega = \omega(0, \cos\lambda, \sin\lambda)$.

<Part label="a" points={9}>

The velocity seen from an inertial frame is $\vec v + \vec\omega\times(R_e\hat z + \vec r)$, where $\vec v$ is the velocity in the Earth's frame and $R_e$ the Earth's radius. So

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the Lagrangian in the Earth's frame" tex="L = \frac{m}{2}\left[\vec v + \vec\omega\times\left(\vec r + R_e\hat z\right)\right]^2 - V" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 3" />

with $V$ the potential energy of gravity near the surface; air resistance is neglected. This is the Lagrangian of eq. (2.25) of the notes, with the origin moved to the Earth's surface. Write everything in terms of $\theta$, $\phi$ and the fixed angle $\lambda$, and derive the equations of motion of the pendulum. Keep only terms up to first order in $\omega$ from the start. The term proportional to $\omega R_e$ may be dropped: it is a total time derivative.

<Hint>

To first order in $\omega$, $L = \frac{m}{2}\vec v^{\,2} + m\,\vec v\cdot(\vec\omega\times\vec r) - V$, plus the $\omega R_e$ term. Rewrite the middle term as $m\,\vec\omega\cdot(\vec r\times\vec v)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\vec v^{\,2} = \ell^2(\dot\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2)$ and $V = -mg\ell\cos\theta$. For $\vec r\times\vec v$ you need its $y$ and $z$ components only, since $\omega_x = 0$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Expand the square, keep the term linear in $\omega$, and write it as $m\,\vec\omega\cdot(\vec r\times\vec v)$: the rotation couples to the angular momentum of the bob about the pivot.

<Step title="The Lagrangian to first order">

The term $m R_e\,\vec v\cdot(\vec\omega\times\hat z) = mR_e\omega\cos\lambda\,\dot x$ is the time derivative of $mR_e\omega\cos\lambda\,x$, so it changes no equation of motion. The terms quadratic in $\omega$ (the centrifugal ones) are dropped. With $\vec r\times\vec v$ worked out from the position of the bob,

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the Lagrangian in θ and φ" tex="L = \frac{m\ell^2}{2}\left(\dot\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2\right) + m\ell^2\omega\left[\sin\lambda\,\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi + \cos\lambda\left(\sin\theta\cos\theta\sin\phi\,\dot\phi - \cos\phi\,\dot\theta\right)\right] + mg\ell\cos\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The equation for θ">

Lagrange's equation for $\theta$, divided by $m\ell^2$:

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the θ equation" tex="\ddot\theta - \sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2 + \frac{g}{\ell}\sin\theta - 2\omega\sin\theta\left(\sin\lambda\cos\theta - \cos\lambda\sin\theta\sin\phi\right)\dot\phi = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The equation for φ">

Lagrange's equation for $\phi$, divided by $m\ell^2$:

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the φ equation" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi\right) + 2\omega\sin\theta\left(\sin\lambda\cos\theta - \cos\lambda\sin\theta\sin\phi\right)\dot\theta = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With $\omega = 0$ these are the equations of a spherical pendulum. The bracket $\sin\lambda\cos\theta - \cos\lambda\sin\theta\sin\phi$ is $-\hat\omega\cdot\hat r$: the component of the Earth's rotation along the rod, from the bob up to the pivot. That is what one expects. The velocity $\vec v$ is at right angles to the rod. The part of $\vec\omega$ at right angles to the rod gives a Coriolis force $-2m\vec\omega\times\vec v$ along the rod, which the rod takes up; only the part of $\vec\omega$ along the rod pushes the bob sideways. We checked both equations with a computer algebra program while writing this page.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Since $\ell$ is large, the swing is small. Use the small-angle approximation for $\theta$ to simplify the equations of part (a), and show that the plane of swing turns at the rate $\omega\sin\lambda$. (The sheet writes $\dot\phi = \omega\sin\lambda$ with its $\phi$ counted clockwise; with ours the sign is minus.)

Then two boxes of our own. Take $\omega = 7.29\times 10^{-5}$ s⁻¹ (one turn of the Earth in a sidereal day) and the latitude of Paris, $\lambda = 48.85°$.

<Hint>

At small $\theta$, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ and $\cos\theta \approx 1$. The terms with $\cos\lambda$ carry one more power of $\theta$ than those with $\sin\lambda$: drop them.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The $\phi$ equation becomes a total time derivative. Which quantity is conserved, and what is it for a pendulum that swings through the vertical?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2b-sense" label="Seen from above in the northern hemisphere, the plane of swing turns:" choices={["anticlockwise, with the Earth", "it does not turn at small angles", "clockwise, against the Earth's turning", "anticlockwise at first, then clockwise"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="p2b-time" label="Time for the plane of swing to turn through a full $360°$ at Paris:" value="31.8" unit="hours" tolerance="1%" calc="p2b-time" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At small angles only the vertical part of $\vec\omega$ matters, and the $\phi$ equation says that a combination of $\theta$ and $\dot\phi$ is conserved. For a swing through the vertical that combination is zero, which fixes $\dot\phi$.

<Step title="Small angles">

With $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, $\cos\theta \approx 1$ and the $\cos\lambda$ terms dropped (they are smaller by a factor $\theta$):

<Formula label="Problem 2(b): the small-angle equations" tex="\ddot\theta - \theta\,\dot\phi^2 + \frac{g}{\ell}\,\theta - 2\omega\sin\lambda\,\theta\,\dot\phi = 0, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left[\theta^2\left(\dot\phi + \omega\sin\lambda\right)\right] = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The precession">

So $\theta^2(\dot\phi + \omega\sin\lambda)$ is constant. A pendulum started from the vertical with a sideways push passes through $\theta = 0$, so the constant is zero. One released from rest has the small constant $\theta_0^2\,\omega\sin\lambda$: its path is a very thin ellipse, and its plane turns at the same rate. With the constant zero:

<Formula label="Problem 2(b): the precession of the plane of swing" tex="\dot\phi = -\,\omega\sin\lambda" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 3 (our own solution, with our φ)" />

Put this into the $\theta$ equation: $\dot\phi^2 + 2\omega\sin\lambda\,\dot\phi = -\omega^2\sin^2\lambda$, of second order in $\omega$, so $\ddot\theta + (g/\ell)\theta = 0$. The bob swings as an ordinary pendulum in a plane that turns at the rate $\omega\sin\lambda$ (here $\theta$ is allowed to change sign as the bob passes the vertical, so that $\phi$ labels the plane).

</Step>

<Step title="The sign">

The sheet's figure marks $\phi$ from $\hat y$ (north) towards the plane of swing, clockwise seen from above; in that angle the motion reads $\dot\phi = +\omega\sin\lambda$, as the sheet and the notes (Section 2.2, p. 35) write. With our $\phi$ it is $-\omega\sin\lambda$. Both say clockwise in the north.

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

A full turn of the plane takes $2\pi/(\omega\sin\lambda)$. At Paris, $\sin 48.85° = 0.7530$ and $2\pi/\omega = 23.94$ h, so the time is $23.94/0.7530 = 31.8$ hours.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At the north pole, $\lambda = 90°$, the plane stays fixed in space while the Earth turns under it once a day, and $\omega\sin\lambda = \omega$. At the equator the vertical part of $\vec\omega$ is zero and the plane does not turn. In the Earth's frame the Coriolis force pushes a moving body to its right in the northern hemisphere, so each swing is bent to the right: clockwise seen from above, as found.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Angular velocity with Euler angles" points={9} mit="Problem 3">

The Euler angles $\phi$, $\theta$, $\psi$ give the orientation of the body axes $(x', y', z')$ relative to the fixed axes $(x, y, z)$, with $\vec r\,' = U\vec r$ and $U = U_\psi U_\theta U_\phi$ (eq. (2.5) of the notes):

<Formula label="Problem 5(a): the inertia tensor about the tip" tex="I^{\text{tip}} = \begin{pmatrix} I_\perp & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & I_\parallel & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & I_\perp \end{pmatrix}, \qquad I_\parallel = \frac{3}{10}Mh^2\tan^2\alpha, \qquad I_\perp = \frac{3}{20}Mh^2\left(4 + \tan^2\alpha\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

The axes are principal axes by symmetry: the cone looks the same after any turn about $y'$, so every axis at right angles to $y'$ through the tip has the same moment, and no products of inertia survive.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With the radius of the base, $h\tan\alpha$, $I_\parallel = \frac{3}{10}M(h\tan\alpha)^2$: less than the $\frac12 M(h\tan\alpha)^2$ of a cylinder of the same mass and radius, since the cone's mass is closer to its axis. In our calculation file each moment is also found by a numerical integral over the slices.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

What is the inertia tensor if the axes of part (a) are moved, without turning, so that their origin is at the CM? Type the moment about an axis through the CM at right angles to the cone's axis.

<Hint>

Use the parallel axis theorem from the tip to the CM, a distance $\frac34 h$ along $y'$. Which components does it change?

<Answer id="p5b-perp" label="$I^{\text{CM}}_{x'x'} = I^{\text{CM}}_{z'z'} =$" expr="3/80*M*h^2*(1+4*tan(alpha)^2)"

The tensor stays diagonal, with $I^{\text{CM}}_\perp$ in the $x'x'$ and $z'z'$ places and $I^{\text{CM}}_\parallel$ in the $y'y'$ place.

$I^{\text{CM}}_\perp$ is positive for every $\alpha$, as a moment of inertia must be. It is the smallest moment across the cone of all parallel axes, since the CM is where the parallel axis term vanishes.

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Now the cone rolls on the fixed plane. Take new axes $(x, y, z)$ with the origin at the tip, $z$ at right angles to the plane, and $y$ along the line of contact at that instant. The sheet calls them body axes, but they turn with the line of contact, not with the cone; but the cone is symmetric about its axis, so its tensor in them does not change in time. Our choice: $z$ points up, away from the plane, and $y$ points from the tip along the line of contact towards the base. Find the inertia tensor in these axes. Give $I_{yy}$ and $I_{yz}$.

<Hint>

Where is the cone's axis in the new axes? It lies in the $yz$-plane, at the angle $\alpha$ above the line of contact.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For a body symmetric about a unit vector $\hat n$, the tensor about a point on that axis is $I_{ab} = I_\perp\delta_{ab} + (I_\parallel - I_\perp)\,n_an_b$.

<Answer id="p5c-yy" label="$I_{yy} =$" expr="3/20*M*h^2*sin(alpha)^2*(6+tan(alpha)^2)"

  mistakes={[

<Solution>

**The idea.** The new axes are the axes of part (a) turned by $\alpha$ about $x$, so the tensor follows from $I_\perp\delta_{ab} + (I_\parallel - I_\perp)n_an_b$ with the cone's axis $\hat n$ written in the new axes.

<Step title="The cone's axis">

The line of contact is a line on the cone's side, at the angle $\alpha$ from its axis. So, with $z$ up and $y$ towards the base, the cone's axis points along $\hat n = (0, \cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)$.

</Step>

<Step title="The tensor">

<Formula label="Problem 5(c): the inertia tensor in the rolling axes" tex="I = \begin{pmatrix} I_\perp & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & I_\perp\sin^2\alpha + I_\parallel\cos^2\alpha & (I_\parallel - I_\perp)\sin\alpha\cos\alpha \\ 0 & (I_\parallel - I_\perp)\sin\alpha\cos\alpha & I_\perp\cos^2\alpha + I_\parallel\sin^2\alpha \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

With the moments of part (a):

<Formula label="Problem 5(c): the components" tex="I_{yy} = \frac{3}{20}Mh^2\sin^2\alpha\left(6 + \tan^2\alpha\right), \qquad I_{yz} = \frac{3}{20}Mh^2\left(\tan^2\alpha - 4\right)\sin\alpha\cos\alpha, \qquad I_{xx} = I_\perp" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

This is the rule $I' = UIU^T$ of the notes (eq. (2.55)) for a turn by $\alpha$ about the $x$ axis.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The trace does not change under a turn: $I_{xx} + I_{yy} + I_{zz} = 2I_\perp + I_\parallel$, the trace of part (a). For the cones with $\tan\alpha < 2$, that is $\alpha < 63.4°$, $I_\parallel < I_\perp$ and $I_{yz}$ is negative.

</Step>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

Find the kinetic energy of the rolling cone, in terms of `M`, `h`, `alpha` and `tau`. There are two ways: one uses part (b), the other part (c). The first two boxes are our additions.

<Hint>

Rolling without slipping: which points of the cone are at rest at this instant? The angular velocity is along the line through them.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The CM goes round a horizontal circle about the vertical through the tip, once in the time $\tau$. Its speed can be written in two ways: from that circle, and as $|\vec\omega\times\vec r_{\text{CM}}|$. Equate them to find $|\vec\omega|$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5d-axis" label="At each instant the cone turns about:" choices={["the vertical line through the tip", "the line of contact with the plane", "the cone's own axis", "the line from the tip through the CM"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p5d-omega" label="Magnitude of the cone's angular velocity, $|\vec\omega| =$" expr="2*pi/(tau*tan(alpha))"
  vars={{ tau: [1, 5], alpha: [0.2, 0.6] }} names={{ tau: "\\tau", alpha: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*pi/tau", hint: "2π/τ is the rate at which the line of contact goes round the vertical. The cone itself turns about the line of contact, faster: equate the two expressions for the speed of the centre of mass." },
    { expr: "2*pi*tan(alpha)/tau", hint: "The ratio is the other way up: the speed of the CM is |ω| (3h/4) sin α from the turning about the line of contact, and (2π/τ)(3h/4) cos α from its circle." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p5d-t" label="$T =$" expr="3*pi^2/10*M*h^2*(1+5*cos(alpha)^2)/tau^2"
  vars={{ M: [1, 3], h: [0.5, 2], alpha: [0.2, 0.6], tau: [1, 5] }} names={{ tau: "\\tau", alpha: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "9*pi^2/8*M*h^2*cos(alpha)^2/tau^2", hint: "That is only the kinetic energy of the motion of the centre of mass. The cone also turns about its centre of mass." },
    { expr: "3*pi^2/10*M*h^2*sin(alpha)^2*(6+tan(alpha)^2)/tau^2", hint: "The cone turns about the line of contact at |ω| = (2π/τ) cot α, not at 2π/τ, the rate at which the line of contact goes round." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The tip is fixed and the points on the line of contact are at rest, so the cone turns about the line of contact: $\vec\omega$ lies along the $y$ axis of part (c). The CM's circle fixes $|\vec\omega|$, and then $T = \frac12 I_{yy}\omega^2$.

<Step title="The angular velocity">

Write $\Omega = 2\pi/\tau$ for the rate at which the line of contact goes round. The CM is at $\frac34 h\,\hat n$, a height $\frac34 h\sin\alpha$ above the plane and a distance $\frac34 h\cos\alpha$ from the vertical through the tip. Its speed is $\Omega\cdot\frac34 h\cos\alpha$ from its circle and $\omega\cdot\frac34 h\sin\alpha$ from the turning about the line of contact. So

<Formula label="Problem 5(d): the angular speed" tex="\omega = \Omega\cot\alpha = \frac{2\pi}{\tau\tan\alpha}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="From part (c)">

With $\vec\omega = \omega\hat y$, only $I_{yy}$ enters:

<Formula label="Problem 5(d): the kinetic energy" tex="T = \frac{1}{2}I_{yy}\,\omega^2 = \frac{3}{40}Mh^2\sin^2\alpha\left(6 + \tan^2\alpha\right)\Omega^2\cot^2\alpha = \frac{3}{40}Mh^2\Omega^2\left(1 + 5\cos^2\alpha\right) = \frac{3\pi^2Mh^2}{10\,\tau^2}\left(1 + 5\cos^2\alpha\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

using $\sin^2\alpha\left(6 + \tan^2\alpha\right)\cot^2\alpha = 6\cos^2\alpha + \sin^2\alpha = 1 + 5\cos^2\alpha$. In terms of the density, $T = \frac{\pi^3\rho h^5}{10\,\tau^2}\tan^2\alpha\left(1 + 5\cos^2\alpha\right)$.

</Step>

<Step title="From part (b)">

The CM moves with speed $v = \Omega\cdot\frac34 h\cos\alpha$. Relative to the CM, $\vec\omega$ has the component $\omega\cos\alpha$ along the cone's axis and $\omega\sin\alpha$ across it, so

<Formula label="Problem 5(d): the kinetic energy split at the CM" tex="T = \frac{1}{2}Mv^2 + \frac{1}{2}\left(I^{\text{CM}}_\parallel\omega^2\cos^2\alpha + I^{\text{CM}}_\perp\omega^2\sin^2\alpha\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

Putting in $v$, $\omega = \Omega\cot\alpha$ and the moments of part (b) gives the same result; our calculation file compares the two routes.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$T$ is an energy: $Mh^2/\tau^2$ has units kg m² s⁻². At $\alpha = 45°$ the two routes are easy to compare by hand. From part (c), $\frac{3}{40}\left(1 + \frac52\right) = \frac{21}{80}$ in units of $Mh^2\Omega^2$. From part (b), with $\omega = \Omega$, the motion of the CM gives $\frac12\cdot\frac{9}{16}\cdot\frac12 = \frac{9}{64}$ and the turning about the CM $\frac12\cdot\frac12\left(\frac{3}{10} + \frac{3}{16}\right) = \frac{39}{320}$; the sum is $\frac{45 + 39}{320} = \frac{21}{80}$.

</Step>
