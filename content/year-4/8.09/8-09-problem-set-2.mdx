## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A spherical pendulum with friction" points={8} mit="Problem 1">

A small sphere of mass $m$ hangs from a fixed point on a massless rod of length $\ell$. It can move in three dimensions, as far as the rod allows: a spherical pendulum. Gravity acts on it, and it moves slowly through a viscous fluid, so the drag on it obeys Stokes' law, a force opposite to its velocity and proportional to its speed.

Our choices (listed in the introduction): use the angle $\theta$ of the rod from the downward vertical and the azimuth $\phi$ about the vertical; treat the sphere as a point mass, and let the drag act on it alone, with radius $a$ and fluid viscosity $\eta$, so that Stokes' law reads

<Formula label="Problem 1: Stokes' law for the sphere" tex="\vec F = -6\pi\eta a\,\vec v" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.4, p. 21" />

and leave out buoyancy. You do not need Lagrange multipliers here: the two angles satisfy the constraint of the rod automatically.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

What is the dissipation function $\mathcal{F}$ of the pendulum?

The box is our addition. Type `eta`, `a`, `l`, `th`, `thd` and `phd`.

<Hint>

First write the speed of the sphere in terms of $\theta$, $\dot\theta$ and $\dot\phi$. On what circle does it move when only $\phi$ changes?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For a friction force of size $h(v)$, $\mathcal{F} = \int_0^v h(v')\,dv'$. Here $h$ is linear in $v$.

</Hint>

  vars={{ eta: [0.5, 2], a: [0.01, 0.05], l: [0.5, 2], th: [0.3, 1.2], thd: [0.2, 1.5], phd: [0.5, 2] }} names={{ eta: "\\eta", th: "\\theta", thd: "\\dot\\theta", phd: "\\dot\\phi", l: "\\ell" }}

    { expr: "3*pi*eta*a*l^2*(thd^2 + phd^2)", hint: "When only φ changes, the sphere moves on a horizontal circle of radius ℓ sin θ, so that part of the speed is ℓ sin θ φ̇, not ℓ φ̇." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a drag linear in the speed, $\mathcal{F} = \frac12\cdot 6\pi\eta a\,v^2$: all that is needed is $v^2$ in the two angles.

<Step title="The speed">

The sphere is at $\ell(\sin\theta\cos\phi, \sin\theta\sin\phi, -\cos\theta)$. A change of $\theta$ moves it along a great circle of radius $\ell$, a change of $\phi$ along a horizontal circle of radius $\ell\sin\theta$, and the two directions are at right angles, so $v^2 = \ell^2\left(\dot\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2\right)$.

</Step>

<Step title="The dissipation function">

With $h(v) = 6\pi\eta a\,v$, $\mathcal{F} = \int_0^v 6\pi\eta a\,v'\,dv' = 3\pi\eta a\,v^2$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the dissipation function" tex="\mathcal{F} = 3\pi\eta a\,\ell^2\left(\dot\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\mathcal{F}$ has the units of a force times a speed, a power: in fact, as part (b) shows, the energy is lost at the rate $2\mathcal{F}$. Its $\dot\theta$ derivative, divided by $\ell$, is $6\pi\eta a\,\ell\dot\theta$, Stokes' drag for the speed $\ell\dot\theta$, as it should be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the equations of motion, with the effect of the viscous friction.

In the second, give the angular momentum about the vertical, $L_z = m\ell^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi$, at time $t$; type `Lz0` for its value at $t = 0$, and `eta`, `a`, `m` and `t`. In the third, choose the rate at which the energy $E = T + V$ changes.

<Hint>

Write $T$ and $V$ in the two angles, and put $L = T - V$ and $\mathcal{F}$ into the Euler–Lagrange equations with friction. Do not forget that $T$ depends on $\theta$ as well as on the velocities.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The $\phi$ equation has the form $\frac{d}{dt}p_\phi = -(\ldots)\,\dot\phi$. Write its right side in terms of $p_\phi$ itself.

</Hint>

  vars={{ Lz0: [0.5, 2], eta: [0.5, 2], a: [0.01, 0.05], m: [0.1, 1], t: [0.5, 3] }} names={{ eta: "\\eta", Lz0: "L_{z0}" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Lz0*exp(-3*pi*eta*a*t/m)", hint: "The friction torque is −∂𝓕/∂φ̇ = −6πηa ℓ² sin²θ φ̇, which is (6πηa/m) L_z: the factor 2 from differentiating φ̇² stays." }

<Answer id="p1b-energy" label="The energy $E = T + V$ changes at the rate:" choices={["−𝓕, minus the dissipation function", "−2𝓕, minus twice the dissipation function", "−½𝓕, minus half the dissipation function", "zero, since the rod does no work on the sphere"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The angles take care of the rod, so the only thing to add to the frictionless pendulum is $-\partial\mathcal{F}/\partial\dot q_j$ on the right of each equation.

<Step title="The Lagrangian">

With $v^2$ from part (a) and the height $-\ell\cos\theta$ of the sphere:

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): the Lagrangian" tex="L = \frac{m\ell^2}{2}\left(\dot\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2\right) + mg\ell\cos\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The two equations">

For $\theta$: $\partial L/\partial\dot\theta = m\ell^2\dot\theta$, $\partial L/\partial\theta = m\ell^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2 - mg\ell\sin\theta$ and $\partial\mathcal{F}/\partial\dot\theta = 6\pi\eta a\,\ell^2\dot\theta$. For $\phi$: $\partial L/\partial\phi = 0$ and $\partial\mathcal{F}/\partial\dot\phi = 6\pi\eta a\,\ell^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi$. So

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): the equations of motion" tex="m\ell^2\ddot\theta - m\ell^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2 + mg\ell\sin\theta = -6\pi\eta a\,\ell^2\dot\theta, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left(m\ell^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi\right) = -6\pi\eta a\,\ell^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Dividing the first by $m\ell^2$: $\ddot\theta = \sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2 - \frac{g}{\ell}\sin\theta - \frac{6\pi\eta a}{m}\dot\theta$.

</Step>

<Step title="The angular momentum decays">

Without friction $\phi$ is cyclic and $p_\phi = m\ell^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi = L_z$ is conserved. With friction the right side of the $\phi$ equation is $-(6\pi\eta a/m)\,L_z$, whatever $\theta$ does, so

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): the decay of the angular momentum" tex="\frac{dL_z}{dt} = -\frac{6\pi\eta a}{m}\,L_z \quad\Rightarrow\quad L_z(t) = L_z(0)\,e^{-6\pi\eta a\,t/m}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The energy">

$E = T + V$ is the $H$ of this problem. Multiplying each equation by its $\dot q_j$ and adding gives $dE/dt = -\sum_j\dot q_j\,\partial\mathcal{F}/\partial\dot q_j$. $\mathcal{F}$ is quadratic in the velocities, so by Euler's theorem on homogeneous functions the sum is $2\mathcal{F}$: $dE/dt = -2\mathcal{F} = -6\pi\eta a\,v^2$, the drag force times the speed.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Without friction ($\eta = 0$) these are the equations of the spherical pendulum, with $L_z$ conserved. For motion in a plane ($\dot\phi = 0$) and small angles, $\ddot\theta + \frac{6\pi\eta a}{m}\dot\theta + \frac{g}{\ell}\theta = 0$, a damped oscillator. And the decay time $m/(6\pi\eta a)$ of $L_z$ is the time constant of the lesson's sphere settling to its terminal speed (with $m$ for the lesson's $m'$).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A bead spiralling on a helix" points={12} mit="Problem 2">

A bead of mass $m$ slides without friction on a wire bent into a helix whose radius grows with height. In cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$ the wire is

<Formula label="Problem 2: the helix" tex="r = bz, \qquad \theta = az" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

so it winds around the $z$ axis while widening, like a spring pulled into a cone. At $t = 0$ the bead is at height $z = h$ and moves upwards with $\dot z = v_0 > 0$. There is no gravity. Our choice: $a$, $b$ and $h$ are positive.

<Part label="a" points={8}>

By integrating the equation of motion, find $\dot z$ as a function of $z$. Then find the constraint torque $Z_\theta$ (the generalized force of constraint for the coordinate $\theta$) as a function of $a$, $b$, $m$, $z$, $v_0$ and $h$. The sheet's hint:

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the sheet's hint" tex="\int dt\,\frac{\ddot z}{\dot z} = \ln\dot z" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

The boxes are our additions. Type `a`, `b`, `m`, `z`, `v0` and `h`.

<Hint>

Keep all three coordinates and add a multiplier for each constraint, $r - bz = 0$ and $\theta - az = 0$. The $\theta$ equation then says what $Z_\theta$ is.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $\dot z(z)$, use the constraints to write $L$ in $z$ and $\dot z$ alone. The Euler–Lagrange equation can be divided by $\dot z$ times the coefficient of $\dot z^2$; both sides are then time derivatives of logarithms.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2a-zdot" label="$\dot z =$" expr="v0*sqrt((1 + b^2 + a^2*b^2*h^2)/(1 + b^2 + a^2*b^2*z^2))"

    { expr: "v0", hint: "The speed of the bead is constant, but not ż: as the helix widens, more of that speed goes into going round, so ż falls." }
  ]} />

  vars={{ a: [0.5, 2], b: [0.3, 1.5], m: [0.5, 2], z: [2, 4], h: [0.5, 1.5], v0: [0.5, 2] }} names={{ v0: "v_0" }}

  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Without gravity and with a fixed wire, the only force is the constraint force, which does no work; the equation for $z$ integrates once, and the $\theta$ equation gives the torque as the rate of change of $m r^2\dot\theta$.

<Step title="Coordinates, constraints and multipliers">

With $L = \frac m2\left(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2 + \dot z^2\right)$ and the constraints $f_1 = r - bz = 0$, $f_2 = \theta - az = 0$:

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the equations with multipliers" tex="m\ddot r - mr\dot\theta^2 = \lambda_1, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left(mr^2\dot\theta\right) = \lambda_2, \qquad m\ddot z = -b\lambda_1 - a\lambda_2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The generalized force of constraint for $\theta$ is $\lambda_2\,\partial f_2/\partial\theta = \lambda_2$, so $Z_\theta = \lambda_2 = \frac{d}{dt}\left(mr^2\dot\theta\right)$, the torque of the wire about the $z$ axis.

</Step>

<Step title="The equation for z">

On the wire, $\dot r = b\dot z$ and $\dot\theta = a\dot z$, so $L = \frac m2 D(z)\,\dot z^2$ with $D(z) = 1 + b^2 + a^2b^2z^2$. Its Euler–Lagrange equation is $D\ddot z + a^2b^2z\,\dot z^2 = 0$. Dividing by $D\dot z$:

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the equation of motion, integrated" tex="\frac{\ddot z}{\dot z} = -\frac{a^2b^2z\dot z}{1 + b^2 + a^2b^2z^2} = -\frac12\frac{d}{dt}\ln\left(1 + b^2 + a^2b^2z^2\right) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \dot z = v_0\sqrt{\frac{1 + b^2 + a^2b^2h^2}{1 + b^2 + a^2b^2z^2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

using the sheet's hint and the starting values $\dot z = v_0$ at $z = h$.

</Step>

<Step title="The torque">

$mr^2\dot\theta = mab^2z^2\dot z$, so $Z_\theta = mab^2\left(2z\dot z^2 + z^2\ddot z\right)$. With $\ddot z = -a^2b^2z\dot z^2/D$ from the equation of motion, $Z_\theta = mab^2z\dot z^2\left(2D - a^2b^2z^2\right)/D$, and with $\dot z^2$ from the previous step:

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the constraint torque" tex="Z_\theta = \frac{mab^2v_0^2\,z\left(1 + b^2 + a^2b^2h^2\right)\left(2 + 2b^2 + a^2b^2z^2\right)}{\left(1 + b^2 + a^2b^2z^2\right)^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

If the helix does not widen ($b = 0$), there is no torque and $\dot z = v_0$: the bead moves along the axis. The torque is positive for $z > 0$: the wire pushes the bead round faster as the radius grows. In our calculation file the torque is also found as $\dot z\,dL_z/dz$, with the derivative taken numerically.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Hint>

</Hint>

  vars={{ a: [0.5, 2], b: [0.3, 1.5], m: [0.5, 2], z: [2, 4], h: [0.5, 1.5], v0: [0.5, 2] }} names={{ v0: "v_0" }}

<Solution>

**The idea.** The energy is constant because the constraint is fixed in time; the angular momentum grows because the wire exerts a torque, which part (a) found to be positive.

<Step title="The energy">

The constraints $r - bz = 0$ and $\theta - az = 0$ do not contain $t$, so a real displacement of the bead is also a virtual one, and the constraint force does no work on it. There is no other force, so $E = T$ is constant. In the formulas: $T = \frac m2 D\dot z^2 = \frac m2 v_0^2\left(1 + b^2 + a^2b^2h^2\right)$ by part (a), the same at every $z$. (This is the conserved $H$ of the sheet's hint: $L$ has no explicit $t$.)

</Step>

<Step title="The angular momentum">

$L_z = mr^2\dot\theta = mab^2z^2\dot z$, and with $\dot z$ from part (a):

<Formula label="Problem 2(b): the angular momentum" tex="L_z = mab^2v_0\,z^2\sqrt{\frac{1 + b^2 + a^2b^2h^2}{1 + b^2 + a^2b^2z^2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Its $z$ derivative is $mab^2v_0\sqrt{1 + b^2 + a^2b^2h^2}\;z\left(2 + 2b^2 + a^2b^2z^2\right)/D^{3/2}$, positive for $z > 0$. Since $\dot z > 0$ at every time ($\dot z$ never reaches zero), $z$ keeps growing and so does $L_z$. Equivalently, $dL_z/dt = Z_\theta > 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Far up, where $a^2b^2z^2$ is much larger than $1 + b^2$, the bead goes almost round a circle of radius $r = bz$ at the constant speed $v = v_0\sqrt{1 + b^2 + a^2b^2h^2}$, so $L_z \approx mrv$ grows in proportion to $z$. ## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A rope of unlimited length over two pulleys" points={8} mit="Problem 4">

The sheet's problem is one piece; its split into three parts, and their points, are ours.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Show that the curve is the one that makes an integral of the form $\int f(y, y')\,dx$ a minimum. What is $f$?

The box is our addition.

<Hint>

In equilibrium the potential energy of the whole rope is a minimum. Which parts of it depend on the shape of the curve between the pulleys?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4a-free" label="No condition on the length of rope between the pulleys is needed, because:" choices={["the rope cannot stretch, so its length between the pulleys never changes", "the pulleys exert no force along the rope on the part between them", "rope can be drawn from or returned to the heaps, whose energy is zero, so the span's length is free", "the length between the pulleys is fixed by the two heights y₁ and y₂"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The parts beyond the pulleys have an energy that does not depend on the span, and rope in the heaps has zero energy, so only the span's energy is to be minimized, with its length free.

<Step title="The energy">

Take the ground as zero of potential energy. A vertical piece hanging from height $y_i$ to the ground has energy $\int_0^{y_i}\sigma g\,y\,dy = \frac12\sigma g\,y_i^2$, fixed. Rope in a heap is at height zero. So the total energy is $\frac12\sigma g\left(y_1^2 + y_2^2\right)$ plus the energy of the span:

<Formula label="Problem 4(a): the energy of the span" tex="U[y] = \sigma g\int y\,ds = \sigma g\int_{x_1}^{x_2} y\sqrt{1 + y'^2}\;dx" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

<Step title="A free length">

The pulleys turn freely, so rope can move from the heaps into the span or back. A curve with more or less rope between the pulleys is therefore an allowed shape, and the length needs no constraint (no Lagrange multiplier). The equilibrium curve is the one that makes $\int y\sqrt{1 + y'^2}\,dx$ a minimum, with $y(x_1) = y_1$ and $y(x_2) = y_2$: $f = y\sqrt{1 + y'^2}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The same integral, times $2\pi$, is the area of the surface made by turning the curve about the $x$ axis, so the rope solves the problem of the soap film stretched between two rings. With a rope of fixed length, by contrast, the length would enter as a constraint with its own multiplier.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Solve the Euler–Lagrange equation for the curve.

The box is our addition: give $y(x)$ with two constants, $c$ (the height of the lowest point of the curve, if it has one between the pulleys) and $x_0$; type `c`, `x` and `x0`.

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

  vars={{ c: [1, 3], x: [-2, 2], x0: [-1, 1] }} names={{ x0: "x_0" }} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The first integral replaces a second-order equation by a first-order one, which separates.

<Step title="The Euler–Lagrange equation and its first integral">

The equation is that of the lesson, with $x$ for the parameter $s$:

<Formula label="Problem 4(b): the Euler–Lagrange equation" tex="\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y'}\right) = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.78), p. 22" />

</Step>

**The idea.** While both contacts hold, the ladder has one degree of freedom, $\theta$, and the energy is conserved, since smooth contacts do no work.

<Step title="The energy">

With $x = \frac{\ell}{2}\cos\theta$ and $y = \frac{\ell}{2}\sin\theta$, $\dot x^2 + \dot y^2 = \frac{\ell^2}{4}\dot\theta^2$ and $T = \frac{M\ell^2}{6}\dot\theta^2$. Energy conservation from rest at $\theta_0$:

<Formula label="Problem 5(c): the energy" tex="\frac{M\ell^2}{6}\dot\theta^2 + \frac{Mg\ell}{2}\sin\theta = \frac{Mg\ell}{2}\sin\theta_0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \dot\theta^2 = \frac{3g}{\ell}\left(\sin\theta_0 - \sin\theta\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The time">

With $\dot\theta < 0$:

<Formula label="Problem 5(c): time as a function of angle" tex="t(\theta) = \sqrt{\frac{\ell}{3g}}\int_\theta^{\theta_0}\frac{d\theta'}{\sqrt{\sin\theta_0 - \sin\theta'}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The integrand is infinite at $\theta' = \theta_0$, but only as $1/\sqrt{\cos\theta_0\,(\theta_0 - \theta')}$, so the integral is finite. This holds until the ladder leaves the wall, part (d).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The derivative of $\frac12\dot\theta^2$ with respect to $\theta$ gives $\ddot\theta = -\frac{3g}{2\ell}\cos\theta$. The equations of part (b), with $\lambda_1 = M\ddot x$ and $\lambda_2 = M(\ddot y + g)$ put into the $\theta$ equation, give the same: the multipliers drop out and $\frac{M\ell^2}{3}\ddot\theta = -\frac{Mg\ell}{2}\cos\theta$, the equation of a rod pivoted at one end, as the parallel-axis check of part (a) suggests.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

At what angle does the ladder leave the wall? What is the height of its upper end at that angle?

The boxes are our additions. In the first, give the height; type `l` and `th0`. For the other boxes take $\ell = 3.0$ m, $\theta_0 = 60^\circ$ and $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$: give the angle in degrees, and the time from the release until the ladder leaves the wall in seconds (from the integral of part (c), evaluated numerically).

<Hint>

The ladder leaves the wall where $\lambda_1 = M\ddot x$ would become negative. Differentiate $x = \frac{\ell}{2}\cos\theta$ twice, and use $\ddot\theta$ and $\dot\theta^2$ from part (c).

<Solution>

**The idea.** The wall can only push, so the contact ends where the multiplier $\lambda_1$ reaches zero.

<Step title="The wall's push">

$\ddot x = -\frac{\ell}{2}\left(\sin\theta\,\ddot\theta + \cos\theta\,\dot\theta^2\right)$. With part (c):

<Formula label="Problem 5(d): the push of the wall" tex="\lambda_1 = M\ddot x = \frac{3Mg}{4}\cos\theta\left(3\sin\theta - 2\sin\theta_0\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

It starts at $\frac{3Mg}{4}\cos\theta_0\sin\theta_0 > 0$ and reaches zero at $\sin\theta = \frac23\sin\theta_0$. The upper end is then at the height $\ell\sin\theta = \frac23\,\ell\sin\theta_0$: the ladder leaves the wall when its top has fallen to two thirds of its starting height, whatever $\theta_0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Our numbers">

For $\theta_0 = 60^\circ$: $\sin\theta = \frac23\cdot 0.8660 = 0.5774$ and $\theta = 35.3^\circ$; the top is at $1.73$ m. The integral of part (c) from $35.26^\circ$ to $60^\circ$, evaluated numerically, gives $t = 0.563$ s.

</Step>

<Step title="The floor's push">

The same way, $\lambda_2 = M(\ddot y + g) = \frac{Mg}{4}\left(1 + 9\sin^2\theta - 6\sin\theta\sin\theta_0\right) = \frac{Mg}{4}\left[\left(3\sin\theta - \sin\theta_0\right)^2 + \cos^2\theta_0\right]$, which is positive: before the ladder leaves the wall, the floor always pushes.

</Step>

At the start the floor pushes with $\frac{Mg}{4}(1 + 3\sin^2\theta_0)$, less than the weight, because the centre of mass is accelerating downwards. A ladder released upright ($\theta_0 \to 90^\circ$) leaves the wall at $\sin\theta = \frac23$, $41.8^\circ$.

<Part label="e" points={3}>

What are the equations of motion after the ladder loses contact with the wall?

The box is our addition: give $\ddot\theta$ in terms of $\theta$ and $\dot\theta$; type `g`, `l`, `th` and `thd`.

<Hint>

Drop $\lambda_1$. The $x$ equation now has no force; for the others, use $f_2 = 0$ to write $\ddot y$ in terms of $\theta$, and eliminate $\lambda_2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5e-thdd" label="$\ddot\theta =$" expr="(3*sin(th)*cos(th)*thd^2 - 6*g/l*cos(th))/(1 + 3*cos(th)^2)"
  vars={{ g: [9, 10], l: [2, 4], th: [0.2, 0.6], thd: [-4, -2.2] }} names={{ l: "\\ell", th: "\\theta", thd: "\\dot\\theta" }}

<Solution>

**The idea.** With one contact left, the ladder has two degrees of freedom: the horizontal position of the centre of mass, which now moves freely, and $\theta$.

<Step title="The equations">

With only $f_2$:

<Formula label="Problem 5(e): the equations after the wall" tex="M\ddot x = 0, \qquad M\ddot y + Mg = \lambda_2, \qquad \frac{M\ell^2}{12}\ddot\theta = -\frac{\ell}{2}\cos\theta\,\lambda_2, \qquad y = \frac{\ell}{2}\sin\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(e), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

So $\dot x$ keeps the value it had when the ladder left the wall.

</Step>

<Step title="One equation for θ">

$\ddot y = \frac{\ell}{2}\left(\cos\theta\,\ddot\theta - \sin\theta\,\dot\theta^2\right)$. Putting $\lambda_2 = M(\ddot y + g)$ into the $\theta$ equation and dividing by $M\ell^2/12$:

<Formula label="Problem 5(e): the angular equation" tex="\left(1 + 3\cos^2\theta\right)\ddot\theta = 3\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\theta^2 - \frac{6g}{\ell}\cos\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(e), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At the moment of leaving, $\sin\theta = \frac23\sin\theta_0$ and $\dot\theta^2 = \frac{3g}{2\ell}\sin\theta$, and the right side divided by $1 + 3\cos^2\theta = 4 - 3\sin^2\theta$ is exactly $-\frac{3g}{2\ell}\cos\theta$: the angular acceleration does not jump, as it should not, since $\lambda_1$ was already zero. The energy $\frac M2\dot x^2 + \frac{M\ell^2}{8}\left(\cos^2\theta + \frac13\right)\dot\theta^2 + \frac{Mg\ell}{2}\sin\theta$ is constant along this equation.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="f" points={5}>

Show that the ladder never loses contact with the floor. (You may assume that it does not bounce when it lands, at $\theta = 0$.)

The box is our addition: give the floor's push $\lambda_2$ after the ladder has left the wall, in terms of $\theta$ and $\dot\theta$; type `M`, `g`, `l`, `th` and `thd`.

<Hint>

From the $\theta$ equation of part (e), $\lambda_2 = -\frac{M\ell}{6}\ddot\theta/\cos\theta$. When could it become negative?

</Hint>

<Hint>

You need $\dot\theta^2$ after leaving the wall: use the energy of part (e), with the value of $\dot x$ at the moment of leaving. Then check that $\frac{\ell}{2}\sin\theta\,\dot\theta^2$ stays below $g$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5f-floor" label="$\lambda_2 =$" expr="M*(g - l/2*sin(th)*thd^2)/(1 + 3*cos(th)^2)"
  vars={{ M: [1, 20], g: [9, 10], l: [2, 4], th: [0.2, 0.6], thd: [-2.2, -1.2] }} names={{ l: "\\ell", th: "\\theta", thd: "\\dot\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "M*(g - l/2*sin(th)*thd^2)", hint: "The floor's push is shared between holding up the centre of mass and turning the ladder: the factor 1/(1 + 3 cos²θ) comes from eliminating θ̈." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Before the ladder leaves the wall, part (d) showed $\lambda_2 > 0$. After, $\lambda_2$ has the sign of $g - \frac{\ell}{2}\sin\theta\,\dot\theta^2$, and energy conservation bounds $\dot\theta^2$.

<Step title="The floor's push">

From part (e), $\lambda_2 = -\frac{M\ell}{6}\ddot\theta/\cos\theta$, and with the angular equation:

<Formula label="Problem 5(f): the floor's push after the wall" tex="\lambda_2 = \frac{M\left(g - \frac{\ell}{2}\sin\theta\,\dot\theta^2\right)}{1 + 3\cos^2\theta}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(f), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The speed after the wall">

Write $s = \sin\theta$, $s_0 = \sin\theta_0$, and $s_1 = \frac23 s_0$ for the value at which the ladder left the wall. Then $\dot\theta_1^2 = \frac{3g}{2\ell}s_1$ and $\dot x_1 = -\frac{\ell}{2}s_1\dot\theta_1$, so $\dot x_1^2 = \frac{3g\ell}{8}s_1^3$. The energy of part (e), equal to $\frac{Mg\ell}{2}s_0$, gives

<Formula label="Problem 5(f): the angular speed after the wall" tex="\dot\theta^2 = \frac{3g}{\ell}\,\frac{4\left(s_0 - s\right) - \frac32 s_1^3}{1 + 3\cos^2\theta}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(f), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The bound">

$\lambda_2 > 0$ requires $\frac{\ell}{2}s\,\dot\theta^2 < g$, that is, $\frac{3s}{2}\left[4(s_0 - s) - \frac32 s_1^3\right] < 4 - 3s^2$. With $s_0 = \frac32 s_1$ this is $9ss_1 - 3s^2 - \frac94 ss_1^3 < 4$. After the wall, $0 \le s \le s_1$, and on that range $9ss_1 - 3s^2$ increases with $s$, so it is at most $6s_1^2$. Since $s_1 \le \frac23$, the left side is at most $6 \cdot \frac49 = \frac83 < 4$. So $\lambda_2 > 0$ all the way to the floor: the ladder never leaves it.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The margin is large: even the bound $\frac83$ is well below 4. In our numerical run for $\theta_0 = 60^\circ$ (optional part (g)), the floor's push never falls below $0.20\,Mg$.

</Step>
