Lecture 21 finishes the stars and then starts on black holes. It recaps the TOV equations of [Lecture 20](/courses/8-962/schwarzschild-birkhoff-tov), solves them for an idealised star, explains how real stars are modelled, and then asks what the Schwarzschild metric means when there is no star at all. As before, $c = 1$ except where SI numbers are given, and the signature is $(-,+,+,+)$.

## Recap and recipe

Inside the star, $ds^2 = -e^{2\Phi}dt^2 + dr^2/(1 - 2Gm(r)/r) + r^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)$, with $\rho(r)$ and $P(r)$ vanishing beyond the surface $r_\star$. The mass function, the potential and relativistic hydrostatic equilibrium (the TOV equations) govern the interior; outside, $e^{2\Phi} = 1 - 2GM/r$ with $M = m(r_\star)$. The integral for $m$ is not over proper volume, which would give a larger mass; the difference measures the binding (transcript pp. 1–2). To solve: choose an equation of state and a central density, integrate outwards until $P = 0$, which is the surface, and adjust the constant in $\Phi$ to match the exterior.

## The constant-density star

As an unrealistic but instructive case, take $\rho$ constant. Such matter cannot be compressed at all: it is infinitely stiff, with an infinite speed of sound, $c_s^2 = dP/d\rho \to \infty$, which causality forbids. Still, the mass function becomes trivial (pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Mass function of a uniform star" tex="m(r) = \frac{4\pi}{3}\rho\,r^3 \ \ (r \le r_\star), \qquad M = \frac{4\pi}{3}\rho\,r_\star^3" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3" />

[The transcript has the speed of sound as $dP/d\rho$; it is its square, $c_s^2 = dP/d\rho$.] Inserting this into hydrostatic equilibrium and cancelling the common factors leaves

<Formula label="TOV for constant density" tex="\frac{dP}{dr} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\,r\,\frac{(\rho + P)(\rho + 3P)}{1 - 8\pi G\rho r^2/3}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3" />

Here, in Hughes's words, "nature and analysis conspire and a miracle occurs": it can be integrated exactly. Splitting $1/[(\rho + P)(\rho + 3P)]$ into partial fractions gives, with $P_c$ the central pressure,

<Formula label="Pressure profile of a uniform star" tex="\frac{\rho + P}{\rho + 3P} = \frac{\rho + P_c}{\rho + 3P_c}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 8\pi G\rho r^2/3}}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3" />

(Hughes writes the solution out for $P(r)$; this equivalent form is easier to read.) The surface is where $P = 0$, where the left side equals 1, so for a chosen density the central pressure fixes the radius and vice versa: a one-parameter family. Using $8\pi G\rho r_\star^2/3 = 2GM/r_\star$, the surface condition reads $(\rho + P_c)/(\rho + 3P_c) = \sqrt{1 - 2GM/r_\star}$, so

<Formula label="Central pressure and radius" tex="P_c = \rho\,\frac{1 - \sqrt{1 - 2GM/r_\star}}{3\sqrt{1 - 2GM/r_\star} - 1}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 3–4" />

## Buchdahl's limit

The central pressure diverges when the denominator vanishes:

<Formula label="Maximum compactness" tex="3\sqrt{1 - \frac{2GM}{r_\star}} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{GM}{r_\star} = \frac{4}{9}, \qquad r_\star > \frac{9}{4}\,GM" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 4" />

So even the stiffest fluid imaginable, stiffer than physics allows, cannot be held up if it is more compact than that: the centre would need infinite pressure. Any real fluid does worse. Made precise, this is **Buchdahl's theorem**: no stable, static, spherical fluid configuration has an areal radius below $9GM/(4c^2)$ (with $c$ restored). It holds generally, under mild assumptions (an isotropic fluid whose density does not increase outwards, and a pressure that is nowhere singular); Hughes points to section 11.6 of Weinberg's textbook for the proof (pp. 4–5).

What if you built a star at, say, $10GM/(4c^2)$ and squeezed it? The word that matters is **stable**. The whole analysis assumed a static star and spacetime; squeezed past the limit, the star cannot stay static. It collapses, and the spacetime becomes something else, a topic for later lectures (p. 5).

## Real stars: cold equations of state

Real stars have $P$ depending on $\rho$, and in general on the entropy $s$ as well: the first law, with the $T\,ds$ term included, mixes them. For the stars where general relativity matters, above all **neutron stars**, the fluid is cold enough to drop it (pp. 5–6). "Cold" needs a scale. The fluid is made of fermions, so the scale is the **Fermi temperature**, the Fermi energy divided by Boltzmann's constant:

<Formula label="Fermi temperature" tex="T_F = \frac{E_F}{k_B}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 6" />

For neutron-star matter $T_F$ is around $10^{13}$ K, while observed neutron stars are at $10^6$ to $10^9$ K, a factor $10^4$ to $10^7$ colder. Even at a billion kelvin they are cold, so **cold equations of state**, $P(\rho)$ alone, apply. Nuclear physicists compute these from QCD and effective field theories; the result is rarely a formula, more often a table of pressure against density to be interpolated (pp. 6–7).

## Polytropes and the rest-mass density

For tests and teaching one uses a clean power law, a **polytrope**:

<Formula label="Polytrope" tex="P = K\rho_0^{\Gamma}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 7" />

with constants $K$ and $\Gamma$. Hughes stresses a trap here (important for the homework): $\rho_0$ is the **rest-mass density**, not the energy density $\rho$ in the TOV equations. Squeeze a bucket of nuclear fluid and its density rises for two reasons: the same particles occupy less volume, and the work done against the pressure adds energy. All energy gravitates, so the TOV equations need $\rho$, while nuclear physics naturally gives $P(\rho_0)$.

The conversion uses the first law, $dU = -P\,dV$, per particle of rest mass $m_\text{rest}$. Energy per particle is $m_\text{rest}\,\rho/\rho_0$ and volume per particle is $m_\text{rest}/\rho_0$, so

<Formula label="First law per particle" tex="d\!\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho_0}\right) = -P\,d\!\left(\frac{1}{\rho_0}\right) = K\rho_0^{\Gamma - 2}\,d\rho_0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 8" />

Integrating, $\rho/\rho_0 = K\rho_0^{\Gamma - 1}/(\Gamma - 1) + C$, that is, $\rho = C\rho_0 + P/(\Gamma - 1)$ (the constant multiplies $\rho_0$; Hughes says "plus a constant"). When the pressure vanishes the energy density must become the rest-mass density, so $C = 1$:

<Formula label="Energy density of a polytrope" tex="\rho = \rho_0 + \frac{P}{\Gamma - 1}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 8–9" />

[Hughes reaches this result but says in the lecture that he cannot reconstruct one intermediate step of his notes; the step above, substituting $P = K\rho_0^\Gamma$ before integrating, is ours.]

## Building a stellar model

The recipe for the problem set (pp. 9–10):

1. Given the equation of state, pick $\rho_0$ at $r = 0$; it gives $\rho$ and $P$ there. Set $m(0) = 0$ (with some care at the centre).
2. Integrate the equations for $P$ and $m$ outwards numerically; analytic solutions, Hughes notes, tend to be the unphysical ones. Integrate $d\Phi/dr$ too, starting from a provisional $\Phi(0) = 0$.
3. Where $P = 0$ you have reached the surface $r_\star$, with mass $M = m(r_\star)$. Near it the pressure gradient is steep and the integrator may stop with an error; by then the answer is typically good to a part in a million.
4. By Birkhoff's theorem the exterior is Schwarzschild, so $\Phi(r_\star) = \tfrac12\ln(1 - 2GM/r_\star)$. Shift the provisional $\Phi$ by a constant to match.

## Schwarzschild everywhere: vacuum with mass

Now a detour: suppose the Schwarzschild form holds for **all** $r$, with no star. Its Einstein tensor vanishes identically, so $T_{\mu\nu} = 0$ everywhere, yet distant radial geodesics fall as if towards a mass $M$. Nothing everywhere, and that nothing has mass $M$ (p. 11).

Electrostatics has the same puzzle. A point charge's field is

<Formula label="Field of a point charge" tex="\vec E = \frac{q\,\vec r}{r^3}, \qquad \nabla\cdot\vec E = 0 \ \ (r \neq 0)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 11" />

no charge density anywhere, yet a total charge $q$: the charge sits at a singular point, $r = 0$ (in 8.07 terms, a delta function). The Schwarzschild metric does something similar for gravity, and since gravity is nonlinear, whatever happens at $r = 0$ may be "really funny" (pp. 11–12).

## Which radii are singular?

The metric itself misbehaves at $r = 0$ and at $r = 2GM$, where $g_{tt}$ vanishes and $g_{rr}$ blows up. But linearised gravity taught that coordinates can mislead. To get at the physics, build a curvature **invariant**. The Ricci tensor vanishes here, so use the Riemann tensor, contracted with itself: the **Kretschmann scalar**,

<Formula label="Kretschmann scalar of Schwarzschild" tex="I = R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}R^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} = \frac{48\,G^2M^2}{r^6}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 12–13" />

Roughly, $\sqrt I$ measures tidal forces, an invariant version of what the geodesic-deviation equation says. At $r = 2GM$ nothing happens: $I$ changes smoothly from $2.001GM$ to $1.999GM$. At $r = 0$ it diverges: tidal forces become infinite. So $r = 0$ is a true singularity; $r = 2GM$ needs another explanation (p. 13).

<WorkedExample title="Tides at r = 2GM">

Estimate the tidal scale $c^2\sqrt I = \sqrt{48}\,GM/r^3$ at $r = 2GM/c^2$ for a black hole of 10 solar masses and for one of $4\times10^6$ solar masses (like the one at the centre of our Galaxy). Use $GM_\odot = 1.327\times10^{20}$ m³/s².
</Problem>
<Step title="10 solar masses">

</Step>
<Step title="Four million solar masses">
$r = 1.18\times10^{10}$ m and $\sqrt{48}\,GM/r^3 = 2.2\times10^{-3}$ s⁻²: across 2 m, a few millimetres per second squared, unnoticeable.
</Step>
<Step title="The scaling">
At $r = 2GM$, $\sqrt I \propto M/M^3 = 1/M^2$: the bigger the black hole, the gentler the tides at that radius, by $(4\times10^5)^2 = 1.6\times10^{11}$ between these two. Nothing local marks $r = 2GM$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A coordinate singularity

Draw a circle of areal radius $r$ in the equatorial plane and let it sweep forward in Schwarzschild time, tracing a tube in spacetime. Its proper area is

<Formula label="Area of the world tube" tex="A = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\!\!\int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{-g_{tt}\,g_{\phi\phi}}\;d\phi\,dt = 2\pi r\sqrt{1 - \frac{2GM}{r}}\;\Delta t" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 13–14" />

[In the equatorial plane $g_{\phi\phi} = r^2$; the transcript says "just $r$", meaning $\sqrt{g_{\phi\phi}}$, and similarly calls $g_{tt}$ "the square root of $1 - 2GM/r$", meaning $\sqrt{-g_{tt}}$.] As $r \to 2GM$ the area goes to zero, and inside it the integral makes no sense, yet one can plainly draw such a tube. The time coordinate is at fault: it is a **coordinate singularity**. Hughes's analogy is a map of the globe drawn on a rectangle of $\theta$ and $\phi$: the top and bottom edges are really single points, the poles, and the drawing spreads them out (as flat maps inflate Greenland). Similarly, all values of $t$ will turn out to map to a single sphere at $r = 2GM$ (pp. 14–15).

## Falling in: two clocks

Drop a rock from rest at $r_0$, not in the weak field (say $r_0 = 4GM$), and follow it with two notions of time (pp. 15–16). By its own clock the solution is most simply written as a cycloid, with a parameter $\eta$ running from $0$ at release to $\pi$ at the centre:

<Formula label="Radial infall in proper time" tex="r = \frac{r_0}{2}\left(1 + \cos\eta\right), \qquad \tau = \sqrt{\frac{r_0^3}{8GM}}\left(\eta + \sin\eta\right)" cite="mtw" at="section 25.5 (standard result; Hughes writes τ as a function of r on the board, transcript pp. 15–16)" />

It reaches $r = 0$ after the finite proper time $\pi\sqrt{r_0^3/8GM}$, with tidal forces diverging on the way. (8.286 met this result in [Falling into a black hole](/courses/8-286/falling-into-a-black-hole).) In Schwarzschild time the expression is longer, and contains a logarithm,

<Formula label="Radial infall in Schwarzschild time" tex="\frac{t}{2GM} = \ln\left|\frac{\sqrt{r_0/2GM - 1} + \tan(\eta/2)}{\sqrt{r_0/2GM - 1} - \tan(\eta/2)}\right| + \sqrt{\frac{r_0}{2GM} - 1}\left[\eta + \frac{r_0}{4GM}\left(\eta + \sin\eta\right)\right]" cite="mtw" at="section 25.5 (standard result, used to check the statement on transcript p. 16)" />

which diverges when $\tan(\eta/2) = \sqrt{r_0/2GM - 1}$, exactly where $r = 2GM$. So $r$ only approaches $2GM$ as $t \to \infty$.

What does $t$ measure? Far away, $r \gg 2GM$, the metric becomes flat spacetime in spherical coordinates: the spacetime is **asymptotically flat**. So $t$ is the time of distant observers, while $\tau$ is the time of the falling body. Schwarzschild time inherits the Einstein synchronisation of special relativity, in which clocks are synchronised with light signals; so the strange behaviour must come from how light moves near $r = 2GM$. That is where Lecture 22 begins (pp. 16–17).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Where compact objects meet cosmology">
The Schwarzschild exterior and Birkhoff's theorem also underpin cosmology's Newtonian shortcuts: an overdense sphere in an expanding universe evolves on its own, as in the 8.902 page on [spherical collapse](/courses/8-902/spherical-collapse), and the massive halos that form there host the supermassive black holes whose tides, as the worked example shows, are surprisingly gentle at $r = 2GM$.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A constant-density star has $m = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi\rho r^3$ and an exact pressure profile; its central pressure diverges at $GM/r_\star = 4/9$.
- Buchdahl's theorem: no stable static fluid star is smaller than $9GM/(4c^2)$, only 12.5% outside $2GM/c^2$.
- Neutron-star matter is cold compared with its Fermi temperature (about $10^{13}$ K), so $P = P(\rho)$. For a polytrope $P = K\rho_0^\Gamma$ in the rest-mass density, $\rho = \rho_0 + P/(\Gamma - 1)$. Models are integrated numerically from the centre and matched to Schwarzschild at the surface.
- Schwarzschild everywhere is vacuum with mass, like a point charge. The Kretschmann scalar $48G^2M^2/r^6$ is finite at $r = 2GM$ and infinite at $r = 0$: only $r = 0$ is a curvature singularity.
- A falling body reaches $r = 0$ in finite proper time, but in Schwarzschild time (the time of distant observers) it never reaches $r = 2GM$, a sign of a coordinate singularity.
