MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

A few things to know about the source. The sheet's header reads "8.962 Spring 2006": for the 2020 course MIT reused an older sheet, and our source is the 2020 course's page for it. The sheet has printed page numbers, used below as "p. 1" to "p. 3". The lecture transcripts have no printed page numbers, so a transcript page below is a page of the PDF. A transcript records what was said, not what was written on the board, so a formula cited to a transcript page is printed here in the form of the lesson for that lecture. The sheet has no figure. Its pointer to a mechanics textbook for the two-body problem is left out: the hint of Problem 2 says what is needed.

Some details of the sheet are handled as follows. The sheet sets $G = 1$ in Problem 1 and in the field equation it recalls in Problem 3, but keeps $G$ in the equations it asks for; we keep $G$ everywhere, as the lessons do. In Problem 1 the sheet writes the traceless quadrupole tensor as a script letter I, and once names the original tensor with the indices jk where kl is meant; we write the traceless tensor $Q_{kl}$, as the lesson for Lecture 17 does. In Problem 2 the sheet calls the distance between the two bodies $R$ and later $r$; we call it $a$, as the lessons do, so that $r$ stays the distance to the observer and $R$ the Riemann tensor.

Some boxes are our additions, each marked where it appears: the two choice boxes of Problem 1, the frequency and polarization boxes of Problem 2(a), the box for the chirp mass in Problem 2(d), all of Problem 2(e) (a pair of neutron stars, with our own numbers), and every box of Problems 3 and 4.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit named in the question. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `3*G*M/a^2`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. It treats its letters as independent, so each question says which letters to type: `mu` for $\mu$, `Omega` for $\Omega$, `m1` and `m2` for $m_1$ and $m_2$, and `Mc` for the chirp mass $\mathcal{M}$.

Give a number answer to 3 significant figures and keep 4 in intermediate steps; an answer with only 2 significant figures may or may not be accepted. Where a box needs more digits, its label says so.

MIT's set gives no points. The points here, and their split between parts, are ours. Several parts ask you to show something. Those parts have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The conventions are those of the lessons. The speed of light is 1 and Newton's constant $G$ is kept (Problem 2(e) puts $c$ back). The metric has the signature $(-,+,+,+)$, and $\eta_{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$. Greek indices run over $(t, x, y, z)$, with $x^0 = t$; the Latin indices $i, j, k, l$ run over the space coordinates only. In the nearly flat region of Problems 1 to 3 an upper and a lower space index mean the same component, and a repeated index is summed over. An overdot is $d/dt$. $\partial_\mu$ means $\partial/\partial x^\mu$, $\nabla_\mu$ is the covariant derivative, and $\Box = \eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\partial_\nu = -\partial_t^2 + \nabla^2$ is the wave operator of flat spacetime (in Problem 4, $\Box = \nabla^\mu\nabla_\mu$). The Riemann tensor $R_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu}$ has the sign of the lessons, the Ricci tensor is $R_{\alpha\beta} = R^\mu{}_{\alpha\mu\beta}$.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The trace does not radiate" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

The quadrupole formula of the lesson gives the transverse-traceless wave field far from a slowly moving source:

<Formula label="Problem 1: the quadrupole formula" tex="h^{\text{TT}}_{ij} = \frac{2G}{r}\left(P_{ik}P_{jl} - \frac{1}{2}P_{ij}P_{kl}\right)\ddot I_{kl}, \qquad I_{kl} = \int d^3x\;\rho\,x^k x^l" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

Here $r$ is the distance to the observer, $\hat n$ the unit vector from the source to the observer, $P_{ij} = \delta_{ij} - n_i n_j$ the projection onto the plane at right angles to $\hat n$, and $\ddot I_{kl}$ is taken at the retarded time $t - r$. The combination of projections makes the result transverse and traceless.

Show that the same $h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}$ comes out if the quadrupole moment $I_{kl}$ is replaced by its traceless part,

<Formula label="Problem 1: the traceless quadrupole moment" tex="Q_{kl} = I_{kl} - \frac{1}{3}\,\delta_{kl}\,I_{mm}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

This takes some care, because two different traces appear: the trace with the metric of space, $\delta_{kl}$, which defines $Q_{kl}$, and the trace with the metric of the plane at right angles to the wave, $P_{kl}$, which the projection removes. In the lecture Hughes remarks that the TT definition involves more than one notion of trace (Lecture 16 transcript, p. 18).

The sheet adds a remark on counting. A radiating field of multipole order $l$ has $2l + 1$ components: one for a scalar, three for a dipole, five for a quadrupole. A symmetric $3\times 3$ tensor has six. The exercise shows that the sixth, the trace, carries nothing that radiates, which is why the formula is often written with $Q_{kl}$.

The two boxes are our additions.

<Hint>

Write $\ddot Q_{kl} = \ddot I_{kl} - \frac13\delta_{kl}\ddot I_{mm}$ and put it into the formula. What is left to show is that the bracket of projections gives zero when it acts on $\delta_{kl}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You need two properties of $P_{ij}$: what $P_{ik}P_{kj}$ is, and what its trace $P_{kk}$ is. Work both out from $P_{ij} = \delta_{ij} - n_i n_j$ with $n_k n_k = 1$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-pp" label="$P_{ik}P_{kj} =$" choices={["δ_ij, because the projection is its own inverse", "0, because each factor removes the part along n, so nothing is left", "P_ij, because projecting twice is the same as projecting once", "2 P_ij, one P_ij from each of the two factors"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="p1-trace" label="$P_{kk} =$" choices={["3, the number of space dimensions the sum runs over", "2, the number of dimensions of the plane across the wave", "1, since the projection removes two of the three directions", "0, since the projection is traceless like the wave field"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The bracket of projections, acting on $\delta_{kl}$, gives $P_{ij} - \frac12 P_{ij}P_{kk}$. Because the plane across the wave has two dimensions, $P_{kk} = 2$ and the two terms cancel. So the trace part of $I_{kl}$ never reaches $h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}$.

<Step title="Two properties of the projection">

With $n_k n_k = 1$:

<Formula label="Problem 1: the projection is idempotent and has trace 2" tex="P_{ik}P_{kj} = \left(\delta_{ik} - n_i n_k\right)\left(\delta_{kj} - n_k n_j\right) = \delta_{ij} - 2n_i n_j + n_i n_j = P_{ij}, \qquad P_{kk} = 3 - n_k n_k = 2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The trace part drops out">

Call the bracket $\Lambda_{ij,kl} = P_{ik}P_{jl} - \frac12 P_{ij}P_{kl}$. Acting on $\delta_{kl}$ it gives

<Formula label="Problem 1: the projection acting on the metric of space" tex="\Lambda_{ij,kl}\,\delta_{kl} = P_{ik}P_{jk} - \frac{1}{2}P_{ij}P_{kk} = P_{ij} - \frac{1}{2}\cdot 2\,P_{ij} = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

($P$ is symmetric, so $P_{ik}P_{jk} = P_{ik}P_{kj}$.) Therefore

<Formula label="Problem 1: the result" tex="\Lambda_{ij,kl}\,\ddot Q_{kl} = \Lambda_{ij,kl}\,\ddot I_{kl} - \frac{1}{3}\,\ddot I_{mm}\,\Lambda_{ij,kl}\,\delta_{kl} = \Lambda_{ij,kl}\,\ddot I_{kl}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

and $h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}$ is the same with either tensor.

</Step>

<Step title="The two traces">

The trace removed from $I_{kl}$ is taken with $\delta_{kl}$, in three dimensions; the trace that the bracket removes is taken with $P_{kl}$, in the two dimensions across the wave. They are different operations, and the factor $\frac12$ in the bracket is $1/P_{kk}$. The result holds because that same number, 2, appears when the bracket acts on $\delta_{kl}$: the part of $\delta_{kl}$ across the wave is $P_{kl}$ itself, which the bracket removes, and the part along the wave, $n_k n_l$, is killed by the projections.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The trace $\int d^3x\,\rho\,|\vec x|^2$ measures how spread out the source is, the same in every direction. A pulsating sphere changes it but stays spherical, and by Birkhoff's theorem a spherical body sends out no gravitational waves: the formula agrees. The count also works: six components of $I_{kl}$ minus one trace leave five, the $2l + 1$ of a quadrupole with $l = 2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A binary system and its chirp" points={40} mit="Problem 2">

Two bodies of masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ move on a circular orbit about each other, a distance $a$ apart, in the $xy$-plane. Newtonian gravity describes the orbit well enough, and the problem uses it to find the leading effects of the gravitational waves the pair sends out.

As in any two-body problem, the motion is that of a single body of the reduced mass $\mu$ on a circular orbit of radius $a$ about a fixed body of the total mass $M$:

<Formula label="Problem 2: reduced and total mass" tex="\mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}, \qquad M = m_1 + m_2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1" />

<Part label="a" points={8}>

Find the wave field $h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}$ seen by an observer at distance $r$ on the axis of the orbit (the $z$ axis). Give the amplitude of $h_+ = h^{\text{TT}}_{xx}$ in terms of $G$, $\mu$, $M$, $a$ and $r$, using Kepler's third law to remove the orbital angular frequency $\Omega$. Type `a` for $a$.

The two choice boxes are our additions.

<Hint>

Put the bodies at the distances $\frac{m_2}{M}a$ and $\frac{m_1}{M}a$ from their centre of mass, on opposite sides, along the direction $(\cos\Omega t, \sin\Omega t, 0)$, and add up $m\,x^k x^l$ for the two.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\cos^2\Omega t$ and $\sin\Omega t\cos\Omega t$ are easier to differentiate as functions of $2\Omega t$. For $\hat n = \hat z$, $P_{ij}$ is the unit matrix on the $xy$-plane: the bracket keeps $I_{xx}$, $I_{yy}$ and $I_{xy}$ and removes their trace in that plane.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2a-amp" label="Amplitude of $h_+$:" expr="4*G^2*mu*M/(r*a)"
  vars={{ G: [0.5, 2], mu: [0.1, 0.2], M: [1, 2], r: [10, 100], a: [1, 5] }} names={{ mu: "\\mu" }}
  mistakes={[

    { expr: "8*G^2*mu*M/(r*a)", hint: "The trace in the plane has to be removed: the xx component of the bracket acting on the second derivative is (Ï_xx − Ï_yy)/2, not Ï_xx − Ï_yy." },
    { expr: "4*G^2*M^2/(r*a)", hint: "The quadrupole moment of the pair is μa² times the product of the unit vectors: the reduced mass, not the total mass, multiplies it." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2a-freq" label="The angular frequency of the wave is:" choices={["Ω/2, half the orbital frequency", "Ω, the orbital frequency itself", "4Ω, four times the orbital frequency", "2Ω, twice the orbital frequency"]} answer={3} />

<Answer id="p2a-pol" label="Seen along the axis, $h_+$ and $h_\times$ have:" choices={["equal amplitudes, a quarter of a cycle apart", "equal amplitudes and the same phase", "different amplitudes, with h× zero on the axis", "different amplitudes, half a cycle apart"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The quadrupole moment of the pair is $\mu a^2$ times the product of two components of the unit vector along the line joining the bodies. Its components oscillate at $2\Omega$, and along the axis the projection keeps the $xy$ block without its trace.

<Step title="The quadrupole moment">

Adding $m\,x^kx^l$ for the two bodies gives $\left(m_1 m_2^2 + m_2 m_1^2\right)a^2/M^2 = \mu a^2$ times the product of the components of that unit vector:

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the quadrupole moment of the binary" tex="I_{xx} = \frac{\mu a^2}{2}\left(1 + \cos 2\Omega t\right), \qquad I_{yy} = \frac{\mu a^2}{2}\left(1 - \cos 2\Omega t\right), \qquad I_{xy} = \frac{\mu a^2}{2}\,\sin 2\Omega t" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The components with $z$ are zero. Two derivatives give $\ddot I_{xx} = -\ddot I_{yy} = -2\mu a^2\Omega^2\cos 2\Omega t$ and $\ddot I_{xy} = -2\mu a^2\Omega^2\sin 2\Omega t$.

</Step>

<Step title="Project">

For $\hat n = \hat z$: $h^{\text{TT}}_{xx} = \frac{2G}{r}\left[\ddot I_{xx} - \frac12\left(\ddot I_{xx} + \ddot I_{yy}\right)\right] = \frac{G}{r}\left(\ddot I_{xx} - \ddot I_{yy}\right)$, $h^{\text{TT}}_{yy} = -h^{\text{TT}}_{xx}$, $h^{\text{TT}}_{xy} = \frac{2G}{r}\ddot I_{xy}$, and every component with $z$ is zero. With Kepler's law $\Omega^2 = GM/a^3$:

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the wave on the axis" tex="h_+ = -\frac{4G\mu a^2\Omega^2}{r}\cos 2\Omega(t - r) = -\frac{4G^2\mu M}{r\,a}\cos 2\Omega(t - r), \qquad h_\times = -\frac{4G^2\mu M}{r\,a}\sin 2\Omega(t - r)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The wave has twice the orbital frequency, and the two polarizations have equal amplitudes a quarter cycle apart: seen face-on, the wave is circularly polarized.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$G\mu/r$ and $GM/a$ are both pure numbers with $c = 1$, so $h$ is a pure number. For two equal masses $m$ ($\mu = m/2$, $M = 2m$) the amplitude is $4G^2m^2/(ra) = 2Gma^2\Omega^2/r$, the result of the lesson's worked example. The frequency doubles because the mass distribution looks the same after half an orbit.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={8}>

Find the power $P$ that the gravitational waves carry away, in terms of $G$, $\mu$, $M$ and $a$.

<Hint>

Use the second quadrupole formula of the lesson for Lecture 17: the power is $\frac{G}{5}$ times the average of the squared third time derivative of $Q_{ij}$, summed over all $i$ and $j$. How does the trace of $I_{ij}$ change in time?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The sum runs over all nine pairs, so both $xy$ and $yx$ count. Each derivative of a function of $2\Omega t$ brings a factor $2\Omega$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2b-power" label="$P =$" expr="32/5*G^4*mu^2*M^3/a^5"
  vars={{ G: [0.5, 2], mu: [0.1, 0.2], M: [1, 2], a: [1, 5] }} names={{ mu: "\\mu" }}
  mistakes={[

<Solution>

**The idea.** The trace of $I_{ij}$ is $\mu a^2$, a constant, so the third derivatives of $Q_{ij}$ and $I_{ij}$ are the same. Three derivatives of the components of part (a), squared and summed, give a constant, so no average is needed.

<Step title="The power formula">

The lesson gives

<Formula label="The quadrupole formula for the power" tex="P = \frac{G}{5}\left\langle \frac{d^3Q_{ij}}{dt^3}\,\frac{d^3Q_{ij}}{dt^3} \right\rangle" cite="reading6" at="transcript p. 20" />

</Step>

<Step title="Third derivatives">

$\frac{d^3I_{xx}}{dt^3} = -\frac{d^3I_{yy}}{dt^3} = 4\mu a^2\Omega^3\sin 2\Omega t$ and $\frac{d^3I_{xy}}{dt^3} = \frac{d^3I_{yx}}{dt^3} = -4\mu a^2\Omega^3\cos 2\Omega t$. The sum of squares is $16\mu^2a^4\Omega^6\left(2\sin^2 2\Omega t + 2\cos^2 2\Omega t\right) = 32\mu^2a^4\Omega^6$. With $\Omega^6 = G^3M^3/a^9$:

<Formula label="Problem 2(b): the radiated power" tex="P = \frac{32}{5}\,G\,\mu^2 a^4\Omega^6 = \frac{32}{5}\,\frac{G^4\mu^2M^3}{a^5}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For two equal masses $m$, $\mu^2M^3 = \frac{m^2}{4}\cdot 8m^3 = 2m^5$ and $P = \frac{64}{5}G^4m^5/a^5$, the lesson's result. With $c$ restored the power is $\frac{32}{5}\frac{G^4\mu^2M^3}{c^5a^5}$, an energy per time. In our calculation file the formula is compared with the sum of squares of numerical second derivatives of the bodies' positions.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={7}>

As the pair loses energy, the orbit shrinks and the frequency rises: it "chirps". Assume that the energy of the orbit and the energy of the waves together are conserved,

<Formula label="Problem 2(c): global energy conservation" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(E_{\text{kinetic}} + E_{\text{potential}} + E_{\text{GW}}\right) = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 2, eq. (1)" />

with $dE_{\text{GW}}/dt = P$. Find the rate $da/dt$ at which the separation shrinks, in terms of $G$, $\mu$, $M$ and $a$.

<Hint>

For a circular Newtonian orbit the kinetic energy is minus half the potential energy. Write the energy of the orbit as a function of $a$ alone.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2c-adot" label="$da/dt =$" expr="-64/5*G^3*mu*M^2/a^3"
  vars={{ G: [0.5, 2], mu: [0.1, 0.2], M: [1, 2], a: [1, 5] }} names={{ mu: "\\mu" }}

<Solution>

**The idea.** The energy of the orbit depends on $a$ only. Its rate of change is minus the power, and the chain rule gives $da/dt$.

<Step title="Energy of a circular orbit">

With $\mu v^2 = GM\mu/a$ on a circle:

<Formula label="Problem 2(c): the energy of the orbit" tex="E_{\text{kinetic}} + E_{\text{potential}} = \frac{GM\mu}{2a} - \frac{GM\mu}{a} = -\frac{GM\mu}{2a}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The rate">

$\frac{d}{dt}\left(-\frac{GM\mu}{2a}\right) = \frac{GM\mu}{2a^2}\,\frac{da}{dt} = -P$, so

<Formula label="Problem 2(c): the shrinking orbit" tex="\frac{da}{dt} = -\frac{2a^2}{GM\mu}\cdot\frac{32}{5}\frac{G^4\mu^2M^3}{a^5} = -\frac{64}{5}\,\frac{G^3\mu M^2}{a^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units with $c$ restored: $G^3\mu M^2/(c^5a^3)$ is (m³/s²)³ over (m/s)⁵ m³, a speed. The rate grows fast as $a$ shrinks, so the end comes quickly: integrating, $a^4$ falls linearly in time, and the pair merges after a time $\frac{5}{256}\frac{a^4}{G^3\mu M^2}$ (for equal masses this is the lesson's $\frac{5}{512}\frac{a^4}{G^3m^3}$).

</Step>

In flat space $\nabla \to \partial$ and this is Problem 3(a). In empty space the Riemann tensor is divergence-free, just as in electromagnetism without charges.

</Step>

<Part label="b" points={10}>

Use the Bianchi identity and part (a) to derive a nonlinear wave equation $\Box R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} = 8\pi G\,[\text{source}]$, with $\Box = \nabla^\mu\nabla_\mu$. The source now has three kinds of terms: second covariant derivatives of $\bar T_{\mu\nu}$, products of $\bar T_{\mu\nu}$ with the Riemann tensor, and products of two Riemann tensors. Penrose first found this equation, and the sheet notes that Teukolsky's variant, built around the curvature of a black hole, has become central to the study of waves from black holes. Our box asks about the equation in empty space.

<Hint>

Take $\nabla^\alpha$ of the Bianchi identity, as in Problem 3(b). This time $\nabla^\alpha$ cannot simply be moved past $\nabla_\beta$: the difference is a commutator.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The commutator of two covariant derivatives acting on a tensor with four lower indices gives one term with the Riemann tensor for each index. In one of them the contraction produces the Ricci tensor, which the field equation turns into $8\pi G\,\bar T$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4b-vac" label="In empty space ($\bar T_{\mu\nu} = 0$), the right side of the equation:" choices={["is zero, so the Riemann tensor obeys a free wave equation exactly", "is linear in the Riemann tensor, through the Ricci tensor", "holds only first derivatives of the metric", "is quadratic in the Riemann tensor: products of two curvatures"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** As in Problem 3(b), the divergence of the Bianchi identity gives $\Box R$ plus derivatives of the divergence of $R$. Moving $\nabla^\alpha$ past the other derivative costs commutators, which bring the products of curvatures.

<Step title="The divergence of the identity">

$\nabla^\alpha$ of the identity, with $R_{\gamma\alpha\mu\nu} = -R_{\alpha\gamma\mu\nu}$, gives $\Box R_{\beta\gamma\mu\nu} = \nabla^\alpha\nabla_\beta R_{\alpha\gamma\mu\nu} - \nabla^\alpha\nabla_\gamma R_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu}$. Write $\nabla^\alpha\nabla_\beta = \nabla_\beta\nabla^\alpha + g^{\alpha\lambda}[\nabla_\lambda, \nabla_\beta]$. The first piece brings in part (a). For the commutator, the rule for a one-form $p_\sigma$, used for each lower index, is

<Formula label="Riemann tensor from commutators of covariant derivatives" tex="\left[\nabla_\mu, \nabla_\nu\right]p_\sigma = -R^\lambda{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}\,p_\lambda" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 19" />

applied once to each of the four indices of $R_{\alpha\gamma\mu\nu}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Collect the terms">

In the term for the index $\alpha$, the contraction gives $g^{\alpha\lambda}R^\sigma{}_{\alpha\lambda\beta} = -R^\sigma{}_\beta$, a Ricci tensor, which becomes $8\pi G\,\bar T^\sigma{}_\beta$. The other three terms are products of two Riemann tensors; two of them (from the second index, with $\beta$ and $\gamma$ swapped) combine by the cyclic identity into one. Renaming the indices to $\alpha\beta\gamma\delta$:

<Formula label="Problem 4(b): the Penrose wave equation" tex="\begin{aligned} \Box R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} ={}& 8\pi G\left(\nabla_\alpha\nabla_\gamma \bar T_{\beta\delta} - \nabla_\alpha\nabla_\delta \bar T_{\beta\gamma} - \nabla_\beta\nabla_\gamma \bar T_{\alpha\delta} + \nabla_\beta\nabla_\delta \bar T_{\alpha\gamma}\right) + 8\pi G\left(\bar T^{\sigma}{}_{\alpha}\,R_{\sigma\beta\gamma\delta} - \bar T^{\sigma}{}_{\beta}\,R_{\sigma\alpha\gamma\delta}\right) \\ & - R_{\alpha\beta}{}^{\lambda\sigma}R_{\lambda\sigma\gamma\delta} - R^{\sigma}{}_{\gamma}{}^{\lambda}{}_{\alpha}\,R_{\lambda\beta\sigma\delta} - R^{\sigma}{}_{\delta}{}^{\lambda}{}_{\alpha}\,R_{\lambda\beta\gamma\sigma} + R^{\sigma}{}_{\gamma}{}^{\lambda}{}_{\beta}\,R_{\lambda\alpha\sigma\delta} + R^{\sigma}{}_{\delta}{}^{\lambda}{}_{\beta}\,R_{\lambda\alpha\gamma\sigma} \end{aligned}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

Raising $\alpha$ with the metric gives the sheet's form $\Box R^\alpha{}_{\beta\gamma\delta}$, since the metric passes through $\Box$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In the linearized limit both kinds of product terms are of second order and drop out, and $\nabla \to \partial$: the first bracket is the source of Problem 3(b). In empty space only the products of two Riemann tensors remain, so the curvature sources itself: gravity is nonlinear. The signs and factors depend on the conventions stated in "How to answer". While writing this page we checked the equation numerically: for a metric with random coefficients, both sides agree at a point, with the Ricci tensor standing for $8\pi G\,\bar T$.

</Step>
