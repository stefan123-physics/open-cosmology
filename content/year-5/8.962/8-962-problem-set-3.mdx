## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit named in the question. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `x + 1/g` or `-r*sin(th)^2`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. It treats its letters as independent. Type the angles $\theta$ and $\phi$ (and $\theta_0$ in Problem 6) as `th` and `ph`, the small number $\varepsilon$ of Problem 2 as `eps`, the field strength $E_0$ of Problem 3 as `E0` and the angular speed $\omega$ of Problem 5 as `w`. All other letters are typed as they are printed: `g`, `t`, `d`, `x`, `P`, `v`, `r`, `n`.

MIT's set gives no points. The points here, and their split between parts, are ours. Much of this set asks you to show something. Those parts have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The conventions are those of the lessons. The speed of light is 1, so a speed is a fraction of the speed of light and time and length have the same unit. The metric of special relativity is $\eta_{\alpha\beta} = \mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$; a general metric is $g_{\alpha\beta}$, its inverse is $g^{\alpha\beta}$ and its determinant is $g$. Greek indices run over the four spacetime coordinates and Latin indices $i, j, k$ over the three coordinates of space. A repeated index, once up and once down, is summed over, and $\partial_\alpha$ means $\partial/\partial x^\alpha$. The Levi-Civita tensor $\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}$ has $\epsilon_{0123} = +1$ in an inertial frame, as in the lessons, so that $\epsilon^{0123} = -1$. The lower indices of a Christoffel symbol are symmetric, $\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} = \Gamma^\mu_{\beta\alpha}$. Several answers depend on these conventions; the solutions say where.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The accelerated observer revisited" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

An astronaut moves along the $x$ axis of an inertial frame with coordinates $(t, x)$. The astronaut feels a constant acceleration $g$: the 4-acceleration $\vec a$ has $\vec a\cdot\vec a = g^2$. With $c = 1$, $g$ has the unit of one over a length. The clock of the astronaut shows the proper time $\bar t$. At $\bar t = 0$ the astronaut is at rest at $x = 0$, at the time $t = 0$. This is the astronaut of MIT's Problem Set 2, and that problem leads to the worldline

<Formula label="Problem 1: the worldline of the astronaut" tex="t = \frac{1}{g}\sinh\left(g\,\bar t\right), \qquad x = \frac{1}{g}\left[\cosh\left(g\,\bar t\right) - 1\right]" cite="pset2" at="Problem 6(a) and (d), p. 3 (the result of those parts, in our own words)" />

The astronaut also carries a coordinate $\bar x$ along the direction of motion, with $\bar x = 0$ at the astronaut. Events with the same $\bar t$ are simultaneous in the inertial frame in which the astronaut is at rest at that moment. The two sets of coordinates are related by

<Formula label="Problem 1: the coordinates of the astronaut" tex="t = \left(\frac{1}{g} + \bar x\right)\sinh\left(g\,\bar t\right), \qquad x = \left(\frac{1}{g} + \bar x\right)\cosh\left(g\,\bar t\right) - \frac{1}{g}" cite="pset2" at="Problem 6(d), p. 3 (the result of that part, in our own words)" />

and the line element in the coordinates of the astronaut is

<Formula label="Problem 1: the line element of the astronaut" tex="ds^2 = -dt^2 + dx^2 = -\left(1 + g\,\bar x\right)^2 d\bar t^{\,2} + d\bar x^2" cite="pset2" at="Problem 6(e), p. 3" />

Throughout this problem consider only $t \ge 0$.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Draw the worldline of the astronaut in a spacetime diagram, with $x$ across and $t$ up. To do so, first remove $\bar t$ from the worldline: give the position $x$ of the astronaut as a function of the time $t$ of the inertial frame. Type `g` and `t`.

<Hint>

The two functions obey $\cosh^2 u - \sinh^2 u = 1$. Solve the worldline for $\cosh(g\bar t)$ and for $\sinh(g\bar t)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1a-x" label="$x(t) =$" expr="(sqrt(1 + g^2*t^2) - 1)/g"
  vars={{ g: [0.5, 2], t: [0.2, 3] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "g*t^2/2", hint: "That is Newton's result. It holds only while g t is much smaller than 1; later it would make the astronaut faster than light." },
    { expr: "sqrt(1 + g^2*t^2)/g", hint: "At t = 0 this gives x = 1/g, but the astronaut starts at x = 0. The worldline has a term −1/g." },
    { expr: "sqrt(t^2 + 1/g^2) + 1/g", hint: "The sign of the last term is reversed: at t = 0 the astronaut is at x = 0, not at 2/g." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The worldline is a hyperbola. Its equation follows from $\cosh^2 - \sinh^2 = 1$.

<Step title="Remove the proper time">

From the worldline, $\sinh(g\bar t) = g t$ and $\cosh(g\bar t) = g x + 1$. So

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the hyperbola" tex="\left(x + \frac{1}{g}\right)^2 - t^2 = \frac{1}{g^2}, \qquad x(t) = \frac{1}{g}\left(\sqrt{1 + g^2t^2} - 1\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The plus sign of the root is the right one, because $\cosh$ is never smaller than 1.

</Step>

<Step title="The diagram">

The graph shows the worldline for $g = 1$, so that lengths and times are in units of $1/g$. The astronaut starts at rest (the curve leaves the origin straight up) and then moves to the right. The speed is $dx/dt = g t/\sqrt{1 + g^2t^2}$, which grows towards 1 but never reaches it. The dashed line is used in parts (b) and (c).

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-2, 3]} range={[0, 4]} xLabel="x" yLabel="t"

The dashed line t = x + 1 is the line that the worldline approaches. The short line is a flash of light that leaves x = −0.5 at t = 0 and reaches the astronaut at t = 0.75." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For small times $\sqrt{1 + g^2t^2} \approx 1 + \tfrac12 g^2t^2$, so $x \approx \tfrac12 g t^2$, Newton's result. For large times $x \approx t - 1/g$: the astronaut moves at almost the speed of light. In units: $g t$ is a pure number and $1/g$ is a length, as it should be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Show that there is a region of spacetime that is causally disconnected from the astronaut: no event in it can affect the astronaut, because nothing travels faster than light.

Start with an example (our addition). A lamp at rest at $x = -d$, a distance $d < 1/g$ behind the astronaut's starting point, flashes at $t = 0$. At what time $t$ of the inertial frame does the flash reach the astronaut? Type `d` and `g`. Then decide what happens when $d$ grows towards $1/g$, and give the general argument.

<Hint>

The flash moves along $x = -d + t$. Along it, the combination $t - x$ keeps the value $d$. What is $t - x$ on the worldline of the astronaut?

</Hint>

<Hint>

On the worldline $t - x = \tfrac{1}{g}\left(1 - e^{-g\bar t}\right)$. Set this equal to $d$, solve for $e^{-g\bar t}$, and put the result into $t = \sinh(g\bar t)/g$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1b-t" label="$t =$" expr="d*(2 - g*d)/(2*(1 - g*d))"
  vars={{ g: [0.5, 2], d: [0.05, 0.4] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "d", hint: "That is the time the flash needs to reach x = 0. By then the astronaut has moved on, and the flash has to catch up." },
    { expr: "d/(1 - g*d)", hint: "Close, but check the limit of small d: there the astronaut has hardly moved and the answer must tend to d, with a correction g d²/2." },
    { expr: "d*(2 - g*d)/(1 - g*d)", hint: "A factor of 2 is missing in the denominator: sinh u is half of the difference of two exponentials." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1b-limit" label="As $d$ grows towards $1/g$, the arrival time" choices={["tends to 2/g, twice the light travel time over the distance 1/g, because the astronaut's speed levels off", "falls back to zero, because lamp and astronaut then start at the same place", "grows without limit: a flash that starts at x = −1/g or further back never arrives", "tends to 1/g, the time that light needs to cross the distance 1/g"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Follow $t - x$. Along anything that moves no faster than light it cannot decrease, and on the worldline of the astronaut it stays below $1/g$.

<Step title="The example">

On the worldline,

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): t − x on the worldline" tex="t - x = \frac{1}{g}\left[\sinh\left(g\,\bar t\right) - \cosh\left(g\,\bar t\right) + 1\right] = \frac{1}{g}\left(1 - e^{-g\bar t}\right) < \frac{1}{g}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The flash has $t - x = d$ all along its path. It meets the astronaut when $e^{-g\bar t} = 1 - g d$. Then

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): arrival time of the flash" tex="t = \frac{1}{2g}\left(e^{g\bar t} - e^{-g\bar t}\right) = \frac{1}{2g}\left(\frac{1}{1 - g d} - \left(1 - g d\right)\right) = \frac{d\left(2 - g d\right)}{2\left(1 - g d\right)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

For the flash in the graph of part (a), $g = 1$ and $d = 0.5$: $t = 0.5\times 1.5/(2\times 0.5) = 0.75$, and the astronaut is then at $x = t - d = 0.25$. As $d \to 1/g$ the denominator goes to zero: the arrival time grows without limit. For $d \ge 1/g$ there is no solution, because $e^{-g\bar t}$ cannot be zero or negative.

</Step>

<Step title="The general argument">

Take any event $(t_e, x_e)$ with $t_e - x_e \ge 1/g$. Whatever leaves this event, light or matter, moves with $|dx/dt| \le 1$, so along its path $d(t - x)/dt = 1 - dx/dt \ge 0$. Its value of $t - x$ therefore never drops below $t_e - x_e \ge 1/g$. The astronaut always has $t - x < 1/g$. The two can never be at the same place at the same time. So the whole region $t \ge x + 1/g$ is causally disconnected from the astronaut: events there cannot affect the astronaut, however long the astronaut waits. In the graph of part (a) it is the region on and above the dashed line.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For small $d$ the arrival time is $d\,(1 + \tfrac12 g d + \dots)$: a little more than the light travel time $d$, because the astronaut has moved on by $\tfrac12 g d^2$ in the meantime. The region exists only because the astronaut keeps accelerating. An observer who stops accelerating moves on a straight line with a speed below 1, and every flash then arrives in the end.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

Find the boundary between the events that can affect the astronaut and those that cannot. Give it first as a line in the inertial frame, $t$ as a function of $x$ (type `x` and `g`), then as a value of the astronaut's own coordinate $\bar x$ (type `g`).

Our addition: for an astronaut who feels $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$, the acceleration of a falling body on Earth, give the distance $1/g$ in light years. In ordinary units this distance is $c^2/g$. Use $c = 299\,792\,458$ m/s and a year of 365.25 days.

(MIT's sheet calls this boundary a particle horizon. For an accelerated observer it is usually called the Rindler horizon. In the language of cosmology it is of the kind called an event horizon: events beyond it are never seen by this observer. The remark on names is ours.)

<Hint>

Part (b) showed that events with $t - x \ge 1/g$ cannot affect the astronaut. Can every event with $t - x < 1/g$ send a flash that arrives?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the second box, put the coordinates of the astronaut into $x + 1/g - t$. The result is a product of two factors.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1c-line" label="$t =$" expr="x + 1/g"
  vars={{ g: [0.5, 2], x: [-2, 2] }}
  mistakes={[

    { expr: "x - 1/g", hint: "That line crosses t = 0 at x = +1/g, ahead of the astronaut. The line that the hyperbola approaches crosses t = 0 behind the astronaut." },
    { expr: "x + g", hint: "The offset must be a length. With c = 1, g is one over a length." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1c-xbar" label="$\bar x =$" expr="-1/g"
  vars={{ g: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/g", hint: "The horizon lies behind the astronaut, on the side of negative x̄." },

  ]} />

<Answer id="p1c-ly" label="$c^2/g =$" value="0.968" unit="light years" tolerance="1%" calc="p1c-ly" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The boundary is the line $t - x = 1/g$, the line that the hyperbola approaches. In the coordinates of the astronaut it is the place where the factor $1 + g\bar x$ of the line element is zero.

<Step title="The boundary in the inertial frame">

Part (b) showed that no event with $t - x \ge 1/g$ can affect the astronaut. Now take an event with $t_e - x_e < 1/g$ that lies to the left of the worldline. A flash sent from it to the right keeps $t - x = t_e - x_e$, and the equation $\tfrac{1}{g}(1 - e^{-g\bar t}) = t_e - x_e$ has a solution $\bar t$: the flash arrives. (An event to the right of the worldline sends its flash to the left, and that flash always meets the worldline, which moves slower than light.) So the boundary is

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): the horizon in the inertial frame" tex="t = x + \frac{1}{g}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

It is the path of a flash of light that leaves $x = -1/g$ at $t = 0$ and chases the astronaut for ever.

</Step>

<Step title="The boundary in the coordinates of the astronaut">

With the coordinates of the astronaut,

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): the horizon in the astronaut's coordinates" tex="x + \frac{1}{g} - t = \left(\frac{1}{g} + \bar x\right)\left[\cosh\left(g\,\bar t\right) - \sinh\left(g\,\bar t\right)\right] = \left(\frac{1}{g} + \bar x\right)e^{-g\bar t}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

This is zero for $\bar x = -1/g$ (or in the limit $\bar t \to \infty$). So the horizon is a fixed distance $1/g$ behind the astronaut, at all times. There $1 + g\bar x = 0$: the line element $-(1 + g\bar x)^2 d\bar t^{\,2} + d\bar x^2$ loses its time term, and the astronaut's coordinates cannot be used at or beyond that point. Nothing special happens to spacetime there; the inertial coordinates $(t, x)$ cover the line without trouble.

</Step>

<Step title="The distance in light years">

A light year is $299\,792\,458\ \text{m/s} \times 365.25\times 86\,400\ \text{s} = 9.4607\times 10^{15}$ m. With $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$,

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): the horizon distance for g = 9.81 m/s²" tex="\frac{c^2}{g} = \frac{\left(299\,792\,458\ \text{m/s}\right)^2}{9.81\ \text{m/s}^2} = 9.162\times 10^{15}\ \text{m} = 0.968\ \text{light years}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own addition)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The horizon is a null line, so it has the same form for every inertial observer, as a boundary set by the speed of light should. Its distance grows when $g$ gets smaller, and for $g \to 0$ it moves away to infinity: an inertial observer has no such horizon. Like the event horizon of a black hole, it lets signals through in one direction only: the astronaut can send signals across it, but receives none from the other side. Unlike a black hole's horizon, it belongs to one observer and disappears when the engine is switched off.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Why the pressure of a perfect fluid is the same in all directions" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

A perfect fluid has no viscosity: no force acts along the surface between two elements of the fluid. In the rest frame of the fluid its stress-energy tensor is then diagonal, and the lesson gives it in Cartesian coordinates $(t, x, y, z)$ as

<Formula label="Problem 2: the perfect fluid in its rest frame" tex="T^{\alpha\beta} = \mathrm{diag}\left(\rho,\ P,\ P,\ P\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1" />

with the energy density $\rho$ and the pressure $P$. Hughes gives the equal pressures without much proof (he calls the pressure an isotropic stress, transcript of Lecture 5, p. 10). Suppose instead that the pressure along $x$ were different:

<Formula label="Problem 2: a fluid with a different pressure along x" tex="T^{\alpha\beta} = \mathrm{diag}\left(\rho,\ P\left(1 + \varepsilon\right),\ P,\ P\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1" />

Here $\varepsilon$ is a number. Now turn the axes about the $z$ axis by the angle $\phi$. The new Cartesian coordinates are $x' = x\cos\phi + y\sin\phi$ and $y' = -x\sin\phi + y\cos\phi$, with $t$ and $z$ unchanged. (The direction of the turn is our choice; the sheet does not fix it.)

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find the components $T^{x'y'}$ and $T^{x'x'}$ in the new coordinates. Type `P`, `eps` and `ph`.

<Hint>

A tensor with two upper indices transforms with two copies of the matrix $L^{\alpha'}{}_{\alpha} = \partial x^{\alpha'}/\partial x^\alpha$. Read its four entries in the $x$-$y$ block off the formulas for $x'$ and $y'$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Only $T^{xx}$ and $T^{yy}$ contribute: $T^{x'y'} = L^{x'}{}_x L^{y'}{}_x T^{xx} + L^{x'}{}_y L^{y'}{}_y T^{yy}$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Transform the tensor with one matrix for each index. Only the $x$-$y$ block changes.

<Step title="The matrix">

From $x' = x\cos\phi + y\sin\phi$ and $y' = -x\sin\phi + y\cos\phi$, the matrix of the lesson, $L^{\alpha'}{}_{\alpha} = \partial x^{\alpha'}/\partial x^\alpha$ (transcript of Lecture 5, p. 23), has

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the rotation matrix" tex="L^{x'}{}_{x} = \cos\phi, \qquad L^{x'}{}_{y} = \sin\phi, \qquad L^{y'}{}_{x} = -\sin\phi, \qquad L^{y'}{}_{y} = \cos\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

and $L^{t'}{}_t = L^{z'}{}_z = 1$; all other entries are zero.

</Step>

<Step title="The components">

With $T^{\alpha'\beta'} = L^{\alpha'}{}_{\alpha}L^{\beta'}{}_{\beta}T^{\alpha\beta}$ and a diagonal $T^{\alpha\beta}$:

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the rotated components" tex="T^{x'y'} = -\sin\phi\cos\phi\,P\left(1 + \varepsilon\right) + \sin\phi\cos\phi\,P = -\varepsilon P\sin\phi\cos\phi, \qquad T^{x'x'} = \cos^2\phi\,P\left(1 + \varepsilon\right) + \sin^2\phi\,P = P\left(1 + \varepsilon\cos^2\phi\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** "No viscosity" must hold for every choice of Cartesian axes in the rest frame, not only for one.

<Step title="The off-diagonal component">

$T^{x'y'}$ is the flow of $x'$ momentum across a surface of constant $y'$: a force along that surface, a shear stress. For a perfect fluid it must vanish, whatever the direction of the axes. Part (a) gives $T^{x'y'} = -\tfrac12\varepsilon P\sin 2\phi$, of order $\varepsilon P$. It is zero for every $\phi$ only if $\varepsilon P = 0$. </Step>

<Step title="The angle">

$|\sin 2\phi|$ is largest at $2\phi = 90^\circ$, so at $\phi = 45^\circ$. There $T^{x'y'} = -\tfrac12\varepsilon P$: the shear stress is half of the difference between the two pressures.

</Step>

</Step>

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3a-why" label="Both products vanish because of" choices={["the normalisation U·U = −1 of a 4-velocity, which is used once for each of the two fields", "the vanishing trace of F, which makes its contraction with any two vectors zero", "antisymmetry: an antisymmetric object contracted with two copies of U gives zero", "Maxwell's equations in empty space"]} answer={2} />

**The idea.** $F^{\alpha\beta}$ and $\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}$ are antisymmetric, and $U_\alpha U_\beta$ is symmetric. Their contraction is zero.

<Step title="The electric field">

$\vec E\cdot\vec U = E^\alpha U_\alpha = F^{\alpha\beta}U_\alpha U_\beta$. Exchange the names of the two summed indices and use $F^{\beta\alpha} = -F^{\alpha\beta}$:

<Formula label="Problem 3(a): the electric field is orthogonal to U" tex="F^{\alpha\beta}\,U_\alpha U_\beta = F^{\beta\alpha}\,U_\beta U_\alpha = -F^{\alpha\beta}\,U_\alpha U_\beta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \vec E\cdot\vec U = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The magnetic field">

$\vec B\cdot\vec U = -\tfrac12\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}U_\alpha U_\beta F_{\gamma\delta}$. The Levi-Civita tensor changes sign when $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are exchanged, so the same argument gives $\vec B\cdot\vec U = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In the rest frame of the observer $U^\alpha = (1, 0, 0, 0)$ and $U_\alpha = (-1, 0, 0, 0)$. Then $E^0 = F^{00}U_0 = 0$ and $E^i = F^{i0}U_0 = F^{0i}$, which is the electric field $E_i$ of the lesson's matrix (Lecture 4 transcript, p. 20). For the magnetic field, $B^0$ contains $\epsilon^{00\gamma\delta} = 0$, and $B^x = \tfrac12\left(\epsilon^{x0yz}F_{yz} + \epsilon^{x0zy}F_{zy}\right) = \epsilon^{x0yz}F_{yz} = F_{yz}$, because $\epsilon^{x0yz} = -\epsilon^{0xyz} = +1$. That is $B_x$ in the lesson's matrix. This last step uses the convention $\epsilon_{0123} = +1$ (so $\epsilon^{0123} = -1$) together with the minus sign in the definition of $B^\alpha$; with $\epsilon_{0123} = -1$ the definition would need a plus sign. The projection tensor gives $P^\alpha{}_\beta E^\beta = E^\alpha + U^\alpha\left(U_\beta E^\beta\right) = E^\alpha$.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="b" points={7}>

<Formula label="Problem 3(b): the magnetic field of the right-hand side" tex="-\frac12\,\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\,\epsilon_{\mu\nu\gamma\delta}\,U_\beta U^\mu B^\nu = -\left(\delta^\alpha{}_\nu\,\delta^\beta{}_\mu - \delta^\alpha{}_\mu\,\delta^\beta{}_\nu\right)U_\beta U^\mu B^\nu = -B^\alpha\left(U_\beta U^\beta\right) + U^\alpha\left(U_\beta B^\beta\right) = B^\alpha" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

<Exercise id="p4" title="How Christoffel symbols transform" points={15} mit="Problem 4">

</Step>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write the transport equation for the two components and solve the pair.

<Step title="The equations">

<Formula label="Problem 6(b): the transport equations on the circle" tex="\frac{dA^\theta}{d\phi} = \sin\theta_0\cos\theta_0\,A^\phi, \qquad \frac{dA^\phi}{d\phi} = -\cot\theta_0\,A^\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 3 (our own problem and solution)" />

Together they give $d^2A^\theta/d\phi^2 = -\cos^2\theta_0\,A^\theta$. With $A^\theta = 1$ and $A^\phi = 0$ (so $dA^\theta/d\phi = 0$) at $\phi = 0$:

<Formula label="Problem 6(b): the transported vector" tex="A^\theta = \cos\left(\phi\cos\theta_0\right), \qquad A^\phi = \frac{1}{\sin\theta_0\cos\theta_0}\,\frac{dA^\theta}{d\phi} = -\frac{\sin\left(\phi\cos\theta_0\right)}{\sin\theta_0}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 3 (our own problem and solution)" />

On the equator $\cos\theta_0 = 0$ and the components do not change: the equator is a geodesic, and the vector keeps its angle to it.

</Step>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

After one full turn, $\phi = 2\pi$, the vector is back at its starting point. For the circle $\theta_0 = 70^\circ$, give the value of $A^\theta$ after the full turn. What does the result say about the sphere?

<Hint>

Put $\phi = 2\pi$ into the result of part (b). The argument of the cosine is in radians.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6c-at" label="$A^\theta(2\pi) =$" value="-0.5465" tolerance="0.006" calc="p6c-at" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The vector comes back turned by the angle $2\pi\cos\theta_0$. On a flat surface it would come back unchanged.

<Step title="The number">

$\cos 70^\circ = 0.34202$, so the angle is $2\pi\times 0.34202 = 2.1490$ rad, or 123.13°. Then

<Formula label="Problem 6(c): the vector after one turn" tex="A^\theta\left(2\pi\right) = \cos\left(2\pi\cos 70^\circ\right) = \cos\left(2.1490\right) = -0.5465" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 3 (our own problem and solution)" />

and $A^\phi(2\pi) = -\sin(2.1490)/\sin 70^\circ = -0.8912$.

</Step>

<Step title="What it means">

The vector started with $A^\theta = 1$ and comes back to the same point with $A^\theta = -0.5465$: it is a different vector, though it was kept parallel to itself at every step. In the flat plane this cannot happen: the lesson on parallel transport carries a vector round a circle in the plane and finds it unchanged. The mismatch after a closed path shows that the surface of the sphere is curved. The later lessons on curvature turn this into the definition of the Riemann tensor.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Two limits. On the equator the angle is $2\pi\cos 90^\circ = 0$: no change, as for any geodesic that closes. Close to the pole $\cos\theta_0 \to 1$ and the angle tends to $2\pi$, a full turn of the components. That too is no change of the vector itself: the small circle lies in an almost flat patch, and it is the basis vectors that turn once round as $\phi$ goes from 0 to $2\pi$. The length after the turn is $\sqrt{0.5465^2 + (\sin 70^\circ\times 0.8912)^2} = 1$.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 7

<Exercise id="p7" title="Connection identities" points={15} mit="Problem 7">

Prove the six identities below. $\Gamma_{\mu\nu\lambda}$ is the Christoffel symbol with its first index lowered, $\Gamma_{\mu\nu\lambda} = g_{\mu\kappa}\Gamma^\kappa_{\nu\lambda}$, and $g$ is the determinant of the metric $g_{\mu\nu}$. Parts (d) to (f) hold in a coordinate basis and rely on the identity that Hughes proves in Lecture 9 (he calls it a party trick):

<Formula label="The party trick" tex="\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\lambda} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\partial_\lambda\sqrt{|g|} = \partial_\lambda\ln\sqrt{|g|}" cite="reading5" at="transcript p. 8 (proved on pp. 8–10)" />

<Part label="a" points={2}>

<Formula label="Problem 7(a)" tex="\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu} = \Gamma_{\mu\nu\lambda} + \Gamma_{\nu\mu\lambda}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(a), p. 3" />

<Hint>

Write out both lowered symbols with the formula in terms of derivatives of the metric, and add.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each lowered symbol is half a sum of three derivatives of the metric. In the sum of the two, four terms cancel.

<Step title="Add the two symbols">

The lesson's formula with the first index lowered is $\Gamma_{\mu\nu\lambda} = \tfrac12\left(\partial_\nu g_{\lambda\mu} + \partial_\lambda g_{\nu\mu} - \partial_\mu g_{\nu\lambda}\right)$ (transcript of Lecture 6, pp. 16–17). Exchange $\mu$ and $\nu$ for the second symbol and add:

<Formula label="Problem 7(a): the sum of the two symbols" tex="\Gamma_{\mu\nu\lambda} + \Gamma_{\nu\mu\lambda} = \tfrac12\left(\partial_\nu g_{\lambda\mu} + \partial_\lambda g_{\nu\mu} - \partial_\mu g_{\nu\lambda}\right) + \tfrac12\left(\partial_\mu g_{\lambda\nu} + \partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu} - \partial_\nu g_{\mu\lambda}\right) = \partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The first term of each bracket cancels the third term of the other, because the metric is symmetric.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This is the statement $\nabla_\lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 0$ of Lecture 6, written out: $\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu} - \Gamma^\kappa_{\lambda\mu}g_{\kappa\nu} - \Gamma^\kappa_{\lambda\nu}g_{\mu\kappa} = 0$. In plane polar coordinates, with $\mu = \nu = \phi$ and $\lambda = r$: $\partial_r g_{\phi\phi} = 2r$, and $2\,\Gamma_{\phi\phi r} = 2\,g_{\phi\phi}\Gamma^\phi_{\phi r} = 2r^2/r = 2r$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

<Formula label="Problem 7(b)" tex="g_{\mu\kappa}\,\partial_\lambda g^{\kappa\nu} = -g^{\kappa\nu}\,\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\kappa}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(b), p. 3" />

<Hint>

The metric and the inverse metric multiply to something that is the same everywhere.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Differentiate the statement that $g^{\kappa\nu}$ is the inverse of $g_{\mu\kappa}$.

<Step title="Differentiate the product">

$g_{\mu\kappa}g^{\kappa\nu} = \delta_\mu{}^\nu$, and the components of the Kronecker delta are constants. So

<Formula label="Problem 7(b): the derivative of the product" tex="0 = \partial_\lambda\!\left(g_{\mu\kappa}\,g^{\kappa\nu}\right) = g^{\kappa\nu}\,\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\kappa} + g_{\mu\kappa}\,\partial_\lambda g^{\kappa\nu}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

which is the identity.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In plane polar coordinates with $\mu = \nu = \phi$ and $\lambda = r$: the left side is $r^2\,\partial_r(1/r^2) = -2/r$, the right side is $-(1/r^2)\,\partial_r(r^2) = -2/r$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

<Formula label="Problem 7(c)" tex="\partial_\lambda g^{\mu\nu} = -\Gamma^\mu_{\lambda\kappa}\,g^{\kappa\nu} - \Gamma^\nu_{\lambda\kappa}\,g^{\kappa\mu}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(c), p. 3" />

Then say in words what this identity states.

<Hint>

Multiply identity (b) by the inverse metric to free $\partial_\lambda g^{\mu\nu}$, then use identity (a).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7c-means" label="Identity (c) says that" choices={["the partial derivatives of the inverse metric vanish in every system of coordinates", "the Christoffel symbols are the components of a tensor", "the inverse metric has a vanishing divergence only when it is diagonal", "the covariant derivative of the inverse metric vanishes"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Identity (b) trades a derivative of the inverse metric for a derivative of the metric, and identity (a) turns that into Christoffel symbols.

<Step title="Free the derivative of the inverse metric">

Multiply (b) by $g^{\sigma\mu}$ and sum over $\mu$. On the left $g^{\sigma\mu}g_{\mu\kappa} = \delta^\sigma{}_\kappa$:

<Formula label="Problem 7(c): the derivative of the inverse metric" tex="\partial_\lambda g^{\sigma\nu} = -g^{\sigma\mu}\,g^{\kappa\nu}\,\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\kappa} = -g^{\sigma\mu}\,g^{\kappa\nu}\left(\Gamma_{\mu\kappa\lambda} + \Gamma_{\kappa\mu\lambda}\right) = -g^{\kappa\nu}\,\Gamma^\sigma_{\kappa\lambda} - g^{\sigma\mu}\,\Gamma^\nu_{\mu\lambda}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The second step is identity (a), and the third raises the first index of each symbol.

</Step>

<Step title="Rename">

Call $\sigma$ now $\mu$, rename the summed index of the last term $\kappa$, and use the symmetry of the lower indices, $\Gamma^\mu_{\kappa\lambda} = \Gamma^\mu_{\lambda\kappa}$. The result is identity (c).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

By the rule for a tensor with two upper indices, $\nabla_\lambda g^{\mu\nu} = \partial_\lambda g^{\mu\nu} + \Gamma^\mu_{\lambda\kappa}g^{\kappa\nu} + \Gamma^\nu_{\lambda\kappa}g^{\mu\kappa}$ (transcript of Lecture 6, p. 12). Identity (c) says that this is zero: the covariant derivative of the inverse metric vanishes, as that of the metric does. In plane polar coordinates with $\mu = \nu = \phi$ and $\lambda = r$: $\partial_r(1/r^2) = -2/r^3$, and $-2\,\Gamma^\phi_{r\phi}\,g^{\phi\phi} = -2\,(1/r)(1/r^2) = -2/r^3$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

<Formula label="Problem 7(d)" tex="\nabla_\nu A_\mu{}^\nu = |g|^{-1/2}\,\partial_\nu\!\left(|g|^{1/2}A_\mu{}^\nu\right) - \Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\mu}\,A_\lambda{}^\nu" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(d), p. 3" />

<Hint>

Write out the covariant derivative of a tensor with one lower and one upper index: one Christoffel term with a minus sign for the lower index, one with a plus sign for the upper index. Then contract. Which of the two terms has the form $\Gamma^\nu_{\nu\lambda}$?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The correction for the upper index becomes a contracted Christoffel symbol, which the party trick turns into a derivative of $|g|^{1/2}$. The correction for the lower index stays.

<Step title="The covariant derivative">

The lesson's rules give one correction for each index (transcript of Lecture 6, pp. 10–12): $\nabla_\sigma A_\mu{}^\nu = \partial_\sigma A_\mu{}^\nu - \Gamma^\lambda_{\sigma\mu}A_\lambda{}^\nu + \Gamma^\nu_{\sigma\lambda}A_\mu{}^\lambda$. Set $\sigma = \nu$ and sum:

<Formula label="Problem 7(d): the contracted covariant derivative" tex="\nabla_\nu A_\mu{}^\nu = \partial_\nu A_\mu{}^\nu + \Gamma^\nu_{\nu\lambda}\,A_\mu{}^\lambda - \Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\mu}\,A_\lambda{}^\nu" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(d), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

<Step title="Use the party trick">

With $\Gamma^\nu_{\nu\lambda} = |g|^{-1/2}\partial_\lambda|g|^{1/2}$, and the summed index $\lambda$ renamed $\nu$:

<Formula label="Problem 7(d): the first two terms combine" tex="\partial_\nu A_\mu{}^\nu + |g|^{-1/2}\left(\partial_\nu|g|^{1/2}\right)A_\mu{}^\nu = |g|^{-1/2}\,\partial_\nu\!\left(|g|^{1/2}A_\mu{}^\nu\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(d), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

by the product rule. Together with the last term this is identity (d).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Without the index $\mu$ the last term is absent and the identity becomes the lesson's divergence of a vector, $\nabla_\nu V^\nu = |g|^{-1/2}\partial_\nu(|g|^{1/2}V^\nu)$. The words "in a coordinate basis" matter: the party trick, and the formula for $\Gamma$ in terms of derivatives of the metric behind it, hold for the coordinate basis vectors of the lessons.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={3}>

<Formula label="Problem 7(e)" tex="\nabla_\nu F^{\mu\nu} = |g|^{-1/2}\,\partial_\nu\!\left(|g|^{1/2}F^{\mu\nu}\right) \qquad \text{if } F^{\mu\nu} = -F^{\nu\mu}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(e), p. 3" />

<Hint>

Two upper indices give two Christoffel terms with plus signs. One becomes a contracted symbol. In the other, a symmetric pair of indices meets an antisymmetric pair.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** As in (d), but the left-over Christoffel term vanishes by antisymmetry.

<Step title="The contracted covariant derivative">

<Formula label="Problem 7(e): the divergence of a tensor with two upper indices" tex="\nabla_\nu F^{\mu\nu} = \partial_\nu F^{\mu\nu} + \Gamma^\mu_{\nu\lambda}\,F^{\lambda\nu} + \Gamma^\nu_{\nu\lambda}\,F^{\mu\lambda}" cite="reading3" at="transcript p. 12 (the rule for two upper indices, contracted here)" />

In the middle term $\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\lambda}$ is symmetric in $\nu\lambda$ and $F^{\lambda\nu}$ is antisymmetric, so the sum over both indices is zero. The last term is a contracted symbol; with the party trick it combines with the first term exactly as in part (d):

<Formula label="Problem 7(e): the result" tex="\nabla_\nu F^{\mu\nu} = \partial_\nu F^{\mu\nu} + |g|^{-1/2}\left(\partial_\lambda|g|^{1/2}\right)F^{\mu\lambda} = |g|^{-1/2}\,\partial_\nu\!\left(|g|^{1/2}F^{\mu\nu}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(e), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Hughes makes the same point in Lecture 9 (transcript pp. 12–13): for a general tensor a Christoffel term is left over, and for an antisymmetric one it dies. The identity is what makes Maxwell's equations simple in curved coordinates: $\nabla_\nu F^{\mu\nu} = 4\pi J^\mu$ needs only $|g|^{1/2}$ and partial derivatives. For a symmetric tensor such as the stress-energy tensor the middle term does not vanish.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="f" points={3}>

For a scalar function $S$:

<Formula label="Problem 7(f)" tex="\Box S \equiv g^{\mu\nu}\,\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu S = |g|^{-1/2}\,\partial_\mu\!\left(|g|^{1/2}g^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_\nu S\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(f), p. 3" />

Our addition: apply it to flat spacetime in the coordinates $(t, r, \theta, \phi)$, with the metric $\mathrm{diag}(-1,\ 1,\ r^2,\ r^2\sin^2\theta)$, and to the function $S = r^n$ with a constant $n$. Type `n` and `r`.

<Hint>

The covariant derivative of a scalar is its partial derivative, and $V^\mu = g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu S$ is a vector. The metric can be moved through a covariant derivative.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the box: $|g|^{1/2} = r^2\sin\theta$, and $S$ depends on $r$ only, so only the term with $\mu = \nu = r$ is left.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7f-box" label="$\Box\,r^n =$" expr="n*(n + 1)*r^(n - 2)"
  vars={{ n: [1.5, 4], r: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[

**The idea.** $\Box S$ is the divergence of the gradient vector, and the divergence of a vector has the simple form with $|g|^{1/2}$.

<Step title="A divergence of a vector">

For a scalar, $\nabla_\nu S = \partial_\nu S$. The covariant derivative of the inverse metric vanishes (identity (c)), so $g^{\mu\nu}$ can be moved inside: $g^{\mu\nu}\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu S = \nabla_\mu\left(g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu S\right) = \nabla_\mu V^\mu$ with the vector $V^\mu = g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu S$.
