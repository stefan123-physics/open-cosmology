This is the lecture in which the mathematics of the first eleven lectures becomes a theory of gravity. Hughes first squeezes one more identity out of the Riemann tensor, which produces a curvature tensor with exactly the right properties, and then follows the route Einstein himself took in 1915: carry the laws of special relativity into curved spacetime, and insist that the result reduces to Newton's gravity where Newton works. The page uses Hughes's conventions: metric signature $(-, +, +, +)$, $c = 1$ except where stated, but $G$ kept visible (transcript p. 18).

## The Riemann tensor so far

Hughes opens with a recap of the previous two lectures (transcript pp. 1–3). With all indices down, the Riemann tensor has four symmetries: it is antisymmetric in its last pair of indices, antisymmetric in its first pair, symmetric under swapping the two pairs, and the cyclic sum over its last three indices vanishes:

<Formula label="Symmetries of the Riemann tensor" tex="R_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu} = -R_{\alpha\beta\nu\mu} = -R_{\beta\alpha\mu\nu} = R_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}, \qquad R_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu} + R_{\alpha\mu\nu\beta} + R_{\alpha\nu\beta\mu} = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 1–2" />

Tracing on the first and third indices gives the symmetric **Ricci tensor**, and tracing once more gives the **Ricci scalar**:

<Formula label="Ricci tensor and Ricci scalar" tex="R_{\mu\nu} = R^{\alpha}{}_{\mu\alpha\nu} = R_{\nu\mu}, \qquad R = g^{\mu\nu} R_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 2" />

The physical meaning of the Riemann tensor is geodesic deviation. Two neighbouring freely falling bodies separated by a small vector $\xi^\mu$ accelerate apart or together, with a relative acceleration set by the curvature:

<Formula label="Geodesic deviation" tex="\frac{D^2\xi^\alpha}{d\tau^2} = R^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\mu\nu}\, u^\beta u^\mu \xi^\nu" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 2" />

Free fall is gravity, so a change in the separation of two free-fall paths is a **tidal** effect: the Riemann tensor describes gravitational tides. Finally, the Riemann tensor obeys the **Bianchi identity**, a statement about its covariant derivative in which the last two indices stay put while the derivative index and the first two indices are permuted cyclically (transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="The Bianchi identity" tex="\nabla_{\alpha} R_{\beta\gamma\mu\nu} + \nabla_{\beta} R_{\gamma\alpha\mu\nu} + \nabla_{\gamma} R_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu} = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3" />

## Contracting the Bianchi identity

Four indices are a lot to handle, so Hughes contracts (transcript pp. 3–4). Multiply the identity by $g^{\beta\mu}$. Because the covariant derivative of the metric is zero, the metric passes through each derivative. The first term becomes a derivative of the Ricci tensor. In the second the metric only raises the derivative index, leaving the divergence of the Riemann tensor on its third index. In the third the trace falls on the second and third indices; swapping the first two (a minus sign, by antisymmetry) turns it into a Ricci tensor:

<Formula label="Bianchi identity contracted once" tex="\nabla_{\alpha} R_{\gamma\nu} + \nabla^{\mu} R_{\gamma\alpha\mu\nu} - \nabla_{\gamma} R_{\alpha\nu} = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 3–4" />

Contract again, with $g^{\gamma\nu}$ (transcript p. 4). The first term becomes the derivative of the Ricci scalar. In the second the trace is on the first and fourth indices; antisymmetry in the last pair makes it minus a Ricci tensor. The third becomes a divergence of the Ricci tensor:

<Formula label="Bianchi identity contracted twice" tex="\nabla_{\alpha} R - 2\,\nabla^{\mu} R_{\alpha\mu} = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 4" />

Both Ricci terms were divergences on the same (dummy) index, so they combine. Dividing by $-2$ and pulling the derivative out in front, which needs a metric to line up the indices, gives the result Hughes was after:

<Formula label="A divergence-free curvature tensor" tex="\nabla^{\mu}\left(R_{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{2} g_{\mu\nu} R\right) = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 4" />

The combination in brackets gets a name, the **Einstein tensor**:

<Formula label="The Einstein tensor" tex="G_{\mu\nu} \equiv R_{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{2} g_{\mu\nu} R, \qquad \nabla^{\mu} G_{\mu\nu} = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 4" />

This holds for every metric whatsoever: it is an identity, not yet physics.

One side note (transcript pp. 4–5). The trace of the metric, $g^{\mu\nu}g_{\mu\nu} = \delta^\mu{}_\mu$, is the number of spacetime dimensions, here 4, because the metric with indices up is the matrix inverse of the metric with indices down. So the trace of the Einstein tensor is

<Formula label="Trace of the Einstein tensor" tex="G \equiv g^{\mu\nu} G_{\mu\nu} = R - \frac{1}{2}\cdot 4\,R = -R" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 4–5" />

The Einstein tensor is the **trace-reversed** Ricci tensor. This fact is set aside for now and used below.

## Ingredient 1: minimal coupling

To build a theory of gravity, Hughes needs two ingredients. The first is the principle of equivalence in the form called the **minimal coupling principle** (transcript pp. 5–7): carry the laws of physics that work in the laboratory over to curved spacetime with as little extra coupling to curvature as possible. The recipe has three steps.

1. Take a law that holds in inertial coordinates in flat spacetime, or equivalently in a local Lorentz frame, the small neighbourhood of a freely falling observer.
2. Rewrite it in a coordinate-invariant, tensorial form.
3. Assert that the tensor law holds in curved spacetime.

This is why the course has insisted on writing everything with tensors. The derivation of geodesic deviation in the previous lecture already followed the recipe: a simple acceleration in the freely falling frame was replaced by a tensorial acceleration operator. Whether the recipe is right is decided by experiment, and Hughes notes that it has passed every test so far (transcript p. 6).

Two examples. A freely falling body feels no acceleration in its local Lorentz frame, $d^2x^\mu/d\tau^2 = 0$; the tensorial version that says the same thing in that frame is the geodesic equation. Local conservation of energy and momentum, a vanishing divergence of the stress-energy tensor, becomes a vanishing covariant divergence (transcript pp. 6–7):

<Formula label="Free fall and conservation, made tensorial" tex="u^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu} u^{\nu} = 0, \qquad \nabla_{\mu} T^{\mu\nu} = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 6–7" />

## Ingredient 2: a field equation with the right Newtonian limit

Everything so far describes how bodies move in a curved spacetime; nothing yet says where the curvature comes from. The second ingredient is a **field equation** connecting the geometry to its sources of energy and momentum (transcript p. 7). This lecture finds it the way Einstein did, by demanding agreement with Newtonian gravity in the appropriate limit; the next lecture repeats the job with an action principle. A new theory may change the whole picture (curvature of the manifold of events in place of a potential filling space), but it must still predict what Newton predicts where Newton has been tested.

Newtonian gravity has two parts: a field equation, Poisson's equation, written with the Kronecker delta so that it looks like a trace over a matrix of derivatives, and an equation of motion (transcript p. 8):

<Formula label="Newtonian gravity" tex="\delta^{ij}\,\partial_i\partial_j\Phi = 4\pi G\rho, \qquad \frac{d^2x^i}{dt^2} = -\delta^{ij}\,\partial_j\Phi" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 8" />

Hughes calls this way of writing the Laplace operator only "semi" coordinate-invariant: the Newtonian limit works only when the sources vary slowly enough that time derivatives can be ignored, and Newton's theory was never tested with rapidly varying sources (transcript p. 8). [He says "4π ρ" aloud, leaving out the $G$; the right-hand side is $4\pi G\rho$.] Later he swaps $\delta^{ij}$ for $\eta^{ij}$, the spatial part of the flat metric, which has the same components but looks like a piece of a spacetime tensor (transcript p. 11).

## The equation of motion: g₀₀ = −(1 + 2Φ)

Start from the geodesic equation (transcript pp. 8–9). Every test of Newtonian gravity involves speeds far below light, so $dt/d\tau$ dwarfs the spatial components $dx^i/d\tau$, and only the $00$ Christoffel symbol survives:

<Formula label="Geodesic equation for slow motion" tex="\frac{d^2x^{\mu}}{d\tau^2} + \Gamma^{\mu}{}_{00}\left(\frac{dt}{d\tau}\right)^2 = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 9" />

The Newtonian fields are also static, so the two time derivatives in the Christoffel symbol drop out. Now write the metric as flat spacetime plus a small correction, every component of which is much smaller than 1 (transcript pp. 9–10). Gravity cannot be very different from special relativity in the systems Newton studied, or special relativity could never have been discovered. To first order the inverse metric has the same form, with indices raised by $\eta$:

<Formula label="Weak-field metric and its inverse" tex="g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}, \quad |h_{\mu\nu}| \ll 1; \qquad g^{\mu\nu} = \eta^{\mu\nu} - h^{\mu\nu} + O(h^2), \quad h^{\mu\nu} = \eta^{\mu\alpha}\eta^{\nu\beta}h_{\alpha\beta}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 9–10" />

The derivative of $\eta$ vanishes, so only $h$ is differentiated, and terms of order $h^2$ are dropped:

<Formula label="The Christoffel symbol in the Newtonian limit" tex="\Gamma^{\mu}{}_{00} = -\frac{1}{2} g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu} g_{00} = -\frac{1}{2}\eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu} h_{00}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 9–10" />

For $\mu = 0$ this is a time derivative, which vanishes, so $d^2t/d\tau^2 = 0$. For the spatial components, dividing by $(dt/d\tau)^2$ (transcript p. 10):

<Formula label="Weak-field equation of motion" tex="\frac{d^2t}{d\tau^2} = 0, \qquad \frac{d^2x^{i}}{dt^2} = \frac{1}{2}\,\partial_i h_{00}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 10" />

This is Newton's equation of motion exactly, provided (transcript pp. 10–11)

<Formula label="The metric in the Newtonian limit" tex="h_{00} = -2\Phi, \qquad g_{00} = -(1 + 2\Phi)" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 10–11" />

## Choosing the field equation

Now the field equation (transcript pp. 11–12). Poisson's equation is not tensorial: the mass density $\rho$, up to a factor $c^2$ the energy density, is not a scalar but one component of the stress-energy tensor, and singling out one component of a tensor is bad mathematics and bad physics. So the right-hand side should be the whole of $T_{\mu\nu}$; presumably one component dominated in every situation that shaped Newton's theory.

On the left, the metric plays the role of the potential, and Poisson's equation has two derivatives of the potential. Two derivatives of the metric make a curvature. The Riemann tensor has too many indices to match $T_{\mu\nu}$. The Ricci tensor has the right number, but the left-hand side must also share the other known property of $T_{\mu\nu}$: local conservation, $\nabla^{\mu} T_{\mu\nu} = 0$, demands a **divergence-free** two-index curvature tensor (transcript p. 12). The contracted Bianchi identity has just produced one. So try

<Formula label="Proposed field equation" tex="G_{\mu\nu} = \kappa\,T_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 12" />

with a constant $\kappa$ to fix the dimensions and the Newtonian limit. Because $G_{\mu\nu}$ is the trace-reversed Ricci tensor, the same equation can be trace-reversed, with $T = g^{\mu\nu}T_{\mu\nu}$; this form makes the next calculation easier (transcript p. 13):

<Formula label="The same equation, trace-reversed" tex="R_{\mu\nu} = \kappa\left(T_{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{2} g_{\mu\nu} T\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 13" />

## Fixing the constant: κ = 8πG

For the source, take a static perfect fluid (transcript pp. 13–14):

<Formula label="Perfect-fluid stress-energy tensor" tex="T_{\mu\nu} = (\rho + P)\,u_\mu u_\nu + P\,g_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 13" />

With $c$ restored, $\rho$ is really $\rho c^2$, so in Newtonian situations $\rho \gg P$ and the pressure can be dropped. The fluid does not flow, so only $u^0$ is non-zero, but it is not simply 1: the normalisation $g_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu = -1$ with $g_{00} = -1 + h_{00}$ gives, to first order in $h$ (transcript p. 14),

<Formula label="Four-velocity of the static fluid" tex="u^0 = 1 + \frac{1}{2} h_{00}, \qquad u_0 = -\left(1 - \frac{1}{2} h_{00}\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 14" />

So $T_{00} = \rho\,u_0u_0 = \rho(1 - h_{00})$ and the trace is $T = g^{00}T_{00} \approx -\rho$. [On the board Hughes lowers the index with a sign change only, getting $u_0 = -(1 + \tfrac12 h_{00})$ and $T_{00} = \rho(1 + h_{00})$; the first-order correction should have the opposite sign, as written here. It makes no difference, because that correction is dropped next.] The $00$ component of the right-hand side is then

<Formula label="Right-hand side, 00 component" tex="T_{00} - \frac{1}{2} g_{00} T = \frac{1}{2}\rho + O(\rho\,h)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 14" />

and the term $\rho\,h$, small compared with $\rho$, is neglected. On the left, $R_{00}$ is the trace of the Riemann tensor on its first and third indices; the time part $R^0{}_{000}$ vanishes by antisymmetry, so only spatial indices are summed. Products of Christoffel symbols are of order $h^2$ and time derivatives vanish, which leaves one term (transcript pp. 14–15):

<Formula label="R₀₀ in the Newtonian limit" tex="R_{00} = R^{i}{}_{0i0} = \partial_i\Gamma^{i}{}_{00} = -\frac{1}{2}\delta^{ij}\partial_i\partial_j h_{00} = -\frac{1}{2}\nabla^2 h_{00}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 14–15" />

Putting the two sides together, $-\frac12\nabla^2 h_{00} = \frac12\kappa\rho$, so $\nabla^2 h_{00} = -\kappa\rho$. The equation of motion already said $h_{00} = -2\Phi$, which turns this into $\nabla^2\Phi = \kappa\rho/2$. Poisson's equation requires $4\pi G\rho$ on the right, so $\kappa = 8\pi G$ (transcript p. 15):

<Formula label="The Einstein field equation" tex="G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 15" />

<WorkedExample title="Checking the trace-reversed form">
<Problem>
Take the trace of $G_{\mu\nu} = \kappa T_{\mu\nu}$ and show that it is equivalent to $R_{\mu\nu} = \kappa(T_{\mu\nu} - \frac12 g_{\mu\nu}T)$. What does the field equation say about $R_{\mu\nu}$ in empty space?
</Problem>
<Step title="Trace both sides">
The trace of the left is $G = -R$ and of the right $\kappa T$, so $R = -\kappa T$.
</Step>
<Step title="Substitute back">
$R_{\mu\nu} = G_{\mu\nu} + \frac12 g_{\mu\nu}R = \kappa T_{\mu\nu} - \frac12 g_{\mu\nu}\kappa T$, the trace-reversed form. The steps run backwards just as well, so the two forms carry the same content.
</Step>
<Step title="Vacuum">
Where $T_{\mu\nu} = 0$, the trace-reversed form gives $R_{\mu\nu} = 0$ directly. The Riemann tensor need not vanish there: tides, described by geodesic deviation, exist outside a star even though the Ricci tensor is zero.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Einstein's route, and the cosmological constant

Hughes sums up (transcript pp. 15–16). The geodesic equation in a nearly flat spacetime with slow bodies gave $h_{00} = -2\Phi$; Poisson's equation, promoted to a tensor equation, needed a divergence-free two-index curvature tensor on the left and $T_{\mu\nu}$ on the right; and the Newtonian limit fixed the constant. This, in outline, is how Einstein reached the field equation, published in 1915. Special relativity dates from 1905, and Hughes points out that much of the decade between went into learning the differential geometry this course has covered in six or seven weeks, with help from mathematician colleagues.

The derivation is admittedly somewhat ad hoc (transcript pp. 16–17). Any divergence-free tensor could be added to one side without spoiling the Newtonian limit, as Einstein himself noted. The metric is one: its covariant derivative vanishes. With a constant $\Lambda$, the **cosmological constant**, to fix the dimensions:

<Formula label="The field equation with a cosmological constant" tex="G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda\,g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 17" />

The extra term can equally be moved to the right and read as a special source of stress-energy (transcript p. 17):

<Formula label="The cosmological constant as stress-energy" tex="T^{(\Lambda)}_{\mu\nu} = -\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi G}\,g_{\mu\nu}, \qquad \rho = \frac{\Lambda}{8\pi G}, \qquad P = -\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi G}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 17" />

In a freely falling frame, where $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu}$, this is a perfect fluid with energy density $\Lambda/8\pi G$ and pressure equal to minus that. [Hughes says "$\rho = 8\pi G$" at this point; the density is $\Lambda/8\pi G$, as the board's tensor gives.] Such a tensor arises in quantum field theory as the zero-point energy of the vacuum: an isotropic stress-energy tensor that looks the same in every local Lorentz frame must be proportional to the metric, an argument due to Yakov Zeldovich (transcript p. 17). Long a curiosity, the cosmological constant became much more relevant once observations of the large-scale universe, in the 15 or 20 years before this 2020 lecture, appeared to support it.

<Callout kind="cosmology">
The fluid with $P = -\rho$ is the vacuum energy of cosmology. In the Friedmann equations it drives accelerated expansion, which is how distant supernovae revealed it in 1998; 8.286 tells that story and the puzzle of its tiny size. The 8.962 cosmology lectures will solve $G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}$ for a homogeneous, isotropic universe.
</Callout>

## Units: setting G = 1, and how stiff spacetime is

Many relativists also set $G = 1$ (transcript p. 18). Hughes keeps it, and so does Carroll's textbook: it shows how terms couple and later serves to count orders. The reason for $G = 1$ is that $G$ is the most poorly measured fundamental constant. Hughes recalls five or six digits; the 2018 CODATA value, $6.67430\times10^{-11}$ in SI units, has a relative uncertainty of about $2\times10^{-5}$, so closer to four or five. Gravity measures the product $GM$, often far better: for the Sun to some nine or ten digits. Dividing by $G$ to get $M$ throws that precision away.

With $G = c = 1$, mass, length and time share one unit, and combinations of $G$ and $c$ become conversion factors (transcript pp. 18–19):

<Formula label="Mass as a length and as a time" tex="\frac{GM_\odot}{c^2} \approx 1.477\ \text{km}, \qquad \frac{GM_\odot}{c^3} \approx 4.925\times10^{-6}\ \text{s}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 18" />

[Hughes quotes 1.47 km and $4.92\times10^{-6}$ s; these are the same numbers cut off rather than rounded.] Likewise $G/c^4$ turns an energy into a length. A component of $T_{\mu\nu}$ is an energy per volume, so $G/c^4$ times it is one over a length squared, the dimension of curvature. With $G$ and $c$ written out, the field equation reads (transcript p. 19)

<Formula label="The field equation in SI units" tex="G_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 19" />

$G$ is small and $c^4$ is enormous: a great deal of stress-energy makes very little curvature. As Hughes puts it, spacetime is hard to bend.

<WorkedExample title="How much curvature does a glass of water make?">
<Problem>
Water has $\rho = 1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$, so $T_{00} = \rho c^2$. Estimate the curvature $8\pi G T_{00}/c^4$ it produces, and the corresponding radius of curvature. Compare the Earth's $GM/c^2$ with $M_\oplus = 5.97\times10^{24}$ kg.
</Problem>
<Step title="Curvature">
$8\pi G\rho/c^2 = 8\pi \times 6.674\times10^{-11} \times 1000 / (8.988\times10^{16}) \approx 1.87\times10^{-23}\ \text{m}^{-2}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Radius of curvature">
One over the square root: $(1.87\times10^{-23})^{-1/2} \approx 2.3\times10^{11}$ m, about one and a half times the Earth–Sun distance. A ball of water of exactly that radius would have $2GM/c^2$ equal to exactly a third of its radius: spacetime around it would be strongly curved, and a somewhat larger ball would be a black hole.
</Step>
<Step title="The Earth as a length">
$GM_\oplus/c^2 = 6.674\times10^{-11} \times 5.97\times10^{24} / (8.988\times10^{16}) \approx 4.43$ mm. Compared with the Earth's radius of 6371 km this is $7\times10^{-10}$: the size of $\Phi$, and of $h_{00}/2$, at the Earth's surface.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Contracting the Bianchi identity twice gives $\nabla^{\mu} G_{\mu\nu} = 0$ for the **Einstein tensor** $G_{\mu\nu} = R_{\mu\nu} - \frac12 g_{\mu\nu}R$, the trace-reversed Ricci tensor ($G = -R$).
- **Minimal coupling**: write a law of a local Lorentz frame in tensor form and assert it in curved spacetime; free fall becomes $u^\mu\nabla_{\mu} u^\nu = 0$, conservation becomes $\nabla_{\mu} T^{\mu\nu} = 0$.
- With $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}$, slow motion and static fields, the geodesic equation is Newton's law if $g_{00} = -(1 + 2\Phi)$.
- Promoting Poisson's equation to tensors needs a divergence-free curvature tensor: $G_{\mu\nu} = \kappa T_{\mu\nu}$. A static dust source gives $\nabla^2 h_{00} = -\kappa\rho$, so $\kappa = 8\pi G$: **$G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}$**, equivalently $R_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G(T_{\mu\nu} - \frac12 g_{\mu\nu}T)$.
- A term $\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$ may be added: the **cosmological constant**, equivalent to a fluid with $\rho = \Lambda/8\pi G$ and $P = -\rho$, the vacuum energy.
- With $G$ and $c$ restored the coupling is $8\pi G/c^4$; $GM_\odot/c^2 \approx 1.477$ km. A great deal of stress-energy makes little curvature.
