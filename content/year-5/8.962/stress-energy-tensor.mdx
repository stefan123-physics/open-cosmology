This lecture introduces what Hughes calls perhaps the most important quantity of the whole course, the stress-energy tensor: the object that describes energy and momentum, how they flow, and, eventually, what makes gravity. He builds it for dust, generalises it to the perfect fluid, argues that it must be symmetric and divergence-free, gives two more examples, and in the last minutes starts the "prelude to curvature" that leads to the Christoffel symbol in the next lecture. Units have $c = 1$; the signature is $(-, +, +, +)$.

## Recap: counting dust

Dust is a cloud of non-interacting particles. In the rest frame of a small element its number density is $n_0$, and the number-flux 4-vector is $\vec N = n_0\vec u$; its components give the density and flux in any frame (transcript pp. 1–2). Conservation of particle number is $\partial_\alpha N^\alpha = 0$ in every frame, and once a frame is chosen it becomes an integral balance between the particles inside a 3-volume and the flux through its boundary (transcript pp. 2–3).

## Energy density is a tensor component

Now give every dust particle the same rest mass $m$. In the rest frame of an element, each particle has rest energy $m$ (that is, $mc^2$), so the **rest energy density** is (transcript p. 3)

<Formula label="Rest energy density of dust" tex="\rho_0 = m\,n_0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3" />

In a frame where the element moves, each particle's energy is $\gamma m$, and the Lorentz contraction packs the particles into a smaller volume, so the number density is $\gamma n_0$. Both effects multiply (transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Energy density seen by a moving observer" tex="\rho = \left(\gamma m\right)\left(\gamma n_0\right) = \gamma^2\rho_0" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 3–4" />

Two factors of $\gamma$ cannot come from a 4-vector component, which changes linearly under a Lorentz transformation, and energy density is not a scalar either, since it differs between frames (transcript p. 4). It must be a component of a two-index tensor. It was built from two time components, the energy $p^0$ of the 4-momentum and the density $N^0$ of the number vector, so it is the $00$ component of their tensor product (transcript pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="Stress-energy tensor of dust" tex="T = \vec p\otimes\vec N = \rho_0\;\vec u\otimes\vec u, \qquad T^{\alpha\beta} = \rho_0\,u^\alpha u^\beta" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 5" />

using $\vec N = n_0\vec u$ and $\vec p = m\vec u$ (Hughes says "four-momentum" for $\vec u$ here; he means the 4-velocity). This is the **stress-energy tensor**. Its components can be read out by putting basis one-forms into its slots, and since basis one-forms catch fluxes, each component has a direct meaning (transcript p. 5):

> $T^{\alpha\beta}$ is the flux of 4-momentum component $\alpha$ in the $x^\beta$ direction.

Component by component (transcript pp. 5–6):

- $T^{00}$: the flux of energy in the time direction, which is the **energy density**: energy just sitting there, "flowing through time", until someone brings a cup of anti-water.
- $T^{0i}$: energy flowing in the $x^i$ direction, the **energy flux**.
- $T^{i0}$: momentum component $i$ flowing in the time direction, the **momentum density**. (Hughes labels this one $T^{0i}$ too in the moment; he means $T^{i0}$.)
- $T^{ij}$: momentum component $i$ flowing in the $x^j$ direction, the **momentum flux** or stress.

This reading holds for every stress-energy tensor, not just dust (transcript pp. 6–7). For dust moving with velocity $\vec v$, using $u^\alpha = (\gamma, \gamma\vec v)$ (transcript p. 7):

<Formula label="Components of the dust stress-energy tensor" tex="T^{00} = \gamma^2\rho_0, \qquad T^{0i} = T^{i0} = \gamma^2\rho_0\,v^i, \qquad T^{ij} = \gamma^2\rho_0\,v^iv^j" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 7" />

Energy flux and momentum density are the same. Hughes stresses that this is true in all of physics; it is hidden in everyday units by factors of $c$ and because rest energy is usually left out of energy flows (transcript pp. 7–8). The tensor is also symmetric, obviously so for dust, since it is built from $\vec u$ twice; an argument below says it must be symmetric for any matter (transcript p. 8).

<WorkedExample title="A stream of dust">
<Problem>
Dust with rest energy density $\rho_0 = 2$ (in some unit) streams along $x$ at $v = 0.6$. Find $T^{00}$, $T^{0x}$ and $T^{xx}$, and check them with an invariant.
</Problem>
<Step title="The factor">
$\gamma = 1.25$, so $\gamma^2 = 1.5625$ and $\gamma^2\rho_0 = 3.125$.
</Step>
<Step title="The components">
$T^{00} = 3.125$, $T^{0x} = T^{x0} = 3.125 \times 0.6 = 1.875$, $T^{xx} = 3.125 \times 0.36 = 1.125$. The energy density is more than 1.5 times the rest value.
</Step>
<Step title="An invariant check">
Contract with the metric: $\eta_{\alpha\beta}T^{\alpha\beta} = -T^{00} + T^{xx} = -3.125 + 1.125 = -2$. For dust this trace is $\rho_0\,\vec u\cdot\vec u = -\rho_0$, the same in every frame.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The perfect fluid

Rather than a general recipe (which comes later, from varying a Lagrangian density as in field theory), Hughes builds stress-energy tensors by going to a suitable rest frame and thinking about what each component means (transcript pp. 8–9). The workhorse of astrophysics is the **perfect fluid** (transcript p. 9). It is perfect in two ways: there is a frame (its "rest" frame) in which no energy flows, and there are no lateral stresses, $T^{ij} = 0$ for $i \ne j$. Lateral stress, momentum carried sideways, is what viscosity describes, so a perfect fluid has no viscosity: it would not even make your hand wet, which is why it has been called the physics of "dry water" (transcript pp. 9–10).

What remains is the energy density $\rho$ and the pressure $P$, an isotropic spatial stress. With no energy flux there is, by symmetry, no momentum density either (transcript p. 10):

<Formula label="Perfect fluid in its rest frame" tex="T^{\alpha\beta} = \mathrm{diag}\left(\rho,\ P,\ P,\ P\right) \quad \text{in the rest frame}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 10" />

Dust is the perfect fluid with $P = 0$. The pressure will turn out to generate gravity too, alongside energy (transcript p. 10). These are components in one frame (Hughes marks them with his dotted equals sign); to write the tensor in any frame, note that the $\rho$ part is the energy density times $\vec u\otimes\vec u$, and the pressure part lives in the directions orthogonal to $\vec u$, which the projection tensor $\eta^{\alpha\beta} + u^\alpha u^\beta$ (built on the first problem set) picks out (transcript pp. 10–11):

<Formula label="Perfect fluid in any frame" tex="T^{\alpha\beta} = \rho\,u^\alpha u^\beta + P\left(\eta^{\alpha\beta} + u^\alpha u^\beta\right) = \left(\rho + P\right)u^\alpha u^\beta + P\,\eta^{\alpha\beta}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 11" />

Hughes flags this formula for long-term memory: through the principle of equivalence, two lectures on, it will describe perfect fluids in any spacetime, with only the meaning of the metric changed (transcript p. 11).

## What this means for gravity

Newtonian gravity has a field equation, Poisson's equation for the potential $\Phi_g$, with the mass density, or with $c = 1$ the energy density, as its source (transcript p. 12):

<Formula label="Newtonian field equation" tex="\nabla^2\Phi_g = 4\pi G\rho" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 12" />

That should make us suspicious: $\rho$ is one component of a tensor. A theory that picks out one component of a geometric object is unhealthy, like a version of electromagnetism with one set of Maxwell's equations for $E_x$ and another for $E_y$ (transcript p. 12). So in a relativistic theory the whole stress-energy tensor must be the source of gravity. The other side of the equation, built from two derivatives of a potential-like object that will turn out to be the metric itself, is the subject of the lectures up to spring break (transcript pp. 12–13).

## Why the tensor is symmetric

Hughes motivates (he insists it is not a proof) why $T^{ij}$ must be symmetric (transcript pp. 13–16). Take a small cube of side $l$ in some material. $T^{ij}$ is the flux of momentum, so the force through a face is the momentum flux times the area: on the face normal to $x$ it is $F^i = T^{ix}l^2$, on the face normal to $y$ it is $T^{iy}l^2$, and the opposite faces carry nearly equal and opposite forces, so there is no net force (transcript p. 14). (Hughes relabels the faces mid-way to stay in step with his notes.) The forces do produce a torque about the vertical axis through the centre (transcript p. 15):

<Formula label="Torque and moment of inertia of a small cube" tex="\tau_z \sim l^3\left(T^{xy} - T^{yx}\right), \qquad I \sim \alpha\,\rho\,l^5" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 15" />

where $\alpha$ is a number set by the geometry. The torque vanishes as $l \to 0$, but the moment of inertia vanishes faster, so the angular acceleration grows without bound for small cubes (transcript pp. 15–16):

<Formula label="Angular acceleration of the cube" tex="\ddot\theta = \frac{\tau_z}{I} \sim \frac{T^{xy} - T^{yx}}{\alpha\,\rho\,l^2}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 15" />

Microscopic eddies would spin up spontaneously in every cup of water. To avoid that absurdity, $T^{xy} = T^{yx}$, and the same argument about the other axes makes all of $T^{ij}$ symmetric; the time-space components are symmetric because energy flux equals momentum density (transcript p. 16). The field-theory derivation makes the symmetry manifest (transcript p. 16).

## Conservation of energy and momentum

Because $T^{\alpha\beta}$ describes the flow of energy and momentum, one short equation expresses the conservation of both (transcript p. 16):

<Formula label="Conservation of energy and momentum" tex="\partial_\beta T^{\alpha\beta} = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 16" />

Which part is energy and which is momentum? That cannot be said in general: energy and momentum are defined only after a frame is chosen, and one observer's energy is another's mixture of energy and momentum (transcript pp. 16–17). In a chosen frame (transcript p. 17):

<Formula label="Energy and momentum conservation in a chosen frame" tex="\frac{\partial T^{00}}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial T^{0i}}{\partial x^i} = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial T^{i0}}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial T^{ij}}{\partial x^j} = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 17" />

and, integrating over a 3-volume as was done for the number vector, the energy inside changes only by what flows through the boundary (transcript pp. 17–18):

<Formula label="Integral form of energy conservation" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V_3} T^{00}\,d^3x = -\oint_{\partial V_3} T^{0i}\,dA_i" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 17" />

with $T^{j0}$ and $T^{ji}$ in place of $T^{00}$ and $T^{0i}$ for momentum component $j$ (transcript p. 18).

## Two more examples

**A point particle** (transcript pp. 18–20). Much of Hughes's own research treats a binary as a black hole with a small, particle-like companion. A particle of rest mass $m_0$ moves on a world line $z^\alpha(\tau)$; its stress-energy is concentrated on that line, so it needs a Dirac delta function, as the charge density of a point charge does. Hughes writes it on the board without reading it out; in standard form it is

<Formula label="Stress-energy tensor of a point particle" tex="T^{\alpha\beta}(x) = m_0\int d\tau\;u^\alpha u^\beta\;\delta^4\!\left(x - z(\tau)\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 19" />

The four-dimensional delta is a product of four one-dimensional ones. Using $\int f(x)\,\delta(g(x))\,dx = f(x_0)/|g'(x_0)|$ at the zero $x_0$ of $g$ on the time delta, and $dz^0/d\tau = u^0$, the integral can be done (transcript pp. 19–20):

<Formula label="Point particle after the proper-time integral" tex="T^{\alpha\beta}(t, \vec x) = \frac{m_0\,u^\alpha u^\beta}{u^0}\;\delta^3\!\left(\vec x - \vec z(t)\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 20" />

The factor $1/u^0$ acts like a $1/\gamma$ and gives the tensor the right transformation properties (transcript p. 20).

**The electromagnetic field** (transcript pp. 20–21). In Gaussian units with $c = 1$, written with the Faraday tensor of the last lecture, the standard form (which Hughes quotes on the board) is

<Formula label="Stress-energy tensor of the electromagnetic field" tex="T^{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{4\pi}\left(F^{\mu\alpha}F^{\nu}{}_{\alpha} - \frac14\,\eta^{\mu\nu}F^{\alpha\beta}F_{\alpha\beta}\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 20" />

In a given frame its components are familiar (transcript pp. 20–21): the energy density, the Poynting vector as energy flux, and the momentum flux, which Hughes quotes from Griffiths and Jackson (it is minus the Maxwell stress tensor as Griffiths defines it):

<Formula label="Its components in a frame" tex="T^{00} = \frac{E^2 + B^2}{8\pi}, \qquad T^{0i} = \frac{\left(\vec E\times\vec B\right)^i}{4\pi}, \qquad T^{ij} = \frac{1}{4\pi}\left[\frac12\,\delta^{ij}\left(E^2 + B^2\right) - E^iE^j - B^iB^j\right]" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 20–21" />

Between the plates of a parallel-plate capacitor, with a uniform field $E$ along $x$ and no magnetic field, there is no energy flow; $T^{00} = E^2/8\pi$, $T^{xx} = -E^2/8\pi$ is a tension that pulls the plates together, and $T^{yy} = T^{zz} = +E^2/8\pi$ is a pressure sideways (transcript p. 21). Very different from a perfect fluid: fields produce anisotropic pressures, and in strong fields they contribute to gravity (transcript p. 21).

## Prelude to curvature: polar coordinates

Flat spacetime, Hughes will argue in a couple of lectures, is not enough for gravity: some notion of curvature is needed, and the tools for it come first (transcript p. 22). The gentle start is special relativity in curvilinear coordinates, where the physics is unchanged but the basis vectors are no longer constant. Take plane polar coordinates, $x = r\cos\phi$, $y = r\sin\phi$, and keep a coordinate basis, $d\vec x = dr\,\vec e_r + d\phi\,\vec e_\phi$ (transcript pp. 22–23). Since $d\phi$ is an angle, $\vec e_\phi$ must carry a length; from $x = r\cos\phi$, $y = r\sin\phi$ it is $(-r\sin\phi, r\cos\phi)$, and

<Formula label="The angular basis vector is not a unit vector" tex="\vec e_\phi\cdot\vec e_\phi = r^2 \neq 1" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 23" />

so the basis is not normalised (transcript p. 23). For transformations between coordinate systems in general, not just between Lorentz frames, Hughes introduces a new symbol, keeping $\Lambda$ for Lorentz transformations (transcript p. 23):

<Formula label="Transformation matrix between coordinate systems" tex="L^{\alpha}{}_{\bar\mu} = \frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial x^{\bar\mu}}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 23" />

With the barred coordinates polar and the unbarred Cartesian, the matrix Hughes writes and its inverse are, worked out from $x = r\cos\phi$, $y = r\sin\phi$ (transcript p. 24),

<Formula label="Polar to Cartesian and back" tex="L^{\alpha}{}_{\bar\mu} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\phi & -r\sin\phi \\ \sin\phi & r\cos\phi \end{pmatrix}, \qquad L^{\bar\mu}{}_{\alpha} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\phi & \sin\phi \\ -\sin\phi/r & \cos\phi/r \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 24" />

where rows are labelled by the upper index ($x$, $y$ on the left; $r$, $\phi$ on the right) and the second matrix is the inverse of the first. Different entries have different dimensions, a feature of the coordinate basis rather than a bug, and as the next lecture shows, the metric in these coordinates is no longer constant: that reflects basis vectors that change from point to point, which is where the Christoffel symbol comes in (transcript p. 24).

<Callout kind="cosmology">
The cosmic fluid of 8.286 is a perfect fluid: matter is dust with $P = 0$, radiation has $P = \rho/3$, and vacuum energy has $P = -\rho$. Applied to the expanding universe, $\partial_\beta T^{\alpha\beta} = 0$ in its curved-spacetime form gives the energy-conservation (fluid) equation $\dot\rho = -3(\dot a/a)(\rho + P)$, and the pressure, as Hughes says here, also acts as a source of gravity in the Friedmann acceleration equation.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Dust has rest energy density $\rho_0 = mn_0$ and energy density $\gamma^2\rho_0$ for a moving observer, so energy density is a component of a tensor: the **stress-energy tensor** $T^{\alpha\beta} = \rho_0 u^\alpha u^\beta$.
- $T^{\alpha\beta}$ is the flux of $p^\alpha$ in the $x^\beta$ direction: energy density $T^{00}$, energy flux $T^{0i}$, momentum density $T^{i0}$ (equal to it), stress $T^{ij}$.
- A **perfect fluid** has $T^{\alpha\beta} = \mathrm{diag}(\rho, P, P, P)$ in its rest frame and $T^{\alpha\beta} = (\rho + P)u^\alpha u^\beta + P\eta^{\alpha\beta}$ in general; dust is $P = 0$.
- Newton's source $\rho$ is one component of $T$, so relativistic gravity must be sourced by all of $T^{\alpha\beta}$, pressure included.
- A torque argument motivates $T^{\alpha\beta} = T^{\beta\alpha}$; $\partial_\beta T^{\alpha\beta} = 0$ expresses conservation of energy and momentum together.
- A point particle has $T^{\alpha\beta} = m_0 u^\alpha u^\beta\,\delta^3(\vec x - \vec z(t))/u^0$; the electromagnetic field has energy density $(E^2 + B^2)/8\pi$, the Poynting flux and the Maxwell stresses.
- In polar coordinates the coordinate basis is not normalised ($\vec e_\phi\cdot\vec e_\phi = r^2$), and $L^\alpha{}_{\bar\mu} = \partial x^\alpha/\partial x^{\bar\mu}$ describes general coordinate changes.
