Lecture 19 finishes cosmology. Lecture 18 built the Friedmann–Robertson–Walker (FRW) models; this lecture asks how to **compare them with the sky**. Everything a model predicts is bound up in one function, the scale factor $a(t)$, so the task is to find observable stand-ins for $a$ and for $t$. Hughes uses a new tool for the first (a Killing tensor), and distances for the second. As before, $c = 1$ and the signature is $(-,+,+,+)$.

## Recap: a recipe for a model universe

Hughes recaps the Robertson–Walker metric in its two forms (with $\bar r$, or with $\chi$ and $\bar r = \sin\chi$, $\chi$ or $\sinh\chi$), the Friedmann equations F1 and F2, energy conservation as the first law of thermodynamics, $\rho_\text{crit} = 3H^2/8\pi G$ and $\Omega + \Omega_\text{curv} = 1$ (transcript pp. 1–3). Then he writes a recipe for building a model universe:

1. Pick the spatial curvature, $k = -1$, $0$ or $+1$.
2. Pick a mixture of species, $\rho = \sum_i \rho_i$.
3. Give each species an equation of state, usually $P_i = w_i\rho_i$. Local energy conservation then gives each its own dilution law,

<Formula label="Dilution of each species" tex="\rho_i = \rho_{i,0}\,a^{-n_i}, \qquad n_i = 3\left(1 + w_i\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3; the exponent nᵢ named on p. 13" />

4. Solve the Friedmann equations, numerically if the mixture is complicated.

The model then predicts $a(t)$. To test it we need to measure $a$ for something we see, and to know which $t$ to attach to it (p. 4).

## A hint from radiation

Lecture 18 found that matter dilutes as $a^{-3}$ (the number of particles is fixed) and radiation as $a^{-4}$. The extra power is explained if the energy of each quantum (photon, graviton, "whateveron") falls as $1/a$. That suggests the scale factor is tied to redshift (p. 5). Hughes now makes the argument rigorous.

## Killing tensors

Recall that a Killing vector $\xi^\mu$ is one along which the Lie derivative of the metric vanishes, which is equivalent to the symmetrised covariant derivative vanishing. A **Killing tensor** generalises this to more indices. For rank two:

<Formula label="Killing vector and Killing tensor" tex="\nabla_\mu\xi_\nu + \nabla_\nu\xi_\mu = 0, \qquad \nabla_\alpha K_{\mu\nu} + \nabla_\mu K_{\nu\alpha} + \nabla_\nu K_{\alpha\mu} = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 5–6" />

and for rank $n$ the gradient is symmetrised over all $n + 1$ indices. A Killing vector is simply a Killing tensor of rank one. Contract the Killing tensor with the tangent $p^\mu$ of a geodesic, once for each index. The result is conserved along the geodesic:

<Formula label="The conserved quantity" tex="K = K_{\mu\nu}\,p^\mu p^\nu, \qquad p^\alpha\nabla_\alpha K = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 6" />

The proof is a short calculation: $p^\alpha\nabla_\alpha p^\mu = 0$ by the geodesic equation, so only $p^\alpha p^\mu p^\nu\nabla_\alpha K_{\mu\nu}$ survives, and contracting with the symmetric product $p^\alpha p^\mu p^\nu$ picks out exactly the symmetrised gradient, which is zero. (The class did a similar calculation for Killing vectors, and in a homework problem with an electromagnetic field.)

## The Killing tensor of FRW spacetime

FRW spacetimes have a useful Killing tensor built from the scale factor, the metric and the four-velocity $U^\mu$ of the comoving fluid (the same $U^\mu$ that appears in the perfect-fluid source):

<Formula label="Killing tensor of FRW spacetime" tex="K_{\mu\nu} = a^2\left(g_{\mu\nu} + U_\mu U_\nu\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 6" />

Apply it to light, since telescopes (and now gravitational-wave detectors) observe radiation moving on null geodesics. For a null four-momentum $p^\mu$, $g_{\mu\nu}p^\mu p^\nu = 0$. What remains is $(U_\mu p^\mu)^2$, and $-U_\mu p^\mu$ is the energy $E$ that an observer with four-velocity $U^\mu$ measures for that momentum:

<Formula label="Light in an FRW universe" tex="K_\text{null} = a^2\left(g_{\mu\nu}p^\mu p^\nu + \left(U_\mu p^\mu\right)^2\right) = a^2E^2 = \text{constant}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 7" />

So $aE$ is the same all along the ray. Light emitted when the scale factor was $a_\text{em}$ and received today ($a = 1$) arrives with its energy reduced by the factor $a_\text{em}$. For frequency and wavelength,

<Formula label="Frequency and wavelength" tex="\frac{\omega_\text{obs}}{\omega_\text{em}} = \frac{a_\text{em}}{a_\text{obs}} = a_\text{em}, \qquad \frac{\lambda_\text{em}}{\lambda_\text{obs}} = a_\text{em}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 7–8" />

## Redshift measures the scale factor

Astronomers define the **redshift** from the stretching of wavelengths,

<Formula label="Redshift and scale factor" tex="z = \frac{\lambda_\text{obs} - \lambda_\text{em}}{\lambda_\text{em}}, \qquad a_\text{em} = \frac{1}{1 + z}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 8" />

So the universe hands us its scale factor directly. A spectrum shows the fingerprints of well-known atomic transitions, all stretched by the same factor $1 + z$; measuring that factor measures $a$ when the light left. This, Hughes notes, is why many observational cosmologists are also expert atomic spectroscopists. His example: lines of carbon and iron from hot gas falling onto a black hole at $z = 4.8$ were emitted when the universe was $1/5.8$ of its present size (he first says $1/4.8$ and corrects himself).

The 8.286 page [Cosmological redshift](/courses/8-286/cosmological-redshift) reaches $1 + z = 1/a$ by following successive wave crests; the Killing tensor gives the same result in one line, valid in any FRW spacetime.

## From time to distance

Redshift gives $a$ but not $t$: light does not arrive stamped with its date of emission. What we know is that it travelled on a null geodesic, which connects time to space. So instead of $a(t)$ we build $a(d)$, with $d$ the distance to the source. In Euclidean space $t = d/c$ and that would be the end of it. In a curved spacetime the meaning of "distance" depends on how it is measured (p. 9).

## The proper distance and Hubble's law

Write the metric with the $\chi$ coordinate,

<Formula label="FRW line element with χ" tex="ds^2 = -dt^2 + a^2(t)R_0^2\left[d\chi^2 + S_k^2(\chi)\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right]" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 10" />

where $S_k(\chi) = \sin\chi$, $\chi$ or $\sinh\chi$ for $k = +1$, $0$, $-1$ (the function the lecture writes as $s$ of $k$). Two events separated only radially, at the same instant, are a distance

<Formula label="Instantaneous physical distance" tex="d_p = a\,R_0\,\chi" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 10" />

apart. Carroll calls it the **instantaneous physical distance**: a yardstick from us ($\chi = 0$) to the object, read at one instant. It scales with $a$, so two comoving objects in the Hubble flow separate at the rate

<Formula label="Hubble's law" tex="\dot d_p = \dot a\,R_0\,\chi = H\,d_p" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 10" />

which today is Hubble's law with $H = H_0$. But no one makes instantaneous measurements across millions or billions of light years: the light we see left long ago (p. 11). We need distances defined by what can be measured.

## Three ways to measure distance

In Euclidean space there are three natural methods, and all three agree (pp. 11–13):

<Formula label="Luminosity, angular-diameter and proper-motion distances (Euclidean definitions)" tex="F = \frac{L}{4\pi d_L^2}, \qquad \delta\theta = \frac{\delta\ell}{d_A}, \qquad \dot\theta = \frac{v_\perp}{d_M}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 11–12" />

- **Luminosity distance** $d_L$: compare the flux $F$ we receive with the known luminosity $L$ of an isotropic emitter, a **standard candle**. Nature provides some: variable stars whose period is tied to their luminosity, a class of supernovae with a nearly universal peak brightness, and, more recently, merging compact objects whose gravitational-wave luminosity follows from gravitational physics with few parameters (Hughes has worked on these "standard sirens").
- **Angular-diameter distance** $d_A$: compare the known size $\delta\ell$ of a **standard ruler** with the angle $\delta\theta$ it subtends.
- **Proper-motion distance** $d_M$: compare a known transverse speed $v_\perp$ with the angular speed $\dot\theta$ across the sky.

In an FRW spacetime the three differ. Hughes recommends D. W. Hogg's summary, *Distance measures in cosmology* (astro-ph/9905116), never submitted to a journal but posted for the community. Carroll's textbook derives $d_L$, a problem set derives $d_M$, and the lecture derives $d_A$. The 8.902 page [Observational cosmology and distances](/courses/8-902/observational-cosmology-distances) uses the same distances with $f_k(\chi)$ in place of $S_k$.

## The expansion rate as a function of redshift

All the distances use F1. Count curvature as one more "species" with $\rho \propto a^{-2}$ ($n = 2$) and write every density as a power of $a = 1/(1 + z)$:

<Formula label="F1 in terms of redshift" tex="H^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\sum_i \rho_{i,0}\,a^{-n_i}, \qquad H(z) = H_0\,E(z), \qquad E(z) = \sqrt{\sum_i \Omega_i\left(1 + z\right)^{n_i}}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 13" />

where the $\Omega_i$ are today's density parameters, curvature included, and they sum to 1.

## Deriving the angular-diameter distance

Put us at the origin; by spherical symmetry, orient the coordinates so that a standard ruler lies in the plane $\theta = \pi/2$ and subtends an angle $\delta\phi$ (p. 14). Its proper size, from the line element at the time of emission, gives

<Formula label="Angular-diameter distance in coordinates" tex="\delta\ell = a_\text{em}R_0\,S_k(\chi)\,\delta\phi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d_A = \frac{\delta\ell}{\delta\phi} = a_\text{em}R_0\,S_k(\chi) = \frac{R_0\,S_k(\chi)}{1 + z}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 14" />

True, but not yet useful: $\chi$ is a label, not an observable (no quasar tells us its $\chi$), and $R_0$ is unknown. To remove $\chi$, use the light. It reaches us on a radial null path, so $dt = a R_0\,d\chi$. Change the integration variable from $t$ to $a$, bring in $H = \dot a/a$, and then change to $z$ with $a = 1/(1 + z)$:

<Formula label="The comoving coordinate of the source" tex="\chi = \int_{t_\text{em}}^{t_0}\frac{dt}{a R_0} = \int_{a_\text{em}}^{1}\frac{da}{R_0\,a^2 H(a)} = \frac{1}{R_0 H_0}\int_0^z\frac{dz'}{E(z')}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 14–15" />

Now everything is an observable redshift and the model's $\Omega$s, the knobs a model-builder turns to fit the data. To remove $R_0$, use the curvature parameter, $\Omega_\text{curv} = -\kappa/H_0^2 = -k/(R_0^2H_0^2)$, so that for $k = \pm 1$

<Formula label="R₀ from the curvature parameter" tex="R_0 = \frac{1}{H_0\sqrt{\left|\Omega_\text{curv}\right|}}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 15" />

Assembling the pieces gives three branches:

<Formula label="Angular-diameter distance" tex="d_A = \frac{1}{H_0(1 + z)} \times \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{|\Omega_\text{curv}|}}\,\sin\!\left(\sqrt{|\Omega_\text{curv}|}\displaystyle\int_0^z\frac{dz'}{E(z')}\right), & k = +1 \\[2ex] \displaystyle\int_0^z\frac{dz'}{E(z')}, & k = 0 \\[2ex] \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{|\Omega_\text{curv}|}}\,\sinh\!\left(\sqrt{|\Omega_\text{curv}|}\displaystyle\int_0^z\frac{dz'}{E(z')}\right), & k = -1 \end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 15–16" />

For $k = 0$, $R_0$ cancels from the start. $1/H_0$ (times $c$) sets the overall distance scale. [For $k = +1$ the transcript reads "minus square root" in front of the sine, most likely $|\Omega_\text{curv}|$ to the power minus one half read aloud; the prefactor is $1/\sqrt{|\Omega_\text{curv}|}$.] The other two distances differ only by powers of $1 + z$:

<Formula label="Relations between the distances" tex="d_L = (1 + z)\,d_M = (1 + z)^2\,d_A" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 16" />

so $d_M$ has no factor of $1 + z$ in front, and $d_L$ has $1 + z$ where $d_A$ has $1/(1 + z)$.

<Problem>
Take a flat universe containing only matter, so $E(z) = (1 + z)^{3/2}$, and $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc. Find $d_M$, $d_A$ and $d_L$ for a source at $z = 1$.
</Problem>
<Step title="The integral">
$\int_0^z (1 + z')^{-3/2}dz' = 2\left(1 - 1/\sqrt{1 + z}\right)$, which at $z = 1$ is $2(1 - 0.7071) = 0.586$.
</Step>
<Step title="The distances">
$c/H_0 = 4283$ Mpc, so $d_M = 0.586 \times 4283 = 2509$ Mpc. Then $d_A = d_M/2 = 1254$ Mpc and $d_L = 2\,d_M = 5018$ Mpc.
</Step>
<Step title="A surprise">
$d_A H_0/c = 2(1 - 1/\sqrt{1 + z})/(1 + z)$ is largest, $8/27 = 0.296$, at $z = 1.25$. Beyond that, a standard ruler looks **bigger** the farther away it is, because the light left when the universe, and the ruler's distance from us, was much smaller.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## What is measured in practice

Proper-motion distances are impractical: cosmological sources are so far away that even motion near the speed of light shifts them by a negligible angle in a human lifetime. Luminosity and angular-diameter distances carry most of cosmology (pp. 16–18). Hughes's snapshot of the main measurements:

- **The cosmic microwave background.** After removing our own motion relative to the comoving frame, the typical size of its hot and cold spots is a standard ruler. It gives the angular-diameter distance to the CMB very precisely and is the strongest evidence that $k = 0$: our universe is spatially flat ("current prejudice", or best wisdom, as he puts it).
- **Type Ia supernovae** are standard candles: Hughes's reasoning is that each explodes when a white dwarf reaches the same mass, the Chandrasekhar mass (named for one of his scientific heroes). In practice they are *standardisable* rather than identical: the peak brightness is calibrated from how fast the light curve fades, and some may come from white dwarfs below that mass or from mergers. [Hughes first calls them thermonuclear explosions, then "corrects" this to the collapse of a white dwarf into a neutron star. The first description was right: a type Ia supernova is the thermonuclear explosion of a carbon–oxygen white dwarf near the Chandrasekhar mass, which leaves no neutron star behind.]
- **Galaxy clustering** measures the distribution of mass, hence $\Omega_m$, and is one line of evidence that most matter is dark.
- **Chemical abundances** probe nuclear reactions in the very early universe.
- **Nearby standard candles**, a few tens of millions of light years away, fix the local Hubble law and $H_0$.

These measurements are entangled with one another through the same formulas, so the parameters must be fitted jointly to all the data, with their errors. Redshifts are precise; distances, and so the time attached to a redshift, carry error bars that can skew the results (pp. 18–19). The 8.286 page [Supernova evidence](/courses/8-286/supernova-evidence) tells the type Ia story in detail.

## Two mysteries: flatness and the horizon

**Why is space flat?** Write F1 as $\Omega + \Omega_\text{curv} = 1$ (Hughes abbreviates the curvature parameter to "omega c" here). Matter dilutes as $a^{-3}$, radiation as $a^{-4}$, the curvature "density" only as $a^{-2}$. So

<Formula label="The flatness problem" tex="\frac{\Omega_\text{curv}}{\Omega} \propto a \ \ \text{(matter)}, \qquad \frac{\Omega_\text{curv}}{\Omega} \propto a^2 \ \ \text{(radiation)}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 19; repeated p. 21" />

Any small departure from flatness early on grows as the universe expands. Thinking in terms of $\kappa$, which could be any real number, the flat case is a set of measure zero, and physicists get suspicious when nature picks out zero: it suggests a principle driving it there (pp. 19, 21–22).

**Why is the CMB so uniform?** The early universe was a hot, dense plasma that cooled as it expanded, like a gas stretched quickly. The CMB it left has the same temperature in every direction to a part in 100,000. Yet in a universe driven only by matter or radiation, patches on opposite sides of the sky were out of causal contact in the earliest moments; indeed each patch could only ever have communicated with a surprisingly small region. How can regions that never exchanged information share a temperature (pp. 20, 22)? The 8.286 pages on [the flatness problem](/courses/8-286/flatness-problem) and [the horizon problem](/courses/8-286/horizon-problem) put numbers on both.

## Inflation

The proposed answer to both is **cosmic inflation**, an idea for which, Hughes notes, MIT's own Alan Guth shares much of the credit (p. 22). Suppose that early on the universe behaved as if it had a cosmological constant. Then $a$ grows exponentially, $a \propto \exp(\sqrt{\Lambda/3}\,t)$, while that density stays fixed and the curvature density falls as $a^{-2}$:

<Formula label="Inflation drives curvature to zero" tex="\frac{\Omega_\text{curv}}{\Omega_\Lambda} \propto a^{-2} \propto \exp\!\left(-2\sqrt{\Lambda/3}\;t\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 20–22" />

Run long enough, this drives the curvature as close to zero as you like, so close that the later growth in the radiation and matter eras never catches up. Inflation also puts the whole observable sky in causal contact early on; regions only fall out of contact after it ends (a problem set shows both).

A cosmological constant is equivalent to a vacuum energy, and a **scalar field** $\phi$ filling the early universe can provide one. In an expanding universe it obeys

<Formula label="Scalar field in an expanding universe" tex="\ddot\phi + 3H\dot\phi + \frac{dV}{d\phi} = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 23" />

where $V(\phi)$ is its potential. Coupled to F1, $V(\phi)$ acts as the density: if the potential dominates, the universe inflates. Open questions follow. Can physics provide such a potential? How does the field evolve and how does the potential switch off? What is left behind? Is there a smoking gun? One candidate is a **primordial background of very low-frequency gravitational waves**, which many experiments are searching for; detecting it would probe the inflationary potential directly (pp. 23–24). The details are beyond 8.962; see the 8.286 page [What inflation solves](/courses/8-286/inflation-solutions) and the 8.902 page [Inflation and the scalar field](/courses/8-902/inflation-scalar-field).

## Summary

- A model universe (curvature, species, equations of state) predicts $a(t)$; testing it needs observable stand-ins for $a$ and $t$.
- A Killing tensor has a vanishing symmetrised gradient, $\nabla_\alpha K_{\mu\nu} + \nabla_\mu K_{\nu\alpha} + \nabla_\nu K_{\alpha\mu} = 0$, and gives a conserved $K_{\mu\nu}p^\mu p^\nu$ along geodesics. FRW spacetime has $K_{\mu\nu} = a^2(g_{\mu\nu} + U_\mu U_\nu)$; along light, $aE$ is constant, so $1 + z = 1/a_\text{em}$.
- Time is traded for distance. The proper distance $aR_0\chi$ gives Hubble's law but cannot be measured; the luminosity, angular-diameter and proper-motion distances can, and in FRW they differ: $d_L = (1 + z)d_M = (1 + z)^2 d_A$.
- With $H(z) = H_0E(z)$, $d_A = \frac{1}{H_0(1+z)}S\!\left(\int_0^z dz'/E\right)$, with $S$ a sine, the identity or a sinh scaled by $\sqrt{|\Omega_\text{curv}|}$.
- The CMB (standard ruler), type Ia supernovae (standard candles), galaxy clustering, abundances and local candles are fitted jointly.
- Flatness and the uniformity of the CMB are puzzles in matter- and radiation-dominated models; inflation, driven by a scalar field, solves both and may have left primordial gravitational waves.
