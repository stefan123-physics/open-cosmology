With cosmology finished, the last lectures of 8.962 turn to **compact bodies**: a source occupying a finite region, with a surface and vacuum outside ($T_{\mu\nu} = 0$). Lecture 20 builds the spacetime of a static, spherical one, which Hughes calls a "star" for short. As before, $c = 1$ and the signature is $(-,+,+,+)$. The 8.033 page [Schwarzschild black holes](/courses/8-033/schwarzschild-black-holes) introduced the resulting metric and its coordinates; here it is derived.

## The most general static spherical metric

Spherical symmetry means the metric functions can depend only on radius. The most general static form is

<Formula label="Static, spherically symmetric metric" tex="ds^2 = -e^{2\Phi(r)}\,dt^2 + e^{2\Lambda(r)}\,dr^2 + r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 1–2" />

Two choices hide in this:

- A $dt\,dr$ cross term could be present, but a coordinate transformation removes it: this amounts to choosing a good time coordinate.
- The angular part could have any function of $r$ in front; choosing it to be $r^2$ makes $r$ an **areal radius**: the sphere labelled $r$ has area $4\pi r^2$. It does not make $r$ a radial distance. The proper distance between the spheres at $r_1$ and $r_2$ is $\int e^{\Lambda}dr$, which equals $r_2 - r_1$ only if $\Lambda = 0$, and in general it is not.

These are **Schwarzschild coordinates**. Another useful choice, **isotropic coordinates**, uses a radius $\bar r$ that treats the three spatial directions alike:

<Formula label="Isotropic coordinates" tex="ds^2 = -e^{2\Phi}\,dt^2 + e^{2\mu}\left[d\bar r^2 + \bar r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right]" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3" />

This loses the areal meaning of the radius. The weak-field metric of linearised theory, $-(1 + 2\Phi)dt^2 + (1 - 2\Phi)(dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2)$ with $\Phi$ the Newtonian potential, has this form to first order in $\Phi$, with $\mu = -\Phi$: it was in isotropic coordinates all along (p. 3).

## Curvature and source

With the metric known, the curvature is a matter of algebraic stamina (or a computer algebra notebook; Carroll lists the components in different notation). Writing $'$ for $d/dr$, the non-zero Ricci components are

<Formula label="Ricci tensor of the static spherical metric" tex="R_{tt} = e^{2(\Phi - \Lambda)}\left[\Phi'' + \Phi'^2 - \Phi'\Lambda' + \frac{2}{r}\Phi'\right], \qquad R_{rr} = -\Phi'' - \Phi'^2 + \Phi'\Lambda' + \frac{2}{r}\Lambda'" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 4" />

<Formula label="Angular components" tex="R_{\theta\theta} = e^{-2\Lambda}\left[r\left(\Lambda' - \Phi'\right) - 1\right] + 1, \qquad R_{\phi\phi} = \sin^2\theta\,R_{\theta\theta}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 4, 6" />

The products $\Phi'^2$ and $\Phi'\Lambda'$ are the nonlinear terms that weak-field calculations threw away.

The source is a perfect fluid with $\rho(r)$ and $P(r)$, at rest: only $U^t$ is non-zero, and normalising $U_\mu U^\mu = -1$ gives

<Formula label="Static fluid four-velocity" tex="U^t = e^{-\Phi}, \qquad U_t = -e^{\Phi}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 5" />

The star has a surface at $r = r_\star$: $\rho = P = 0$ for $r \ge r_\star$. [Hughes says $e^{\phi}$ and then corrects himself to $e^{-\phi}$ for $U^t$.]

## Outside: the Schwarzschild metric

Outside, $T_{\mu\nu} = 0$, so Einstein's equation is $R_{\mu\nu} = 0$, and any combination of the components vanishes. Multiplying $R_{tt}$ by $e^{-2(\Phi - \Lambda)}$ and adding $R_{rr}$ cancels the nonlinear terms:

<Formula label="Combining tt and rr" tex="e^{-2(\Phi - \Lambda)}R_{tt} + R_{rr} = \frac{2}{r}\left(\Phi' + \Lambda'\right) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Phi = -\Lambda + \text{constant}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 5–6" />

The constant only rescales the time coordinate, so it carries no physics; set it to zero. The angular equation is linear; with $\Lambda = -\Phi$ it becomes

<Formula label="The θθ equation outside" tex="e^{2\Phi}\left(-2r\Phi' - 1\right) + 1 = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dr}\left(r\,e^{2\Phi}\right) = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Phi = \frac{1}{2}\ln\left(1 + \frac{a}{r}\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 6" />

with $a$ a constant of integration of dimension length.

**Fixing $a$ by the Newtonian limit.** Consider slow radial free fall far away, $r \gg |a|$, with $dt/d\tau \gg dr/d\tau$. The geodesic equation gives $d^2r/d\tau^2 = -\Gamma^r{}_{tt}(dt/d\tau)^2$, and from the Christoffel symbols

<Formula label="Radial free fall far away" tex="\Gamma^r{}_{tt} = e^{2(\Phi - \Lambda)}\Phi' = -\frac{a}{2r^2}\left(1 + \frac{a}{r}\right) \approx -\frac{a}{2r^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d^2r}{dt^2} \approx \frac{a}{2r^2}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 7" />

Newton says $d^2r/dt^2 = -GM/r^2$, so $a = -2GM$. The result is the **Schwarzschild metric**:

<Formula label="Schwarzschild metric" tex="ds^2 = -\left(1 - \frac{2GM}{r}\right)dt^2 + \frac{dr^2}{1 - 2GM/r} + r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 7" />

Hughes tells its history. Einstein's field equations appeared in November 1915. Karl Schwarzschild, a professor serving as an artillery officer on the Eastern Front, found this exact solution and published it early in 1916 (Hughes tells it as work done while ill and recuperating); it astonished Einstein, who had not expected so simple an exact solution. Schwarzschild died a few months later. [Hughes describes his illness as a lung infection caught in the trenches and his death as several weeks after the solution; Schwarzschild in fact suffered from pemphigus, an autoimmune skin disease, and died in May 1916 (MacTutor biography).] Hughes adds, speaking during the 2020 lockdown, that the story helps him get out of bed in the morning.

<Problem>
A neutron star has $M = 1.4\,M_\odot$ and areal radius $r_\star = 12$ km. Using $GM_\odot/c^2 = 1477$ m, find $2GM/(r_\star c^2)$ there, compare with the Sun's surface ($r = 6.96\times10^8$ m), and find the redshift of light leaving the neutron star.
</Problem>
<Step title="The neutron star">
$GM/c^2 = 1.4 \times 1477 = 2068$ m, so $2GM/(r_\star c^2) = 4136/12\,000 = 0.345$. Corrections of that size are not small.
</Step>
<Step title="The Sun">
$2 \times 1477/(6.96\times10^8) = 4.24\times10^{-6}$: weak field, as linearised theory assumed.
</Step>
<Step title="Redshift">
A static clock at the surface ticks at $\sqrt{-g_{tt}} = \sqrt{1 - 0.345} = 0.810$ of the rate of one far away, so light climbing out is redshifted by $1/0.810 - 1 = 0.235$.
</Step>

## Birkhoff's theorem

The Schwarzschild metric matters so much because of **Birkhoff's theorem**: the vacuum outside *any* spherically symmetric body is Schwarzschild, even if the body changes with time, as long as it stays spherical (radial pulsations, for example). A spherical motion has no time-varying quadrupole moment, so it emits no gravitational waves. Outside, you learn nothing about how the matter is distributed in radius, only its mass $M$ and your $r$, much as Gauss's law makes a spherical charge distribution look like a point charge (pp. 9–10).

The proof (p. 10–11) lets the metric functions depend on time, $\Phi(t, r)$ and $\Lambda(t, r)$. A new Ricci component appears:

<Formula label="The new Ricci component" tex="R_{tr} = \frac{2}{r}\,\frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial t} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Lambda = \Lambda(r)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 10" />

$R_{\theta\theta}$ keeps its static form, so the earlier analysis goes through. Moreover its time derivative must vanish, which requires

<Formula label="Separating Φ" tex="\frac{\partial^2\Phi}{\partial t\,\partial r} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Phi(t, r) = \Phi_1(r) + \Phi_2(t)" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 10–11" />

The $\Phi_2(t)$ part multiplies $dt^2$ by $e^{2\Phi_2(t)}$, which a new time coordinate $dt' = e^{\Phi_2(t)}dt$ absorbs, and the Schwarzschild form returns. So, as Hughes puts it, a spherically symmetric vacuum spacetime has a Killing vector $\partial_t$, timelike wherever $r > 2GM$ (in particular everywhere outside a star), which gives a conserved energy for orbits, useful in the next lectures.

## Inside: the mass function

Inside, the perfect fluid has $T^{\mu}{}_{\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(-\rho, P, P, P)$, so it is convenient to write Einstein's equation with one index up. The $tt$ component depends on $\Lambda$ alone:

<Formula label="The tt component of the Einstein tensor" tex="G^{t}{}_{t} = -\frac{1}{r^2}\,\frac{d}{dr}\left[r\left(1 - e^{-2\Lambda}\right)\right] = -8\pi G\rho" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 11–12" />

Define a **mass function** $m(r)$ by $e^{-2\Lambda} = 1 - 2Gm(r)/r$, chosen so that it matches the exterior. Then $r(1 - e^{-2\Lambda}) = 2Gm$, and

<Formula label="The mass function" tex="\frac{dm}{dr} = 4\pi r^2\rho \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m(r) = \int_0^r 4\pi r'^2\rho(r')\,dr'" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 12" />

It looks like the obvious mass inside radius $r$, but two remarks show it is not trivial:

- The boundary condition $m(0) = 0$ was imposed. Black holes, Hughes warns, are solutions that violate it.
- $4\pi r^2dr$ is the Euclidean volume element. The proper volume element here carries the extra factor $e^{\Lambda}$, so integrating over proper volume gives a different, larger number:

<Formula label="Mass from the proper volume" tex="m_b = \int_0^r \frac{4\pi r'^2\rho(r')}{\sqrt{1 - 2Gm(r')/r'}}\,dr' \;>\; m(r)" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 12–13" />

Hughes calls $m_b$ the **baryonic mass**: it adds up the rest energy of every particle (a problem set explores how $m_b$ compares with $m$; strictly, counting baryons uses the rest-mass density in place of $\rho$, a distinction Lecture 21 introduces). The mass $m$ that gravitates is smaller. Nothing is lost: binding puts the star in a lower energy state, and the difference measures how tightly gravity holds it together.

## Inside: the potential and hydrostatic equilibrium

The $rr$ component gives the pressure:

<Formula label="The rr component" tex="G^{r}{}_{r} = e^{-2\Lambda}\left(\frac{2}{r}\,\frac{d\Phi}{dr} + \frac{1}{r^2}\right) - \frac{1}{r^2} = 8\pi G P" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 13–14" />

With the mass function, it rearranges into an equation for the metric potential:

<Formula label="The metric potential" tex="\frac{d\Phi}{dr} = \frac{G\left(m + 4\pi r^3P\right)}{r\left(r - 2Gm\right)}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 14" />

Compare the Newtonian $d\Phi/dr = Gm/r^2$. Two things change. In the numerator, **pressure adds to the mass**: pressure is work done squeezing the body, work adds energy, and energy gravitates. In the denominator, $r - 2Gm$ corrects for the non-Euclidean geometry.

Finally, local conservation of stress-energy for a static perfect fluid (a problem-set result) relates the pressure gradient to the four-acceleration of the fluid elements. With only $U^t$ non-zero and $\Phi = \Phi(r)$, it reduces to $(\rho + P)\,\Gamma^r{}_{tt}(U^t)^2 g_{rr} = -dP/dr$, that is,

<Formula label="Relativistic hydrostatic equilibrium" tex="\frac{dP}{dr} = -\left(\rho + P\right)\frac{d\Phi}{dr} = -\left(\rho + P\right)\frac{G\left(m + 4\pi r^3P\right)}{r\left(r - 2Gm\right)}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 14–15" />

## The TOV equations

Together, the mass function, the potential and hydrostatic equilibrium are the **Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff (TOV) equations**, the way static spherical fluid bodies are built in general relativity (p. 15). The recipe:

1. Choose an equation of state relating $P$ and $\rho$.
2. Choose a central density $\rho(0)$, with $m(0) = 0$.
3. Integrate outwards until the pressure vanishes: that is the surface $r_\star$, with total mass $M = m(r_\star)$.
4. $\Phi$ is known up to a constant; fix it by matching to the exterior, $e^{2\Phi(r_\star)} = 1 - 2GM/r_\star$.

This is how professionals model stars (neutron stars above all), and a favourite problem set of Hughes's has students do it numerically. Lecture 21 solves a simple, if unphysical, special case and discusses realistic equations of state.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Birkhoff's theorem in cosmology">
Birkhoff's theorem has a cosmological use too: a spherical region cut out of a uniform universe evolves as if the rest were not there. That is why the Newtonian expanding sphere in 8.286 ([the Friedmann equation](/courses/8-286/friedmann-equation)) and the uniform sphere of 8.902 ([the cosmological principle and expansion](/courses/8-902/cosmological-principle-expansion)) give the right answer, and why the 8.902 [spherical-collapse model](/courses/8-902/spherical-collapse) can treat an overdense region as a small closed universe.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A static, spherical spacetime is $-e^{2\Phi}dt^2 + e^{2\Lambda}dr^2 + r^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)$ in Schwarzschild coordinates, where $r$ is an areal radius, not a distance; isotropic coordinates are an alternative, and the weak-field metric is in that form.
- Outside, $R_{\mu\nu} = 0$ gives $\Phi = -\Lambda$, $e^{2\Phi} = 1 + a/r$, and the Newtonian limit sets $a = -2GM$: the Schwarzschild metric (1916).
- Birkhoff's theorem: any spherical vacuum region is Schwarzschild, even around a pulsating body; spherical motion radiates no gravitational waves, and outside a star there is a timelike Killing vector.
- Inside, $e^{-2\Lambda} = 1 - 2Gm/r$ with $dm/dr = 4\pi r^2\rho$; the gravitating mass is less than the proper-volume (baryonic) mass by the binding energy.
- The TOV equations, $d\Phi/dr = G(m + 4\pi r^3P)/[r(r - 2Gm)]$ and $dP/dr = -(\rho + P)\,d\Phi/dr$, plus an equation of state and a central density, build relativistic stars.
