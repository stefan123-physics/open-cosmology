## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit named in the question. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `G*M/b^2` or `a*cos(Omega*t)/sin(theta0)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. It treats its letters as independent, so each question says which letters to type: `Omega` for $\Omega$, `theta` for $\theta$, `theta0` for $\theta_0$, and in Problem 6 `p1`, `p2` and `q1` for $\phi'$, $\phi''$ and $\psi'$.

MIT's set gives no points. The points here, and their split between parts, are ours; so is the split of Problem 5 into parts. Several parts ask you to show something. Those parts have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The conventions are those of the lessons. The speed of light is 1; Newton's constant $G$ is kept, as on MIT's sheet, so $GM$ is a length. Only the number boxes of Problems 1 and 2 use ordinary units. The metric has the signature $(-,+,+,+)$, and $\eta_{\alpha\beta} = \mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$. Greek indices run over the four coordinates, the Latin indices $i, j, k, l$ over the space coordinates only (in Problems 3 and 4 over $\theta$ and $\phi$), and a repeated index, once up and once down, is summed over. $\partial_\alpha$ means $\partial/\partial x^\alpha$, $\nabla_\alpha$ is the covariant derivative, and $\delta_{ij}$ is 1 for $i = j$ and 0 otherwise. A particle has the 4-velocity $u^\alpha = dx^\alpha/d\tau$, with $\tau$ its proper time; a light ray has the 4-momentum $p^\alpha = dx^\alpha/d\lambda$, with $\lambda$ a parameter along the ray. Basis vectors such as $\vec e_\theta$ are those of the coordinates, so they need not have length 1; a hat, as in $\hat A^\theta$, marks a component along a basis vector of length 1. The connection coefficients (Christoffel symbols) $\Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\gamma}$ and the Riemann tensor $R^\alpha{}_{\mu\sigma\lambda}$ are those of the lessons; the definition of the Riemann tensor is printed in Problem 4. Its overall sign and the order of its indices differ between textbooks, and the answers of Problems 1(c), 4, 5 and 6 depend on them; the solutions say where.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Space garbage" points={28} mit="Problem 1">

Outside the Earth, in suitable coordinates, spacetime is close to

<Formula label="Problem 1: the weak-field metric of the Earth" tex="ds^2 = -\left(1 - 2\Phi\right)dt^2 + \left(1 + 2\Phi\right)\left[dr^2 + r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right] = -\left(1 - 2\Phi\right)dt^2 + \left(1 + 2\Phi\right)\left(dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2\right), \qquad \Phi \equiv \frac{GM}{r}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

Here $M$ is the mass of the Earth. The second form uses the Cartesian coordinates $x = r\sin\theta\cos\phi$, $y = r\sin\theta\sin\phi$, $z = r\cos\theta$, and in them the metric is $g_{\alpha\beta} = \eta_{\alpha\beta} + 2\Phi\,\mathrm{diag}(1, 1, 1, 1)$. Take $\Phi \ll 1$ throughout.

The space shuttle moves round the Earth on a circular orbit ($u^r = 0$) of radius $R$ in the equatorial plane ($\theta = \pi/2$, $u^\theta = 0$).

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Use the geodesic equation to show that an orbit that starts in the equatorial plane stays in it: $du^\theta/d\tau = 0$ if $u^\theta = 0$ and $\theta = \pi/2$ at the start. Begin with the connection coefficients that are not zero. Type $\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi}$ in the first box, in terms of `theta`. The second box (our addition) asks which kinds of coefficients $\Gamma^\theta_{\beta\gamma}$, with the upper index $\theta$, are not zero.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1a-kinds" label="The coefficients with upper index θ that are not zero:" choices={["one kind only: lower indices φ and φ, exactly as on a sphere with no mass inside it", "three kinds: lower indices t and t, r and θ, and φ and φ", "two kinds: lower indices r and θ, and lower indices φ and φ", "three kinds: lower indices r and r, θ and θ, and φ and φ"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Only two kinds of connection coefficients have the upper index $\theta$. In the geodesic equation one is multiplied by $u^\theta$ and the other by $\cos\theta$, and both are zero at the start.

<Step title="The connection coefficients">

The coefficients follow from the metric:

<Formula label="Connection coefficients from the metric" tex="\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} = \tfrac12\,g^{\mu\gamma}\left(\partial_\alpha g_{\beta\gamma} + \partial_\beta g_{\alpha\gamma} - \partial_\gamma g_{\alpha\beta}\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 16–17" />

The metric is diagonal, with $g_{tt} = -(1 - 2\Phi)$, $g_{rr} = 1 + 2\Phi$, $g_{\theta\theta} = (1 + 2\Phi)r^2$ and $g_{\phi\phi} = (1 + 2\Phi)r^2\sin^2\theta$, and $\Phi$ depends on $r$ only. For the upper index $\theta$ the sum has one term, $\gamma = \theta$. Then $g_{\theta\theta}$ depends on $r$ only, and the only component that depends on $\theta$ is $g_{\phi\phi}$. That leaves two kinds:

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the coefficients with upper index theta" tex="\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} = \Gamma^\theta_{\theta r} = \frac{\partial_r g_{\theta\theta}}{2\,g_{\theta\theta}} = \frac{1 + \Phi}{r\left(1 + 2\Phi\right)}, \qquad \Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi} = -\frac{\partial_\theta g_{\phi\phi}}{2\,g_{\theta\theta}} = -\sin\theta\cos\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The first uses $d\Phi/dr = -\Phi/r$. The same steps give the other coefficients that are not zero. They are needed in part (b):

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the other connection coefficients" tex="\Gamma^t_{tr} = \frac{\Phi}{r\left(1 - 2\Phi\right)}, \quad \Gamma^r_{tt} = \frac{\Phi}{r\left(1 + 2\Phi\right)}, \quad \Gamma^r_{rr} = -\frac{\Phi}{r\left(1 + 2\Phi\right)}, \quad \Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = -\frac{r\left(1 + \Phi\right)}{1 + 2\Phi}, \quad \Gamma^r_{\phi\phi} = \sin^2\theta\;\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta}, \quad \Gamma^\phi_{r\phi} = \Gamma^\theta_{r\theta}, \quad \Gamma^\phi_{\theta\phi} = \cot\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Each is symmetric in its two lower indices. These are exact for the metric as written; to first order in $\Phi$, $\Gamma^t_{tr} \approx \Gamma^r_{tt} \approx \Phi/r = GM/r^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The θ component of the geodesic equation">

A freely falling body obeys the geodesic equation,

<Formula label="The geodesic equation" tex="\frac{du^\alpha}{d\tau} + \Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\gamma}\,u^\beta u^\gamma = 0, \qquad u^\alpha = \frac{dx^\alpha}{d\tau}" cite="reading3" at="transcript p. 16" />

For $\alpha = \theta$, with the two kinds of coefficients (the mixed one counts twice in the sum):

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the theta equation" tex="\frac{du^\theta}{d\tau} = -2\,\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta}\,u^r u^\theta + \sin\theta\cos\theta\left(u^\phi\right)^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

At the start $u^\theta = 0$, so the first term is zero, and $\theta = \pi/2$, so $\cos\theta = 0$ and the second term is zero. Hence $du^\theta/d\tau = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="It stays that way">

One instant is not enough: the equation must keep $\theta = \pi/2$ at all later times. It does. The function $\theta(\tau) = \pi/2$, with $u^\theta = 0$ for all $\tau$, solves the $\theta$ equation whatever $r(\tau)$ and $\phi(\tau)$ do, and it has the right starting values. A second-order differential equation has one solution for given starting values of $\theta$ and $u^\theta$, so this is the orbit.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The metric does not change when $\theta$ is replaced by $\pi - \theta$: the two sides of the equatorial plane are mirror images. A body that starts in the plane with its velocity in the plane has no reason to leave it on one side and not the other. The coefficient $\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi} = -\sin\theta\cos\theta$ is the one of a sphere without any mass, since the factor $1 + 2\Phi$ cancels in it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={7}>

Now require that the orbit stays circular: $du^r/d\tau = 0$. Use the $r$ component of the geodesic equation to find the orbital frequency

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): the orbital frequency" tex="\Omega \equiv \frac{d\phi/d\tau}{dt/d\tau}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

Type $\Omega$ to leading order in $\Phi$, in terms of `G`, `M` and `R`. Does the result look familiar?

Then a number (our addition). Take an orbit 400 km above the Earth's mean radius of 6371 km, so $R = 6771$ km, and $GM = 3.9860\times 10^{14}$ m³/s² (NASA's Earth Fact Sheet). How long does one orbit take, in minutes?

<Hint>

On a circular equatorial orbit only $u^t$ and $u^\phi$ are not zero. Which two connection coefficients with the upper index $r$ survive in the $r$ component of the geodesic equation?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Set $du^r/d\tau = 0$ and solve for the ratio $u^\phi/u^t$. Then keep the leading term for small $\Phi$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1b-omega" label="$\Omega =$" expr="sqrt(G*M/R^3)"

<Answer id="p1b-period" label="One orbit takes" value="92.4" unit="minutes" tolerance="1%" calc="p1b-period" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With $u^r = u^\theta = 0$ the $r$ component of the geodesic equation has two terms, an inward pull from $\Gamma^r_{tt}$ and an outward term from $\Gamma^r_{\phi\phi}$. They must cancel.

<Step title="The r component">

The coefficients with the upper index $r$ are $\Gamma^r_{tt}$, $\Gamma^r_{rr}$, $\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta}$ and $\Gamma^r_{\phi\phi}$. With $u^r = u^\theta = 0$ and $\theta = \pi/2$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): the r equation" tex="\frac{du^r}{d\tau} = -\Gamma^r_{tt}\left(u^t\right)^2 - \Gamma^r_{\phi\phi}\left(u^\phi\right)^2 = -\frac{\Phi}{R\left(1 + 2\Phi\right)}\left(u^t\right)^2 + \frac{R\left(1 + \Phi\right)}{1 + 2\Phi}\left(u^\phi\right)^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

with $\Phi = GM/R$ on the orbit.

</Step>

<Step title="The frequency">

Setting this to zero and dividing by $(u^t)^2$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): Kepler's third law" tex="\Omega^2 = \left(\frac{u^\phi}{u^t}\right)^2 = \frac{\Phi}{R^2\left(1 + \Phi\right)} = \frac{GM}{R^3\left(1 + \Phi\right)} \approx \frac{GM}{R^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

This is Kepler's third law, $\Omega^2R^3 = GM$: the Newtonian balance $\Omega^2R = GM/R^2$ between the acceleration of circular motion and gravity. The factor $1/(1 + \Phi)$ should not be trusted. The metric of the sheet is good to first order in $\Phi$ only; a term of order $\Phi^2$ in $g_{tt}$, which it leaves out, would change $\Gamma^r_{tt}$ by a fraction of order $\Phi$ as well.

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

For $R = 6.771\times 10^6$ m, $\Omega = \sqrt{3.9860\times 10^{14}/(6.771\times 10^6)^3} = 1.133\times 10^{-3}$ rad/s. One orbit takes $2\pi/\Omega = 5545$ s, which is 92.4 minutes.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The speed on this orbit is $\Omega R = 7.67$ km/s, and with the speed of light put back $\Phi = GM/(Rc^2) = 6.55\times 10^{-10}$. So $\Phi \ll 1$ holds well, and the factor $1/(1 + \Phi)$ could not be seen in the period anyway. $\Omega$ is the angle per unit of the coordinate time $t$, the time of a clock at rest far from the Earth.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={9}>

For this part use the Cartesian coordinates, in which the shuttle moves on $x = R\cos\Omega t$, $y = R\sin\Omega t$, $z = 0$. An astronaut lets go of a bag of garbage. Its position differs from the shuttle's by the small displacement $\xi^i = x^i_\text{garbage} - x^i_\text{shuttle}$.

Use the equation of geodesic deviation to find the differential equations for $\xi^x$, $\xi^y$ and $\xi^z$ as functions of time. Neglect terms of order $\Phi^2$ and treat all orbital speeds as much smaller than the speed of light. You will need the connection coefficients in the Cartesian coordinates.

Each equation has the form $d^2\xi^i/dt^2 = (\ldots)\,\xi^x + (\ldots)\,\xi^y + (\ldots)\,\xi^z$. In the first box type the bracket that multiplies $\xi^z$ in the equation for $\xi^z$, and in the second the bracket that multiplies $\xi^x$ in the equation for $\xi^x$. Use `Omega` and `t` only (replace $GM/R^3$ by $\Omega^2$).

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

For a slow body $u^\alpha$ is close to $(1, 0, 0, 0)$. Which components of the Riemann tensor are then left in the deviation equation? To first order in $\Phi$ they are derivatives of the connection coefficients only.

<Solution>

**The idea.** For slow motion the deviation equation keeps only the components $R^i{}_{ttj}$, and to first order in $\Phi$ these are the second derivatives of $\Phi$. The result is the Newtonian tide.

<Step title="The Cartesian connection coefficients">

With $g_{\alpha\beta} = \eta_{\alpha\beta} + 2\Phi\,\mathrm{diag}(1, 1, 1, 1)$, the formula of part (a) gives, to first order in $\Phi$ (so with $g^{\mu\gamma}$ replaced by $\eta^{\mu\gamma}$):

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): the Cartesian connection coefficients" tex="\Gamma^t_{ti} = \Gamma^t_{it} = -\partial_i\Phi, \qquad \Gamma^i_{tt} = -\partial_i\Phi, \qquad \Gamma^i_{jk} = \delta_{ij}\,\partial_k\Phi + \delta_{ik}\,\partial_j\Phi - \delta_{jk}\,\partial_i\Phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

All others ($\Gamma^t_{tt}$, $\Gamma^t_{ij}$, $\Gamma^i_{tj}$) are zero, because nothing depends on $t$. For $\Phi = GM/r$, $\partial_i\Phi = -GMx^i/r^3$.

</Step>

<Step title="The deviation equation for slow motion">

Two nearby freely falling bodies obey

<Formula label="The geodesic deviation equation" tex="\frac{D^2\xi^\alpha}{d\tau^2} = R^\alpha{}_{\mu\beta\nu}\;u^\mu u^\beta\,\xi^\nu" cite="reading5" at="transcript pp. 15, 17" />

The shuttle is slow: $u^\alpha = (1, 0, 0, 0)$ up to terms of the order of its speed $v$, which is far below 1. The displacement is in space only, taken at equal $t$, so $\xi^t = 0$. The leading term is then $D^2\xi^i/d\tau^2 = R^i{}_{ttj}\,\xi^j$.

</Step>

<Step title="The Riemann components">

In the definition of the Riemann tensor (printed in Problem 4) the products of two connection coefficients are of order $\Phi^2$ and are dropped. The rest is

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): the tidal components of the Riemann tensor" tex="R^i{}_{ttj} = \partial_t\Gamma^i_{jt} - \partial_j\Gamma^i_{tt} = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi = \frac{GM\left(3\,x^i x^j - r^2\,\delta_{ij}\right)}{r^5}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The first term is zero, and the last form is for $\Phi = GM/r$. The terms left out are small. Components with one index of $u$ in space, such as $R^i{}_{tkj}$, contain only time derivatives and coefficients that vanish, so there is no term of order $v$; the terms with two such indices are of order $v^2$ times these.

</Step>

<Step title="From D/dτ to d/dt">

On the left side, $\tau$ and $t$ differ by fractions of order $\Phi$ and $v^2$. The covariant derivative $D\xi^i/d\tau$ differs from $d\xi^i/dt$ by terms such as $\Gamma^i_{jk}u^j\xi^k$, of order $v\,\partial\Phi\,\xi$. With $\partial\Phi \sim \Omega^2R$ and $v = \Omega R$ this is $v^2$ times $\Omega\xi$, the size of $d\xi/dt$ itself. So to this accuracy the left side is $d^2\xi^i/dt^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The three equations">

At the shuttle $r = R$ and $x^i = R\,n^i$ with $n^i = (\cos\Omega t, \sin\Omega t, 0)$, and $GM/R^3 = \Omega^2$ by part (b). So $d^2\xi^i/dt^2 = \Omega^2\left(3\,n^i n^j - \delta_{ij}\right)\xi^j$, or written out:

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): the tidal equations for the garbage" tex="\frac{d^2\xi^x}{dt^2} = \Omega^2\left[\left(3\cos^2\Omega t - 1\right)\xi^x + 3\sin\Omega t\cos\Omega t\;\xi^y\right], \qquad \frac{d^2\xi^y}{dt^2} = \Omega^2\left[3\sin\Omega t\cos\Omega t\;\xi^x + \left(3\sin^2\Omega t - 1\right)\xi^y\right], \qquad \frac{d^2\xi^z}{dt^2} = -\Omega^2\,\xi^z" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The three brackets on the diagonal add up to $\Omega^2(3\cos^2\Omega t + 3\sin^2\Omega t - 3) = 0$. That is Laplace's equation for $\Phi$ outside the Earth: the tide stretches along the line from the Earth (at $t = 0$ the $x$ direction, with the rate $2\Omega^2$) and squeezes in the two directions across it (rate $\Omega^2$ each). The same equations follow in Newtonian gravity from the difference of the accelerations $-GMx^i/r^3$ of two nearby bodies. The sign of $R^i{}_{ttj}$ and the place of its indices are those of the lessons' convention; the three equations themselves do not depend on it. <Solution>

**The idea.** The ray gets a small sideways momentum from two connection coefficients, one from $g_{tt}$ and one from the spatial part of the metric. For light they give equal amounts. Adding the kicks along the straight path gives the angle.

<Step title="The equation for the sideways momentum">

For a light ray the geodesic equation is written with the 4-momentum:

<Formula label="Geodesic equation in momentum form" tex="p^\alpha = \frac{dx^\alpha}{d\lambda}, \qquad \frac{dp^\alpha}{d\lambda} + \Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\gamma}\,p^\beta p^\gamma = 0" cite="reading4" at="transcript pp. 2–3" />

The ray starts with $p^\alpha = (p^t, p^x, 0, 0)$, and $p^y$ stays of first order in $\Phi$. So in the $y$ component only $\Gamma^y_{tt}$ and $\Gamma^y_{xx}$ matter; the terms with $\Gamma^y_{xy}\,p^xp^y$ are of second order. From Problem 1(c), $\Gamma^y_{tt} = -\partial_y\Phi$ and $\Gamma^y_{xx} = -\partial_y\Phi$. Light moves on a null path, $g_{\alpha\beta}\,p^\alpha p^\beta = 0$, so $(p^t)^2 = (p^x)^2$ up to a fraction of order $\Phi$. Then

<Formula label="Problem 2: the sideways kick" tex="\frac{dp^y}{d\lambda} = \partial_y\Phi\left(p^t\right)^2 + \partial_y\Phi\left(p^x\right)^2 = 2\,\partial_y\Phi\left(p^x\right)^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The first term comes from the factor $1 - 2\Phi$ on $dt^2$, the second from the factor $1 + 2\Phi$ on the spatial part. They are equal: each gives half of the bending.

</Step>

<Step title="Add up along the straight path">

On the line $y = b$, $z = 0$ the gradient is $\partial_y\Phi = -GMb/(x^2 + b^2)^{3/2}$. With $d\lambda = dx/p^x$ and $p^x$ constant:

<Formula label="Problem 2: the integral along the ray" tex="p^y_\text{final} = -2\,GM\,b\,p^x\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{\left(x^2 + b^2\right)^{3/2}} = -2\,GM\,b\,p^x\cdot\frac{2}{b^2} = -\frac{4\,GM}{b}\,p^x" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The integral follows from $x = b\tan s$: it becomes $b^{-2}\int\cos s\,ds$ from $-\pi/2$ to $\pi/2$, which is $2/b^2$. The minus sign says that the ray is turned towards the Sun.

</Step>

<Step title="The angle">

<Formula label="Problem 2: the bending of light" tex="\Delta\phi = \left|\frac{p^y}{p^x}\right|_{\text{final}} = \frac{4\,GM}{b}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

In ordinary units this is $4GM/(c^2b)$. For the edge of the Sun, $GM/c^2 = 1.32712\times 10^{20}/(299\,792\,458)^2 = 1476.6$ m, so $\Delta\phi = 4 \times 1476.6/(6.957\times 10^8) = 8.49\times 10^{-6}$ rad, which is 1.75 arcseconds. There $\Phi = 2.12\times 10^{-6}$, so the weak-field metric is good enough.

</Step>

<Step title="The path, and a check">

Stopping the integral at $x$ gives the slope $dy/dx = p^y/p^x$ at each point, and one more integration gives the path to first order:

<Formula label="Problem 2: the path of the ray to first order" tex="y(x) = b - \frac{2\,GM}{b}\left(x + \sqrt{x^2 + b^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Far to the left the bracket goes to zero and $y = b$; far to the right it grows as $2x$, and the slope is $-4GM/b$.

While writing we also integrated the null geodesic of this metric numerically, from far on the left to far on the right. The final $p^y/p^x$ agreed with $-4GM/b$ to a fraction of order $GM/b$, and a run with the spatial factor $1 + 2\Phi$ replaced by 1 gave $-2GM/b$. The angle found that way is exactly half of the result above. The other half is due to what the sheet calls spatial curvature, the factor $1 + 2\Phi$ on the spatial part of the metric; this is the second term of the sideways kick. The sheet recalls that an eclipse expedition to Africa led by the British astrophysicist Arthur Eddington measured the positions of stars near the edge of the Sun, compared them with their positions when the Sun was elsewhere in the sky, and confirmed the relativistic value. According to the sheet this did much to convince scientists of general relativity and to make Einstein famous, although the data were not of good quality. <Step title="The equations">

<Formula label="Parallel transport along a curve" tex="\frac{dA^\alpha}{d\lambda} + \Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\mu}\,U^\beta A^\mu = 0" cite="reading2" at="transcript pp. 6–7" />

<Formula label="Problem 3: the transported vector" tex="A^\theta(\phi) = \cos\left(\phi\cos\theta_0\right), \qquad A^\phi(\phi) = -\frac{\sin\left(\phi\cos\theta_0\right)}{\sin\theta_0}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

<Answer id="p4a-count" label="Number of components $R_{ijkl}$ that are not zero:" value="4" tolerance="0.01" calc="p4a-count" />

<Answer id="p4a-ricci" label="Ricci scalar $R =$" expr="2/a^2"
  vars={{ a: [0.5, 3] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/a^2", hint: "That is one diagonal term of the sum, g^θθ R_θθ. The term g^φφ R_φφ adds as much again." },

<Solution>

**The idea.** In two dimensions the Riemann tensor has one independent component. Compute it once and let the symmetries give the rest.

<Step title="One component">

With $\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi} = -\sin\theta\cos\theta$ and $\Gamma^\phi_{\theta\phi} = \cot\theta$, the definition gives

<Formula label="Problem 4(a): one component of the Riemann tensor of the sphere" tex="R^\theta{}_{\phi\theta\phi} = \partial_\theta\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi} - \partial_\phi\Gamma^\theta_{\theta\phi} + \Gamma^\theta_{\theta\nu}\Gamma^\nu_{\phi\phi} - \Gamma^\theta_{\phi\nu}\Gamma^\nu_{\theta\phi} = \left(\sin^2\theta - \cos^2\theta\right) - 0 + 0 + \sin\theta\cos\theta\cot\theta = \sin^2\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

(the lesson works this component out as an example). Lowering the index with $g_{\theta\theta} = a^2$ gives $R_{\theta\phi\theta\phi} = a^2\sin^2\theta$.

</Step>

<Step title="All components">

The tensor with all indices down changes sign when its first two indices are exchanged, and when its last two are exchanged:

<Formula label="Two symmetries of the Riemann tensor" tex="R_{\alpha\mu\lambda\sigma} = -R_{\mu\alpha\lambda\sigma}, \qquad R_{\alpha\mu\lambda\sigma} = -R_{\alpha\mu\sigma\lambda}" cite="reading5" at="transcript pp. 4–5" />

So a component with two equal indices in the first pair, or in the second pair, is zero. With two values of each index that leaves four components:

<Formula label="Problem 4(a): the Riemann tensor of the sphere" tex="R_{\theta\phi\theta\phi} = R_{\phi\theta\phi\theta} = a^2\sin^2\theta, \qquad R_{\theta\phi\phi\theta} = R_{\phi\theta\theta\phi} = -a^2\sin^2\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

All four are one number. With the first index up they are $R^\theta{}_{\phi\theta\phi} = \sin^2\theta$, $R^\theta{}_{\phi\phi\theta} = -\sin^2\theta$, $R^\phi{}_{\theta\phi\theta} = 1$ and $R^\phi{}_{\theta\theta\phi} = -1$.

</Step>

<Step title="The Ricci scalar">

The Ricci tensor is the contraction of the first and third indices,

<Formula label="The Ricci tensor and the Ricci scalar" tex="R_{\mu\nu} = R^\alpha{}_{\mu\alpha\nu}, \qquad R = g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading5" at="transcript pp. 6–7" />

so $R_{\theta\theta} = R^\phi{}_{\theta\phi\theta} = 1$, $R_{\phi\phi} = R^\theta{}_{\phi\theta\phi} = \sin^2\theta$ and $R_{\theta\phi} = 0$. Then $R = 1/a^2 + \sin^2\theta/(a^2\sin^2\theta) = 2/a^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

As a check, compute $R^\phi{}_{\theta\phi\theta}$ directly: $\partial_\phi\Gamma^\phi_{\theta\theta} - \partial_\theta\Gamma^\phi_{\phi\theta} + \Gamma^\phi_{\phi\nu}\Gamma^\nu_{\theta\theta} - \Gamma^\phi_{\theta\nu}\Gamma^\nu_{\phi\theta} = 0 + 1/\sin^2\theta + 0 - \cot^2\theta = 1$. <Solution>

**The idea.** Fill the region with small parallelograms. The trips along shared sides cancel, and in two dimensions the small turns simply add.

<Step title="Cut the region into cells">

Cover the region with a fine grid of lines of constant $\theta$ and constant $\phi$, and carry the vector round every cell in the same sense. Every side inside the region belongs to two cells and is travelled twice, in opposite directions. Carrying a vector along a path and straight back along the same path returns the same vector, so these inner sides drop out. What is left of all the round trips together is one trip round the boundary of the region. This is where "simply connected" is used: the cells must fill everything inside the boundary. A region with a hole has a second boundary, round the hole, and the argument says nothing about one boundary alone.

</Step>

<Step title="The turns add">

By part (b) each cell turns the vector by its own $d\Omega$ and leaves its length alone. Parallel transport keeps the scalar product of two vectors fixed, so it turns all vectors at a point by the same angle. On a surface there is only one plane to turn in, so turns just add, in any order. Hence

<Formula label="Problem 4(c): the turn is the solid angle" tex="\text{angle of turning} = \int d\Omega = \int\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi = \Omega" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The senses fit: going round the circle with $\phi$ increasing keeps the cap on the side of smaller $\theta$, and the cells of part (b) are travelled with $\phi$ increasing on their side of larger $\theta$, which is the same sense of rotation.

</Step>

<Exercise id="p5" title="Killing vectors and curvature" points={10} mit="Problem 5">

<Formula label="Problem 5: the two relations to prove" tex="\nabla_\nu\nabla_\mu\,\xi^\alpha = R^\alpha{}_{\mu\nu\beta}\,\xi^\beta, \qquad \Box\,\xi^\alpha \equiv \nabla^\mu\nabla_\mu\,\xi^\alpha = -R^\alpha{}_\beta\,\xi^\beta" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 3" />

MIT's problem has no parts. The split into three parts is ours, and part (c) is our addition.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Second derivatives of a Killing vector are antisymmetric in their last two indices. The commutator links them to the Riemann tensor, and the three-term symmetry of the Riemann tensor then fixes each of them.

<Step title="What Killing's equation gives">

A Killing vector obeys

<Formula label="Killing's equation" tex="\nabla_\alpha\,\xi_\beta + \nabla_\beta\,\xi_\alpha = 0" cite="reading2" at="transcript p. 13" />

Write $T_{\alpha\beta\gamma} \equiv \nabla_\alpha\nabla_\beta\,\xi_\gamma$ for short. Killing's equation makes it antisymmetric in its last two indices: $T_{\alpha\beta\gamma} = -T_{\alpha\gamma\beta}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The commutator, three times">

The second identity, applied to $\xi_\gamma$, reads

<Formula label="Problem 5: the commutator on the Killing vector" tex="T_{\alpha\beta\gamma} - T_{\beta\alpha\gamma} = -R^\delta{}_{\gamma\alpha\beta}\,\xi_\delta = -R_{\delta\gamma\alpha\beta}\,\xi^\delta" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

Add to it the two equations with $\alpha\beta\gamma$ replaced by $\beta\gamma\alpha$ and by $\gamma\alpha\beta$. The right side becomes $-\xi^\delta\left(R_{\delta\gamma\alpha\beta} + R_{\delta\alpha\beta\gamma} + R_{\delta\beta\gamma\alpha}\right)$, which is zero by the three-term symmetry

<Formula label="The three-term symmetry of the Riemann tensor" tex="R_{\alpha\mu\lambda\sigma} + R_{\alpha\lambda\sigma\mu} + R_{\alpha\sigma\mu\lambda} = 0" cite="reading5" at="transcript pp. 4–5" />

On the left side use the antisymmetry on the three terms with a minus sign: $-T_{\beta\alpha\gamma} = T_{\beta\gamma\alpha}$, $-T_{\gamma\beta\alpha} = T_{\gamma\alpha\beta}$ and $-T_{\alpha\gamma\beta} = T_{\alpha\beta\gamma}$. The six terms are then twice three:

<Formula label="Problem 5: the cyclic sum of second derivatives" tex="T_{\alpha\beta\gamma} + T_{\beta\gamma\alpha} + T_{\gamma\alpha\beta} = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="One second derivative">

Go back to the single commutator equation. Its left side is $T_{\alpha\beta\gamma} - T_{\beta\alpha\gamma} = T_{\alpha\beta\gamma} + T_{\beta\gamma\alpha}$, and by the cyclic sum this equals $-T_{\gamma\alpha\beta}$. So

<Formula label="Problem 5: the second derivative with indices down" tex="\nabla_\gamma\nabla_\alpha\,\xi_\beta = R_{\delta\gamma\alpha\beta}\,\xi^\delta" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The first relation">

Rename the indices ($\gamma \to \nu$, $\alpha \to \mu$, $\beta \to \alpha$, $\delta \to \beta$): $\nabla_\nu\nabla_\mu\,\xi_\alpha = R_{\beta\nu\mu\alpha}\,\xi^\beta$. The Riemann tensor does not change when its first pair of indices is exchanged with its second pair, and it changes sign under an exchange inside either pair (transcript of Lecture 11, pp. 4–5). So $R_{\beta\nu\mu\alpha} = R_{\mu\alpha\beta\nu} = R_{\alpha\mu\nu\beta}$, the last step exchanging inside both pairs. Raising $\alpha$ on both sides (the metric passes through covariant derivatives) gives the first relation, $\nabla_\nu\nabla_\mu\,\xi^\alpha = R^\alpha{}_{\mu\nu\beta}\,\xi^\beta$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In flat spacetime the Riemann tensor is zero, and the relation says that every second derivative of a Killing vector vanishes. In Cartesian coordinates its components are then at most linear in the coordinates. That fits the Killing vectors of flat spacetime: constant ones for the translations, and linear ones for the rotations and the boosts. Part (c) tests the relation in a curved space.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Formula label="Problem 5: the contraction" tex="g^{\mu\nu}R_{\alpha\mu\nu\beta} = -g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\alpha\nu\beta} = -R^\nu{}_{\alpha\nu\beta} = -R_{\alpha\beta}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

</Step>

</Solution>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The combination of part (a) vanishes, so the spacetime is flat. Measured in proper distance from $x_0$ the metric is that of flat spacetime in the coordinates of uniformly accelerated observers, and hyperbolic functions undo it.

<Step title="Flatness">

With $u = x - x_0$: $\phi' = \psi' = 1/(2u)$ and $\phi'' = -1/(2u^2)$, on either side of $x_0$. Then

<Formula label="Problem 6(b): the curvature vanishes" tex="\phi'' + \phi'^2 + \phi'\psi' = -\frac{1}{2u^2} + \frac{1}{4u^2} + \frac{1}{4u^2} = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

Every component of the Riemann tensor of part (a) is zero: the spacetime is flat.

</Step>

<Step title="Proper distance as the coordinate">

For $g\,u > 0$ the line element is $ds^2 = -g\,u\,dt^2 + dx^2/(g\,u)$. Along a line of constant $t$ the proper distance from $x_0$ is

<Formula label="Problem 6(b): the proper distance" tex="\ell = \int_0^u\frac{du'}{\sqrt{g\,u'}} = \frac{2}{g}\sqrt{g\,u} = 2\sqrt{\frac{u}{g}}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad g\,u = \frac{g^2\ell^2}{4}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

and the line element becomes

<Formula label="Problem 6(b): the line element in proper distance" tex="ds^2 = -\left(\frac{g\,\ell}{2}\right)^2dt^2 + d\ell^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The flat coordinates">

Try

<Formula label="Problem 6(b): the change of coordinates" tex="\bar t = \ell\,\sinh\frac{g\,t}{2}, \qquad \bar x = \ell\,\cosh\frac{g\,t}{2}, \qquad \ell = \frac{2}{g}\sqrt{\left|g\left(x - x_0\right)\right|}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

Then $d\bar t = \sinh(gt/2)\,d\ell + (g\ell/2)\cosh(gt/2)\,dt$ and $d\bar x = \cosh(gt/2)\,d\ell + (g\ell/2)\sinh(gt/2)\,dt$. In $-d\bar t^2 + d\bar x^2$ the cross terms cancel, and $\cosh^2 - \sinh^2 = 1$ leaves $d\ell^2 - (g\ell/2)^2dt^2$: the line element above. With the absolute value in $\ell$ the same formulas work on the side where $g\,(x - x_0)$ is negative, because the line element contains only $\left|g\,(x - x_0)\right|$. Any shift, and any Lorentz boost, of $(\bar t, \bar x)$ gives flat coordinates as well; the choice above puts $x = x_0$ at the origin and $t = 0$ on the positive $\bar x$ axis.

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

For $g = 2$ and $u = 0.5$: $\ell = 2\sqrt{0.5/2} = 1$, and at $t = 1$, $\bar x = \cosh 1 = 1.543$ (and $\bar t = \sinh 1 = 1.175$).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\bar x^2 - \bar t^2 = \ell^2$: a point at fixed $x$ moves on a hyperbola in the flat coordinates. That is the path of an observer with the constant proper acceleration $1/\ell$, so the original coordinates are those of a family of uniformly accelerated observers in flat spacetime. They cover only the wedge $\bar x > |\bar t|$ (for $g > 0$), not all of flat spacetime, and $x = x_0$, where $g_{tt} = 0$, is mapped to the corner of the wedge; its edges $\bar x = \pm\bar t$ are reached only as $t \to \pm\infty$, so they are a horizon of these coordinates. So the metric looked curved, and singular at $x_0$, only because of the coordinates. While writing we also checked numerically, at one point on each side of $x_0$, that the flat metric carried back through the change of coordinates gives $g_{tt} = -\left|g\,(x - x_0)\right|$ and $g_{xx} = 1/\left|g\,(x - x_0)\right|$. (The calculation file checks the side $x > x_0$ with numerical derivatives.)

</Step>
