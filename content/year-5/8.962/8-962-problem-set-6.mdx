The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 6, in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own. Problems 3 and 4 build on [Problem Set 5](/courses/8-962/8-962-problem-set-5), where the weak-field metric bent a light ray.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit named in the question. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `-2*Lambda` or `m*g/(1 - v^2)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. It treats its letters as independent, so each question says which letters to type: `Lambda` for $\Lambda$, `phi` for $\phi$, `pi` for $\pi$, and in Problem 3(b) `L` for $\nabla^2\phi$.

MIT's set gives no points. The points here, and their split between parts, are ours. Several parts ask you to show something. Those parts have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The conventions are those of the lessons. The speed of light is 1, and Newton's constant $G$ is kept, except in Problem 5, which is about putting both back. The metric has the signature $(-,+,+,+)$, and $\eta_{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$. Greek indices run over the four coordinates $(t, x, y, z)$, with $x^0 = t$; the Latin indices $i, j, k$ run over the space coordinates only, and a repeated index, once up and once down, is summed over. $\partial_\mu$ means $\partial/\partial x^\mu$, $\nabla_\mu$ is the covariant derivative, $\nabla^2 = \delta^{ij}\partial_i\partial_j$ is the Laplace operator of flat space, and $\Box = \eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\partial_\nu = -\partial_t^2 + \nabla^2$ is the wave operator of flat spacetime. A body has the 4-velocity $u^\mu = dx^\mu/d\tau$, with $\tau$ its proper time. The connection coefficients $\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}$, the Riemann tensor, the Ricci tensor $R_{\mu\nu}$, the Ricci scalar $R$ and the Einstein tensor $G_{\mu\nu} = R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12 g_{\mu\nu}R$ are those of the lessons. With these conventions a sphere has a positive Ricci scalar, and $R_{tt} = \nabla^2\Phi$ in the Newtonian limit. The overall sign of the Riemann tensor, the signature and the side on which the cosmological term is written differ between textbooks, and the answers of Problems 2 and 3 depend on them; the solutions say where. Maxwell's equations in Problem 1 are in Gaussian (cgs) units, as on the sheet.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Constraint and evolution equations" points={8} mit="Problem 1">

Maxwell's equations for the electric field $E^i$ and the magnetic field $B^i$, with the charge density $\rho$ and the current density $J^i$, are

<Formula label="Problem 1: Maxwell's equations" tex="\partial_i E^i = 4\pi\rho, \qquad \partial_i B^i = 0; \qquad \partial_t E^i = \epsilon^{ijk}\,\partial_j B_k - 4\pi J^i, \qquad \partial_t B^i = -\epsilon^{ijk}\,\partial_j E_k" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

In flat space with Cartesian coordinates an index up and the same index down mean the same component. Here $\epsilon^{ijk}$ is $+1$ for $ijk$ an even reordering of $xyz$, $-1$ for an odd one and 0 otherwise. The first two equations hold no time derivative. They tie the fields and the charges to each other at one moment, and they are called constraint equations. The last two give the rate of change of the fields. They are evolution equations: they carry starting data that obey the constraints from one moment to the next.

The Einstein field equation has the same structure. It holds second derivatives of the metric, so the starting data are the metric and its first time derivative on one surface of constant time, just as a particle needs a starting position and a starting velocity. A component of the field equation with at most one time derivative of the metric is then a constraint on the starting data. A component with a second time derivative is an evolution equation.

Pick a time coordinate $x^0 = t$, and let $x^i$ be any coordinates on the surfaces $t = \text{constant}$. Show that the components $G^{0\nu}$ of the Einstein tensor contain no second time derivative of the metric. Then $G^{0\nu} = 8\pi G\,T^{0\nu}$ are constraints, and the remaining components, $G^{ij} = 8\pi G\,T^{ij}$, are the evolution equations. Do not build the Einstein tensor from the metric: use the Bianchi identity.

The two boxes are our additions. The first asks for the highest number of time derivatives of the metric that can appear in $\partial_i G^{i\nu}$. The second asks how the ten field equations divide.

<Hint>

Write out the divergence $\nabla_\mu G^{\mu\nu} = 0$ with partial derivatives and connection coefficients. Move everything except the term with $\partial_0$ to the right side.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The Einstein tensor is built from the metric and its first and second derivatives, and nothing higher. Count the time derivatives on the right side. How many can the left side then have, and what does that say about $G^{0\nu}$ itself?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-order" label="Time derivatives of the metric in $\partial_i G^{i\nu}$, at most:" choices={["one, because a derivative along a space direction removes a time derivative", "three, because the derivative in front adds one to the two inside", "two, because a derivative along a space direction adds no time derivative", "four, because each of the two indices can bring two"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="p1-count" label="The ten field equations divide into:" choices={["one constraint and nine evolution equations", "four constraints and six evolution equations", "six constraints and four evolution equations", "three constraints and seven evolution equations"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The Bianchi identity makes the divergence of the Einstein tensor vanish. That equation gives the time derivative of $G^{0\nu}$ in terms of quantities that hold at most two time derivatives of the metric. So $G^{0\nu}$ itself holds at most one.

<Step title="The identity, written out">

The Bianchi identity, contracted twice, is

<Formula label="The contracted Bianchi identity" tex="\nabla_\mu G^{\mu\nu} = 0, \qquad G^{\mu\nu} = R^{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\,g^{\mu\nu}R" cite="reading3" at="transcript p. 20; also Lecture 12 transcript, p. 4" />

(The lessons print it with the indices down; the metric raises them and passes through the covariant derivative.) The covariant divergence of a tensor with two upper indices has one partial derivative and one connection term for each index. Split the sum over $\mu$ in the partial derivative into the time part and the space part, and solve for the time part:

<Formula label="Problem 1: the time derivative of the 0ν components" tex="\partial_0 G^{0\nu} = -\,\partial_i G^{i\nu} - \Gamma^\mu_{\mu\lambda}\,G^{\lambda\nu} - \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\lambda}\,G^{\mu\lambda}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Count the time derivatives">

The Riemann tensor holds second derivatives of the metric (linearly) and products of first derivatives, and so do the Ricci tensor, the Ricci scalar and the Einstein tensor. Each component $G^{\mu\nu}$ therefore holds at most two time derivatives of the metric.

Now look at the right side. The derivative $\partial_i$ acts along a space direction, so $\partial_i G^{i\nu}$ holds at most two time derivatives. The connection coefficients hold first derivatives only, and they multiply the Einstein tensor without differentiating it, so the last two terms also hold at most two. The whole right side holds at most two time derivatives of the metric.

The left side is one time derivative of $G^{0\nu}$. If $G^{0\nu}$ held a second time derivative of any metric component, the left side would hold a third one, and nothing on the right could balance it. The identity holds for every metric, and at one point the third time derivatives of the metric can be given any values without changing the lower derivatives there. So the terms with third time derivatives must cancel among themselves, and there are none on the right. Hence $G^{0\nu}$ holds at most first time derivatives. The four equations $G^{0\nu} = 8\pi G\,T^{0\nu}$, with the field equation

<Formula label="The Einstein field equation" tex="G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading4" at="transcript p. 15" />

written with its indices up, are constraints. The other six independent equations, $G^{ij} = 8\pi G\,T^{ij}$ (symmetric in $i$ and $j$), are the evolution equations.

</Step>

<Step title="Indices up or down">

MIT's sheet states the result for $G_{00}$ and $G_{0i}$. The proof above gives it for the index 0 up, and the same steps applied to $\nabla_\mu G^\mu{}_\nu = 0$ give it for $G^0{}_\nu$. Lowering the index mixes components: $G_{0\nu} = g_{00}\,G^0{}_\nu + g_{0i}\,G^i{}_\nu$. If the time lines are at right angles to the surfaces $t = \text{constant}$, then $g_{0i} = 0$ and the sheet's components are the constraints as well. If $g_{0i} \ne 0$, the components with the index 0 down contain a share of the evolution equations, and the constraints are $G^{0\nu}$ or $G^0{}_\nu$. (This remark is ours.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Maxwell's equations work the same way. Take $\partial_i$ of the evolution equation for $E^i$: the term with $\epsilon^{ijk}\partial_i\partial_j$ vanishes, and what is left is $\partial_t\left(\partial_i E^i - 4\pi\rho\right) = -4\pi\left(\partial_t\rho + \partial_i J^i\right)$. The right side is zero because charge is conserved. So a constraint that holds at the start holds later. In gravity the Bianchi identity, with $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$, plays the part of charge conservation: the equation of this solution, with $G^{\mu\nu} - 8\pi G\,T^{\mu\nu}$ in place of $G^{\mu\nu}$, shows that constraints which hold at the start keep holding while the evolution equations are obeyed.

The count also fits the starting data: four constraints go with the four coordinates that can be chosen freely.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Action for a cosmological constant" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

Vary the action

<Formula label="Problem 2: the action" tex="S = \int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\left(R + a\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

with respect to the metric. Here $R$ is the Ricci scalar, $g$ is the determinant of the metric and $a$ is a constant. Show that the result is the Einstein equation in empty space with a cosmological constant $\Lambda$, in the form of the lesson:

<Formula label="The field equation with a cosmological constant" tex="G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda\,g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading4" at="transcript p. 17" />

with $T_{\mu\nu} = 0$. Type $a$ in terms of `Lambda`. Then (our addition) type the Ricci scalar $R$ of any solution of this equation in empty space, in terms of `Lambda`.

<Hint>

The lesson varied $\sqrt{-g}\,R$ and found the Einstein tensor. The only new piece is $a\,\delta\sqrt{-g}$, and the lesson gives $\delta\sqrt{-g}$ as well.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the second box, contract the field equation with $g^{\mu\nu}$. In four dimensions $g^{\mu\nu}g_{\mu\nu} = 4$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$a =$" expr="-2*Lambda"
  vars={{ Lambda: [0.5, 3] }} names={{ Lambda: "\\Lambda" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*Lambda", hint: "The sign is reversed. The variation of the square root of −g carries a minus sign when it is written with the variation of the inverse metric." },
    { expr: "-Lambda", hint: "A factor 2 is missing. The variation of the square root of −g brings a factor one half, so the constant enters the field equation as a/2." },
    { expr: "-Lambda/2", hint: "The factor 2 is upside down. The field equation holds a/2, so a is twice as large as the cosmological term, not half." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-r" label="In empty space, $R =$" expr="4*Lambda"
  vars={{ Lambda: [0.5, 3] }} names={{ Lambda: "\\Lambda" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-4*Lambda", hint: "The sign is reversed. The trace of the Einstein tensor is −R, and the trace of Λ times the metric is 4Λ." },
    { expr: "2*Lambda", hint: "The trace of the metric in four dimensions is 4, and the trace of the Einstein tensor is −R, not −2R." },
    { expr: "Lambda", hint: "The trace of the metric is missing: contracting Λ times the metric with the inverse metric gives 4Λ." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The variation of $\sqrt{-g}\,R$ is known from the lesson. The constant $a$ multiplies only $\sqrt{-g}$, whose variation is proportional to the metric. So $a$ adds a term proportional to $g_{\mu\nu}$ to the field equation, which is what a cosmological constant is.

<Step title="The two variations">

From the lesson, with the variation written in terms of the inverse metric:

<Formula label="Variation of the volume factor" tex="\delta\sqrt{-g} = -\tfrac12\,\sqrt{-g}\;g_{\mu\nu}\,\delta g^{\mu\nu}" cite="reading5" at="transcript p. 8" />

<Formula label="Variation of the Ricci term" tex="\delta\left(\sqrt{-g}\,R\right) = \sqrt{-g}\left[\left(R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\,R\,g_{\mu\nu}\right)\delta g^{\mu\nu} + \nabla_\sigma V^\sigma\right]" cite="reading5" at="transcript p. 9" />

The last term is a divergence. Its integral is a term on the boundary, which does not change the field equation (the lesson discusses it).

</Step>

<Step title="The varied action">

Add the two pieces:

<Formula label="Problem 2: the varied action" tex="\delta S = \int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\left(G_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\,a\,g_{\mu\nu}\right)\delta g^{\mu\nu} + \text{(boundary term)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The action is stationary for every small $\delta g^{\mu\nu}$ only if the bracket vanishes: $G_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12 a\,g_{\mu\nu} = 0$. Compare with $G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 0$:

<Formula label="Problem 2: the constant and the cosmological constant" tex="a = -2\Lambda, \qquad S = \int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\left(R - 2\Lambda\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The sign belongs to the lesson's way of writing the cosmological term, on the left of the field equation with a plus sign, and to the signature $(-,+,+,+)$.

</Step>

<Step title="With matter">

The sheet's action has no matter and no factor in front. The lesson's action is $\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,[R/(16\pi G) + \mathcal{L}_M]$ (Lecture 13 transcript, pp. 12–13), and the variation of the matter part gives $-\tfrac12\sqrt{-g}\,T_{\mu\nu}\,\delta g^{\mu\nu}$. Putting $R + a$ in place of $R$ there gives $G_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12 a\,g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}$, the full equation with the same $a = -2\Lambda$.

</Step>

<Step title="The Ricci scalar of empty space">

Contract $G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 0$ with $g^{\mu\nu}$. The trace of the Einstein tensor is $R - \tfrac12\cdot 4R = -R$, and $g^{\mu\nu}g_{\mu\nu} = 4$, so $-R + 4\Lambda = 0$:

<Formula label="Problem 2: the Ricci scalar in empty space" tex="R = 4\Lambda" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own addition)" />

Empty space with a cosmological constant is curved, with the same Ricci scalar everywhere.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $R$ holds two derivatives of the metric, so it is one over a length squared, and so are $a$ and $\Lambda$.

A known solution: a later [lesson on cosmology](/courses/8-962/frw-cosmology-from-symmetry) finds that a flat universe that holds only a cosmological constant expands as $e^{Ht}$ with $H^2 = \Lambda/3$. Its metric is $-dt^2 + e^{2Ht}(dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2)$, and the Ricci scalar of this metric is $12H^2$ (while writing this page we computed it from numerical derivatives of the metric). That is $4\Lambda$, as found. The same universe checks $a$: the trace of $G_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12 a\,g_{\mu\nu} = 0$ is $-R - 2a = 0$, so $a = -R/2 = -6H^2 = -2\Lambda$. In our calculation file both boxes are compared with this solution, with $H$ taken from a numerical derivative of $e^{Ht}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Nordström's theory of gravity" points={30} mit="Problem 3">

In 1913, before general relativity was finished, G. Nordström proposed a theory of gravity in which spacetime has a metric, as in general relativity, but the metric is tied to the stress-energy tensor $T^{\mu\nu}$ by other equations (the date and the description are the sheet's):

<Formula label="Problem 3: the equations of Nordström's theory" tex="C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} = 0, \qquad R = \kappa\,g_{\mu\nu}\,T^{\mu\nu}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2" />

Here $C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}$ is the Weyl tensor (the part of the Riemann tensor that is left when all its traces are removed; see the [lesson](/courses/8-962/riemann-symmetries-geodesic-deviation), Lecture 11 transcript, pp. 7–8), $R$ is the Ricci scalar and $\kappa$ is a constant. The sheet states two facts without proof: the Weyl tensor does not change when the metric is multiplied by a function, and a metric whose Weyl tensor vanishes is conformally flat, which means that coordinates exist in which

<Formula label="Problem 3: a conformally flat metric" tex="g_{\mu\nu} = e^{2\phi}\,\eta_{\mu\nu}, \qquad \phi = \phi(x^\mu)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2" />

So the whole gravitational field is one function $\phi$ of the spacetime coordinates. Work in these coordinates. The Newtonian limit, here and below, means $\phi^2 \ll 1$, $|\partial_t\phi| \ll |\partial_i\phi|$, and bodies that move slowly ($u^i \ll 1$).

<Part label="a" points={7}>

Show that in the Newtonian limit the geodesic equation of a slowly moving test body gives the motion of Newtonian gravity. In the box, say what $\phi$ must be in terms of the Newtonian potential $\Phi$ of the lessons.

<Hint>

Find the connection coefficients of $e^{2\phi}\eta_{\mu\nu}$ from the metric and its first derivatives. Every derivative of the metric is $2\,\partial\phi$ times the metric itself, so the result is three terms, each with one derivative of $\phi$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For a slow body only $\Gamma^i_{tt}$ matters in the space components of the geodesic equation. Compare the result with Newton's $d^2x^i/dt^2 = -\partial_i\Phi$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3a-phi" label="In the Newtonian limit:" choices={["φ equals 2Φ, because the metric holds e to the power 2φ", "φ equals Φ, the Newtonian potential itself", "φ equals −Φ, the potential with its sign reversed", "φ equals Φ/2, half the potential"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a slow body the geodesic equation needs one connection coefficient, $\Gamma^i_{tt}$. For this metric it is the gradient of $\phi$, so $\phi$ acts as the Newtonian potential.

<Step title="The connection coefficients">

The connection coefficients are $\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} = \tfrac12 g^{\mu\nu}(\partial_\alpha g_{\beta\nu} + \partial_\beta g_{\alpha\nu} - \partial_\nu g_{\alpha\beta})$. Here $\partial_\alpha g_{\beta\nu} = 2\,\partial_\alpha\phi\;e^{2\phi}\eta_{\beta\nu}$ and $g^{\mu\nu} = e^{-2\phi}\eta^{\mu\nu}$, so the factors $e^{\pm 2\phi}$ cancel:

<Formula label="Problem 3(a): the connection coefficients" tex="\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} = \delta^\mu_\alpha\,\partial_\beta\phi + \delta^\mu_\beta\,\partial_\alpha\phi - \eta_{\alpha\beta}\,\eta^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_\nu\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

This is exact: no limit has been taken yet. In particular, with $\eta_{tt} = -1$,

<Formula label="Problem 3(a): the coefficients with two lower time indices" tex="\Gamma^i_{tt} = \partial_i\phi, \qquad \Gamma^t_{tt} = \partial_t\phi, \qquad \Gamma^t_{ti} = \partial_i\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The geodesic equation for a slow body">

A freely falling body obeys

<Formula label="The geodesic equation" tex="\frac{du^\mu}{d\tau} + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}\,u^\alpha u^\beta = 0, \qquad u^\mu = \frac{dx^\mu}{d\tau}" cite="reading2" at="transcript p. 16" />

For a slow body $u^i \ll u^t$, so the largest term in the sum is the one with $u^t u^t$, as in the lesson (Lecture 12 transcript, p. 9). The space components become $d^2x^i/d\tau^2 = -\partial_i\phi\,(u^t)^2$. The time component is $du^t/d\tau = -\partial_t\phi\,(u^t)^2 - 2\,\partial_i\phi\,u^t u^i$. Both of its terms are small in the Newtonian limit, the first because $\partial_t\phi$ is small and the second because $u^i$ is small (a third term, with two factors $u^i$, is smaller still), so $u^t$ is constant to this order and $d\tau$ may be replaced by $dt$ times a constant:

<Formula label="Problem 3(a): the equation of motion" tex="\frac{d^2x^i}{dt^2} = -\,\partial_i\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Compare with Newton">

Newtonian gravity is

<Formula label="Newtonian gravity" tex="\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho, \qquad \frac{d^2x^i}{dt^2} = -\,\partial_i\Phi" cite="reading4" at="transcript p. 8" />

The equation of motion is the second of these with $\phi = \Phi$ (up to a constant, which the gradient does not see). The first one, the field equation, is the subject of parts (b) and (c).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

To first order in $\phi$ the metric has $g_{tt} = -e^{2\phi} \approx -(1 + 2\phi)$. The lesson found that any metric theory with geodesic motion has $g_{tt} = -(1 + 2\Phi)$ in the Newtonian limit (Lecture 12 transcript, pp. 10–11). The two agree for $\phi = \Phi$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={7}>

Show that in the Newtonian limit the Ricci scalar is a second-order differential operator acting on $\phi$, and find the operator. Type $R$ in the box, with `L` for $\nabla^2\phi$.

<Hint>

To first order in $\phi$ the metric is $\eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}$ with $h_{\mu\nu} = 2\phi\,\eta_{\mu\nu}$. The lesson on linearized gravity gives the Ricci scalar to first order in $h_{\mu\nu}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You need $h^{\alpha\mu}$ and the trace $h = \eta^{\mu\nu}h_{\mu\nu}$. Then drop the time derivatives.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** For light the interval is zero, and multiplying the metric by a factor does not change which directions have zero interval. The geodesic equation then shows that the direction of a light ray never changes: the ray is a straight line of the flat metric.

<Step title="The geodesic equation for light">

With $p^\mu = dx^\mu/d\lambda$ and the connection coefficients of part (a), the geodesic equation is

<Formula label="Problem 3(e): the geodesic equation for a ray" tex="\frac{dp^\mu}{d\lambda} = -2\left(p^\alpha\partial_\alpha\phi\right)p^\mu + \left(\eta_{\alpha\beta}\,p^\alpha p^\beta\right)\eta^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_\nu\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(e), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Light is bent near the Sun (the sheet of MIT's Problem Set 5 recalls measurements that confirm the value of general relativity), so this theory, which passes the test of part (d), fails this one.

</Step>

**The idea.** A Lorentz transformation keeps $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ but not the matrix $\mathrm{diag}(1,1,1,1)$, so the perturbation changes its form (the lesson's rule $h_{\bar\mu\bar\nu} = \Lambda^\mu{}_{\bar\mu}\Lambda^\nu{}_{\bar\nu}h_{\mu\nu}$, Lecture 14 transcript, p. 4). The force has only a $z$ component, which the transformation does not touch.

<Step title="The metric">

With $dt = \gamma(d\bar t + v\,d\bar x)$ and $dx = \gamma(d\bar x + v\,d\bar t)$,

<Formula label="Problem 4(c): the sum of the two squares" tex="dt^2 + dx^2 = \gamma^2\left(1 + v^2\right)\left(d\bar t^{\,2} + d\bar x^2\right) + 4\gamma^2 v\;d\bar t\,d\bar x" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

while $-dt^2 + dx^2 = -d\bar t^{\,2} + d\bar x^2$. The metric components follow (a cross term $2\,g_{\bar t\bar x}\,d\bar t\,d\bar x$ appears in the line element):

<Formula label="Problem 4(c): the metric in the barred coordinates" tex="g_{\bar t\bar t} = -1 + 2\phi\,\gamma^2\left(1 + v^2\right), \qquad g_{\bar x\bar x} = 1 + 2\phi\,\gamma^2\left(1 + v^2\right), \qquad g_{\bar t\bar x} = 4\phi\,\gamma^2 v, \qquad g_{\bar y\bar y} = g_{\bar z\bar z} = 1 + 2\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

All other components are zero. The metric is no longer diagonal.

</Step>

<Step title="The force">

Only $F^z$ is not zero, and $\bar z = z$, so

<Formula label="Problem 4(c): the force in the barred coordinate basis" tex="F^{\bar t} = F^{\bar x} = F^{\bar y} = 0, \qquad F^{\bar z} = F^z = m g\,\gamma^2\left(1 + v^2\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In the barred coordinates the body is at rest: $x = vt + \text{constant}$ gives $\bar x = \text{constant}$. So part (a) can be repeated with the new metric. A body at rest needs $\Gamma^{\bar z}_{\bar t\bar t} = -\tfrac12 g^{\bar z\bar z}\partial_{\bar z}g_{\bar t\bar t} \approx -\gamma^2(1 + v^2)\,\partial_z\phi = g\,\gamma^2(1 + v^2)$, and $u^{\bar t} \approx 1$, which gives the same $F^{\bar z}$. For $v = 0$ the metric is the one of the statement again.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={7}>

The barred basis vectors $\vec e_{\bar\mu}$ are not orthonormal, since $g_{\bar\mu\bar\nu} \ne \eta_{\bar\mu\bar\nu}$. Show that an orthonormal basis $\vec e_{\hat\mu}$ can be built from them with a matrix (a tetrad) of the form

<Formula label="Problem 4(d): the tetrad" tex="\vec e_{\hat\mu} = E^{\bar\mu}{}_{\hat\mu}\,\vec e_{\bar\mu}, \qquad E^{\bar\mu}{}_{\hat\mu} = \delta^{\bar\mu}{}_{\hat\mu} + \phi\,A^{\bar\mu}{}_{\hat\mu}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(d), p. 3" />

and find a matrix $A^{\bar\mu}{}_{\hat\mu}$ that does it, to first order in $\phi$. Type the component $A^{\bar t}{}_{\hat t}$ in terms of `v`. Then decide whether, to the order kept, the components $F^{\hat\mu}$ differ from $F^{\bar\mu}$.

<Hint>

Orthonormal means $\vec e_{\hat\mu}\cdot\vec e_{\hat\nu} = g_{\bar\mu\bar\nu}E^{\bar\mu}{}_{\hat\mu}E^{\bar\nu}{}_{\hat\nu} = \eta_{\hat\mu\hat\nu}$. Write $g_{\bar\mu\bar\nu} = \eta_{\bar\mu\bar\nu} + 2\phi\,S_{\bar\mu\bar\nu}$, with $S_{\bar\mu\bar\nu}$ read off from part (c), and keep the terms of first order in $\phi$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The condition fixes only the symmetric part of the matrix $\eta_{\bar\mu\bar\lambda}A^{\bar\lambda}{}_{\hat\nu}$. The simplest choice makes that matrix symmetric. Its diagonal entries are the same for every choice.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4d-att" label="$A^{\bar t}{}_{\hat t} =$" expr="(1 + v^2)/(1 - v^2)"
  vars={{ v: [0.1, 0.8] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-(1 + v^2)/(1 - v^2)", hint: "The sign is reversed. Lowering the index t with the flat metric brings a minus sign, so this entry is +S for the time-time component, while the space entries on the diagonal are −S." },
    { expr: "2*(1 + v^2)/(1 - v^2)", hint: "A factor 2 too many: the metric holds 2φS, but the tetrad enters twice, so A holds S once." },
    { expr: "1", hint: "That is the entry for v = 0. In the barred coordinates the time-time component of the perturbation has the factor γ²(1 + v²)." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p4d-diff" label="To the order kept, the components in the orthonormal basis:" choices={["differ from the barred ones by the factor γ, because the hatted basis moves with the body", "are the same as the barred ones, because the difference is of order φg", "differ from the barred ones by the factor 1 + v², because the tetrad mixes t and x", "are exactly the same, because the tetrad is the unit matrix"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** To first order in $\phi$ the orthonormality condition is a linear equation for $A$. It has a symmetric solution, and the tetrad differs from the unit matrix only by terms of order $\phi$, so it changes the force by terms of order $\phi g$, which are dropped.

<Step title="The condition">

Write the result of part (c) as $g_{\bar\mu\bar\nu} = \eta_{\bar\mu\bar\nu} + 2\phi\,S_{\bar\mu\bar\nu}$, where the symmetric matrix $S_{\bar\mu\bar\nu}$ has $S_{\bar t\bar t} = S_{\bar x\bar x} = \gamma^2(1 + v^2)$, $S_{\bar t\bar x} = 2\gamma^2 v$, $S_{\bar y\bar y} = S_{\bar z\bar z} = 1$ and zeros elsewhere. Put this and the tetrad into the condition and keep first order in $\phi$:

<Formula label="Problem 4(d): the orthonormality condition" tex="g_{\bar\mu\bar\nu}\,E^{\bar\mu}{}_{\hat\mu}\,E^{\bar\nu}{}_{\hat\nu} = \eta_{\hat\mu\hat\nu} + \phi\left(\eta_{\hat\mu\bar\lambda}A^{\bar\lambda}{}_{\hat\nu} + \eta_{\hat\nu\bar\lambda}A^{\bar\lambda}{}_{\hat\mu} + 2\,S_{\hat\mu\hat\nu}\right) = \eta_{\hat\mu\hat\nu}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(d), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

(At this order a hatted and a barred index on $\eta$ and $S$ take the same values.) The bracket must vanish: the symmetric part of $\eta A$ must equal $-S$.

</Step>

<Step title="A solution">

Choose $\eta A$ symmetric. Then $\eta A = -S$, and raising the first index with $\eta$ (which is its own inverse) gives

<Formula label="Problem 4(d): the matrix A" tex="A^{\bar\mu}{}_{\hat\nu} = -\,\eta^{\bar\mu\bar\lambda}\,S_{\bar\lambda\hat\nu}: \qquad A^{\bar t}{}_{\hat t} = \gamma^2\left(1 + v^2\right), \quad A^{\bar t}{}_{\hat x} = 2\gamma^2 v, \quad A^{\bar x}{}_{\hat t} = -2\gamma^2 v, \quad A^{\bar x}{}_{\hat x} = -\gamma^2\left(1 + v^2\right), \quad A^{\bar y}{}_{\hat y} = A^{\bar z}{}_{\hat z} = -1" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(d), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

with zeros elsewhere. So an orthonormal basis of the stated form exists. It is not the only one: any matrix $\eta A = -S + W$ with $W$ antisymmetric also works. The extra piece $W$ is a small Lorentz transformation (a boost or a rotation of size $\phi$) of the orthonormal basis, and it changes the off-diagonal entries of $A$ only. One such choice keeps $\vec e_{\hat t}$ along the body's 4-velocity: $A^{\bar x}{}_{\hat t} = 0$ and $A^{\bar t}{}_{\hat x} = 4\gamma^2 v$.

</Step>

<Step title="The force in the orthonormal basis">

Components along the new basis follow from the inverse matrix, which is $\delta - \phi A$ to first order: $F^{\hat\mu} = F^{\bar\mu} - \phi\,A^{\bar\mu}{}_{\hat\nu}F^{\bar\nu}$, with the same index values on both sides. The only component of the force is $F^{\bar z}$, and $A^{\bar z}{}_{\hat z} = -1$, so $F^{\hat z} = (1 + \phi)F^{\bar z}$. The difference is $\phi F^{\bar z}$, of order $\phi g$, which the problem neglects:

<Formula label="Problem 4(d): the force in the orthonormal basis" tex="F^{\hat z} = m g\,\gamma^2\left(1 + v^2\right), \qquad F^{\hat t} = F^{\hat x} = F^{\hat y} = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(d), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

To the order kept the components do not differ. The components in an orthonormal basis at rest with the body (as this one is, to the order kept) are what instruments that move with the body measure, so the scale reads $mg\,\gamma^2(1 + v^2)$ and not $mg$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Check one basis vector: $\vec e_{\hat t} = \left[1 + \phi\gamma^2(1 + v^2)\right]\vec e_{\bar t} - 2\phi\gamma^2 v\,\vec e_{\bar x}$. Its square is $g_{\bar t\bar t}\left[1 + 2\phi\gamma^2(1 + v^2)\right] - 4\phi\gamma^2 v\,g_{\bar t\bar x}$ to first order in $\phi$, which is $-1 + 2\phi\gamma^2(1 + v^2) - 2\phi\gamma^2(1 + v^2) = -1$, as it must be (the term with $g_{\bar t\bar x}$ is of second order). For $v = 0$ the matrix is $A = \mathrm{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)$: clocks and rulers are rescaled by $1 \pm \phi$, and nothing is mixed.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Exercise id="p5" title="Geometrized and ordinary units" points={24} mit="Problem 5">

In geometrized units Newton's constant and the speed of light are both 1, and mass, length and time are measured in the same unit (the [lesson](/courses/8-962/einstein-field-equation) does this for the mass of the Sun; Lecture 12 transcript, p. 18).
