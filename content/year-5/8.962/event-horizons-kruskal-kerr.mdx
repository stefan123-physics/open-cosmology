Lecture 22 is the first of the last two lectures Hughes recorded for the 2020 course (two further, more advanced lectures exist only as notes). It takes up the puzzle that Lecture 21 left open: a falling body crosses $r = 2GM$ in finite proper time, yet never reaches it in Schwarzschild time $t$. Since $t$ is synchronised with light, the answer lies in how light behaves near $r = 2GM$. As before, the signature is $(-,+,+,+)$ and $c = 1$; $G$ is kept.

## Infinite redshift

Suppose the falling body carries a radio beacon. Far away, where spacetime is nearly flat, the outgoing pulse has a radial null four-momentum. Two facts do the work (pp. 2–3):

- The energy an observer with four-velocity $U^\mu$ measures is $E_U = -p_\mu U^\mu$. It holds in special relativity, so by the Einstein equivalence principle it holds in curved spacetime too.
- The spacetime does not depend on $t$, so $p_t$ is conserved along the light ray.

A **static** observer hovers at fixed $r$, $\theta$, $\phi$; this observer is not in free fall but must be held in place. Normalising $g_{\alpha\beta}U^\alpha U^\beta = -1$ with only $U^t$ non-zero gives

<Formula label="Static observer" tex="U^t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 2GM/r}}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 3–4" />

[Hughes first writes the square root itself, then spots the typo in his notes and inverts it.] So a static observer at $r$ measures $E = -p_tU^t$, and the ratio of the energy received far away to the energy emitted at $r$ is

<Formula label="Redshift to infinity" tex="\frac{E_\infty}{E_r} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{2GM}{r}}, \qquad \Delta t_\infty = \frac{\Delta t_r}{\sqrt{1 - 2GM/r}}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 4–5" />

The second relation is the stretching of the interval between pulses. However energetic the source (radio, a green laser pointer, gamma rays), the energy that reaches distant observers goes to zero, and the interval between pulses to infinity, as the emitter approaches $r = 2GM$. The surface $r = 2GM$ is a surface of **infinite redshift**. It breaks the Einstein synchronisation procedure on which $t$ rests; outside the surface $t$ is fine, just as longitude is fine everywhere except at the poles (pp. 5–6).

<WorkedExample title="Messages from near r = 2GM">
<Problem>
A static beacon emits pulses once per second of its own time. How often do they arrive far away, and what fraction of each pulse's energy arrives, if the beacon is at $r = 3GM$, at $r = 2.1GM$, and at $r = 2.01GM$?
</Problem>
<Step title="r = 3GM">
$\sqrt{1 - 2/3} = 0.577$: each pulse arrives with 57.7% of its energy, one every $1/0.577 = 1.73$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="r = 2.1GM">
$\sqrt{1 - 2/2.1} = 0.218$: 21.8% of the energy, one pulse every 4.58 s.
</Step>
<Step title="r = 2.01GM">
$\sqrt{1 - 2/2.01} = 0.0705$: 7% of the energy, one pulse every 14.2 s. The closer the beacon, the redder and slower its messages, without limit.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Light cones in Schwarzschild coordinates

Set $ds^2 = 0$ for a radial light ray:

<Formula label="Radial null rays" tex="\frac{dt}{dr} = \pm\frac{1}{1 - 2GM/r}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 6" />

with $+$ outgoing and $-$ ingoing. Far away $dt/dr = \pm 1$: light moves at 45° in a spacetime diagram, as in special relativity. Closer in, the light cone narrows, and as $r \to 2GM$ both directions become parallel to the line $r = 2GM$: the cone collapses (pp. 6–7). [The transcript says $dt/dr$ goes to 0 here; it is $dr/dt$ that goes to 0, while $dt/dr$ grows without limit, which is what makes the cone close up.]

## The tortoise coordinate

Move the pathology from time into the radial coordinate. Choose $r_*$ so that radial light has $dt = \pm dr_*$ everywhere:

<Formula label="Tortoise coordinate" tex="r_* = r + 2GM\ln\left(\frac{r}{2GM} - 1\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 7" />

In $(t, r_*)$ light cones open at 45° everywhere, because the radial axis is stretched more and more as $r \to 2GM$, which goes to $r_* = -\infty$. The name: walking in equal steps of $r_*$ means ever smaller steps in $r$ as the horizon nears, like the tortoise of the paradox.

## Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates

From the tortoise coordinate build two coordinates adapted to light:

<Formula label="Null coordinates" tex="v = t + r_*, \qquad u = t - r_*" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 8" />

$v$ is constant along ingoing radial light rays, $u$ along outgoing ones: an outgoing ray is simply "the ray with $u = 17$". Replacing $t$ by $v$ (a coordinate change $(t, r, \theta, \phi) \to (v, r, \theta, \phi)$ with the usual matrix of partial derivatives) gives the **ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein** form:

<Formula label="Ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein metric" tex="ds^2 = -\left(1 - \frac{2GM}{r}\right)dv^2 + 2\,dv\,dr + r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 8" />

There is no $dr^2$ term, and no metric component blows up at $r = 2GM$ (only $g_{vv}$ passes through zero). [Hughes says the coordinate singularity is "mollified" rather than removed; the components are in fact finite there, though $v$ and $r$ are not yet a fully symmetric description, which Kruskal–Szekeres coordinates below provide.] Radial light now has two families:

<Formula label="Radial light in (v, r)" tex="\frac{dv}{dr} = 0 \ \ \text{(ingoing)}, \qquad \frac{dv}{dr} = \frac{2}{1 - 2GM/r} \ \ \text{(outgoing)}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 8" />

Draw it with $v$ up and $r$ across (p. 9). Far out the ingoing edge is horizontal ($dv/dr = 0$) and the outgoing edge has slope 2; the familiar 45° cone appears if $v - r$ is put on the vertical axis instead, and Hughes sketches it that way. As $r$ decreases the outgoing edge steepens; at $r = 2GM$ it points straight up; inside, its slope turns negative, so the "outgoing" ray also moves to smaller $r$. Every timelike path lies inside the cone. At $r = 2GM$ the best one can do is skim along the surface at nearly the speed of light; inside, every future-directed path heads to smaller $r$. Whatever crosses $r = 2GM$ never comes back.

That surface of infinite redshift is an **event horizon**: no event inside it can influence any event outside. Spacetimes with one are **black holes**: black because light cannot escape, holes because whatever falls in never comes out (pp. 9–10).

## Kruskal–Szekeres coordinates

A final, strange-looking transformation exposes the whole structure (pp. 10–12). Exponentiate the null coordinates and recombine them:

<Formula label="Kruskal–Szekeres coordinates" tex="v' = e^{v/4GM}, \quad u' = -e^{-u/4GM}, \qquad T = \frac{1}{2}\left(v' + u'\right), \quad R = \frac{1}{2}\left(v' - u'\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 10" />

[In the lecture these definitions are only described in words; the signs given here, including the minus sign in $u'$, are the standard ones that reproduce the relations below.] Outside the horizon, a page or so of algebra gives

<Formula label="Relation to Schwarzschild coordinates (r > 2GM)" tex="T = \sqrt{\frac{r}{2GM} - 1}\;e^{r/4GM}\sinh\frac{t}{4GM}, \qquad R = \sqrt{\frac{r}{2GM} - 1}\;e^{r/4GM}\cosh\frac{t}{4GM}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 10" />

with a second branch inside. Both branches satisfy

<Formula label="Inverse relations" tex="T^2 - R^2 = \left(1 - \frac{r}{2GM}\right)e^{r/2GM}, \qquad \frac{T}{R} = \begin{cases}\tanh(t/4GM), & r > 2GM \\ \coth(t/4GM), & r < 2GM\end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 11" />

and the metric becomes

<Formula label="Kruskal–Szekeres metric" tex="ds^2 = \frac{32\,G^3M^3}{r}\,e^{-r/2GM}\left(-dT^2 + dR^2\right) + r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 11" />

with $r$ now a function of $T$ and $R$. It is regular everywhere except $r = 0$, and radial light obeys $dT = \pm dR$ everywhere: 45° lines, which makes the **causal structure** (which events can influence which) easy to read:

- Surfaces of constant $r$ are hyperbolas, $T^2 - R^2 = \text{constant}$.
- Surfaces of constant $t$ are straight lines through the origin, of slope $\tanh(t/4GM)$ outside.
- The horizon $r = 2GM$ is $T^2 = R^2$: the two 45° lines $T = \pm R$, which are also where $t \to \pm\infty$. So all of Schwarzschild time is squeezed into the region outside, and its infinite end is the horizon.
- The singularity $r = 0$ is the hyperbola $T^2 - R^2 = 1$.

A light ray sent "outward" from inside the horizon moves parallel to the horizon line and can never cross it; it meets the singularity instead. The region $r < 2GM$ is causally disconnected from the outside (pp. 12–13). A further transformation brings infinity to a finite place and gives the **Penrose diagram**, described in Hughes's notes and in Carroll.

## Summary of the Schwarzschild black hole

The spacetime is vacuum everywhere, with whatever happens at $r = 0$ not understood. At $r = 2GM$ there is only a coordinate singularity: the light-based time coordinate fails there, and the surface is one of infinite redshift that nothing can leave (pp. 13–14).

## Charged black holes: Reissner–Nordström

Schwarzschild is not the only black hole. A second spacetime,

<Formula label="Reissner–Nordström metric" tex="ds^2 = -\Delta\,dt^2 + \frac{dr^2}{\Delta} + r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right), \qquad \Delta = 1 - \frac{2GM}{r} + \frac{GQ^2}{r^2}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 14" />

is not vacuum: Einstein's equation gives the stress-energy of a Coulomb electric field of total charge $Q$ (in units Hughes calls goofy; here Gaussian units with $c = 1$; his spoken $q^2/r^2$ uses $q = \sqrt{G}\,Q$). It is a **charged black hole**, with horizons where $\Delta = 0$:

<Formula label="Its horizons" tex="r_\pm = GM \pm \sqrt{G^2M^2 - GQ^2}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 14" />

If $GQ^2 > G^2M^2$ (in geometric units, $|Q| > M$) there is no horizon, but there is still an infinitely strong tidal singularity at $r = 0$: a **naked singularity**.

Where does a horizon come from in general? In a stationary spacetime one can often choose coordinates whose surfaces of constant $r$ are timelike outside the black hole; the horizon is where such a surface turns null, which is where

<Formula label="Horizon condition" tex="g^{rr} = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 14" />

(inside, the surfaces of constant $r$ are spacelike). [Hughes says the surfaces are spacelike outside; with $g^{rr} > 0$ there, the normal to a surface of constant $r$ is spacelike, so the surface itself is timelike.] For Schwarzschild and Reissner–Nordström this happens to coincide with $g_{tt} = 0$, but not for every black hole.

## Spinning black holes: Kerr

The last example is the spacetime Roy Kerr found in 1963. In Boyer–Lindquist coordinates,

<Formula label="Kerr metric" tex="ds^2 = -\left(1 - \frac{2GMr}{\rho^2}\right)dt^2 - \frac{4GMar\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2}\,dt\,d\phi + \frac{\rho^2}{\Delta}\,dr^2 + \rho^2\,d\theta^2 + \left(r^2 + a^2 + \frac{2GMa^2r\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2}\right)\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 15" />

<Formula label="Its functions" tex="\Delta = r^2 - 2GMr + a^2, \qquad \rho^2 = r^2 + a^2\cos^2\theta" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 15" />

[The transcript has "cosine of the square root of theta", a mis-transcription of $\cos^2\theta$.] Since $g^{rr} \propto \Delta$, the horizon is at

<Formula label="Kerr horizon" tex="r_+ = GM + \sqrt{G^2M^2 - a^2}, \qquad a = \frac{J}{M}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 15" />

where $a$ is the spin angular momentum per unit mass. A horizon needs $a \le GM$; at $a = GM$ the black hole is called **maximal** (also extremal). Two features stand out (pp. 15–16):

- It is **not spherically symmetric**: no angle-independent radial coordinate makes the angular part $r^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)$.
- It has a $t\phi$ cross term, $g_{t\phi} = -2GMar\sin^2\theta/\rho^2$ (the line element shows $-4GMar\sin^2\theta/\rho^2$ because $g_{t\phi}$ and $g_{\phi t}$ both contribute). This "gravitomagnetic" term, which a homework problem finds in the linearised field of a spinning body, splits prograde from retrograde orbits.

[Hughes recalls the solution as part of Kerr's PhD work; Kerr had finished his PhD at Cambridge before he found the metric in 1963, at the University of Texas.]

## The no-hair theorem

Charge and spin combine in the **Kerr–Newman** solution, which reduces to Kerr for zero charge and to Schwarzschild for zero spin. So far: Schwarzschild (mass), Reissner–Nordström (mass and charge), Kerr (mass and spin), Kerr–Newman (all three). Does the list go on, say a black hole with a bigger northern hemisphere? No. The **no-hair theorem**: the only stationary spacetimes in 3 + 1 dimensions with event horizons are the Kerr–Newman black holes, fixed entirely by mass, spin and charge (pp. 16–17).

In astrophysics any large charge is quickly neutralised by the surrounding plasma, so every black hole we observe should be exactly **Kerr**. Testing this "Kerr hypothesis" is much of Hughes's own research, and the metric has passed every test so far (as of 2020). The word **stationary** matters: a newly formed black hole can be a complicated mess. During the collapse it radiates gravitational and electromagnetic waves, and swallows some of them, in just such a way that everything not of Kerr–Newman form is removed. Numerical simulations of collapse and collisions always end that way. Richard Price's early calculations of distorted black holes give this its semi-facetious name, **Price's theorem**: everything that can be radiated is radiated (pp. 17–18). The 8.033 page [Evidence for black holes](/courses/8-033/evidence-for-black-holes) surveys what is observed.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Black holes and the universe">
The coordinate lesson here, that a metric's singularities may belong to the coordinates rather than to spacetime, carries over directly to cosmology: in a universe dominated by a cosmological constant, the static form of its metric is singular at the cosmological horizon only because of the coordinates, just as at $r = 2GM$, while the horizon itself is a real causal boundary, and galaxies approaching it redshift without limit much as the beacon here does. Supermassive black holes, all expected to be Kerr, sit in the centres of most galaxies, and their growth is part of the galaxy-formation story of 8.902 ([the G-dwarf problem and AGN](/courses/8-902/g-dwarf-problem-agn)).
</Callout>

## Summary

- A static emitter at $r$ is redshifted by $\sqrt{1 - 2GM/r}$ as seen far away: $r = 2GM$ is a surface of infinite redshift, where the light-based Schwarzschild time fails.
- Radial light has $dt/dr = \pm(1 - 2GM/r)^{-1}$, so light cones close up at $r = 2GM$ in these coordinates. The tortoise coordinate $r_* = r + 2GM\ln(r/2GM - 1)$ and the null coordinates $v = t + r_*$, $u = t - r_*$ fix this.
- In ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates the metric is regular at $r = 2GM$, and inside it every future path moves to smaller $r$: an event horizon. Kruskal–Szekeres coordinates make the metric regular except at $r = 0$, with light at 45° and the horizon at $T = \pm R$.
- Reissner–Nordström (charge) has horizons $GM \pm \sqrt{G^2M^2 - GQ^2}$; Kerr (spin, $a = J/M$) has $r_+ = GM + \sqrt{G^2M^2 - a^2}$, is not spherically symmetric and drags frames through $g_{t\phi}$. In general the horizon is where $g^{rr} = 0$.
- No-hair theorem: stationary black holes are Kerr–Newman, fixed by mass, spin and charge; astrophysical ones should be Kerr.
