This is the lecture that brings in the physics of general relativity: the principle of equivalence. Hughes first finishes the argument, begun in Lecture 6, that gravity rules out an inertial frame covering all of spacetime. Then comes Einstein's way out, the freely falling frame; tides, and the hint that spacetime must be curved; the several versions of the equivalence principle; a counting argument showing that a local Lorentz frame always exists, and how large it can be; and the first sign of trouble with derivatives on a curved manifold. As before, the signature is $(-,+,+,+)$.

## Recap: the covariant derivative

The last lecture introduced the Christoffel symbols, the coefficients that say how derivatives of the basis objects are made of the basis objects, and with them the covariant derivative, which corrects each index of a tensor with one Christoffel symbol (pp. 1–2). Partial derivatives of tensor components are not tensor components, because a change of coordinates brings in derivatives of the transformation matrix; the Christoffel terms produce the same extra pieces with opposite sign and cancel them, a fact the students prove on a problem set (p. 2). Requiring the metric to have zero covariant derivative then gave the Christoffel symbols from derivatives of the metric (p. 2):

<Formula label="Christoffel symbols from the metric (recap)" tex="\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} = \tfrac12\,g^{\mu\gamma}\left(\partial_\alpha g_{\beta\gamma} + \partial_\beta g_{\alpha\gamma} - \partial_\gamma g_{\alpha\beta}\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 2" />

## No global inertial frame: Schild's argument completed

Part one, the rock turned into a photon and back, showed that light must lose energy as it climbs, or perpetual motion would be possible (pp. 2–3). Hughes admits the argument is flimsy but stresses that the result is a precisely tested fact, used every time GPS finds a location; the deeper reason is that photons and particles can be converted into each other, so energy conservation demands it (p. 3). With $c$ written in, light sent up a tower of height $h$ with frequency $\omega_b$ at the bottom arrives at the top with

<Formula label="Gravitational redshift up a tower" tex="\omega_t = \omega_b\left(1 - \frac{g h}{c^2}\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3" />

Part two (pp. 3–4). Suppose a single Lorentz frame did cover a large region near the Earth's surface. Draw a spacetime diagram with time running up and height across, and follow the world lines of two successive crests of the light wave from the bottom of the tower to the top. Without gravity they would be lines at 45°; gravity might bend them in some unknown way. But in a global Lorentz frame no time and no place is special: everything is invariant under translations in time and space. The second crest leaves one period after the first, into a spacetime that is no different, so its world line must be a copy of the first one slid up by one period: the two world lines are **congruent**. Then the time between the crests is the same at the top as at the bottom, $\Delta t_t = \Delta t_b$. Since that time is the period, $2\pi/\omega$, the frequency could not change, contradicting the redshift, which needs $\Delta t_t > \Delta t_b$ (p. 4).

The weak point is the assumption that nothing singles out a direction, and common sense refutes it: if no direction were special, why did the rock fall down (pp. 4–5)? So with gravity there is no global Lorentz frame. Hughes credits the argument to Alfred Schild (p. 5; the transcript spells the name "Shield").

## Freely falling frames

This looks like bad news, since all the mathematics so far was built in Lorentz frames (p. 5). Einstein's insight saves it. Ruling out *global* Lorentz frames still allows *local* ones. A Lorentz frame describes inertial observers, on whom no forces act, and the next best thing is a **freely falling frame** (p. 5).

Inside a freely falling frame, small objects released near you show no acceleration relative to you (p. 6). The reason is that the gravitational force is proportional to mass: with $F = ma$ and $F_g \propto m$, every object gets the same acceleration, so their relative accelerations vanish and they keep their relative velocities, which is exactly what makes a frame inertial (pp. 6–7). Any other force, an electric one on a charged object say, then shows up as the interesting extra force. From now on the freely falling observer is the simple one; people standing on a floor that pushes them up are the complicated ones (p. 6).

## Tides: why the frame must be small

Real gravity is not uniform (p. 7): it is a little stronger at the floor than at the ceiling, and much weaker in geostationary orbit. These variations are tides, and they spoil the idea of a uniform freely falling frame. Picture a very tall, very stiff falling elevator with three people in it, at the top, the middle and the bottom, all starting at rest. Gravity pulls hardest on the lowest one, so in the frame of the middle person the top one drifts up and away and the bottom one drifts down and away (p. 7; once in the recording Hughes says the bottom one moves "up and away", a slip). The world lines of the three are not congruent; their tangent vectors, parallel at the start, do not stay parallel (p. 8). A freely falling frame can therefore be inertial only over a finite region: it is local, not global.

Hughes connects this with geometry (p. 8). Euclid's parallel axiom says that lines which start parallel stay parallel. For centuries it troubled mathematicians, because it could not be justified as firmly as the other axioms, and it turns out to assume that the surface is flat. On a sphere it fails: two people on the equator, one in Brazil and one in Africa, both walking due north start out parallel and meet at the North Pole. Curvature makes initially parallel lines stop being parallel, and tides do exactly this to freely falling world lines. So the manifold that describes spacetime with gravity cannot be flat (p. 8).

## Three equivalence principles

The ideas so far add up to one form of the **principle of equivalence** (p. 9): over small enough regions, the motion of freely falling particles under gravity cannot be told apart from uniform acceleration. This is the **weak equivalence principle**. In Newtonian language it says that the mass in $F_g \propto m$, the gravitational "charge", is the same as the inertial mass in $F = ma$ (p. 9). That is testable: drop materials of very different composition (different ratios of neutrons to protons, nuclei bound by a lot of gluon field energy) and check that they fall at the same rate. They do; Hughes recalls the precision as about a part in $10^{13}$, perhaps better by now (p. 9). (A later development: the MICROSCOPE satellite, which reported its final result in 2022, reached about a part in $10^{15}$.)

The weak principle alone is not enough to do physics, because of tides (p. 10): the change of coordinates into a freely falling frame here differs from the one 10,000 miles above the Earth. There is no single frame, only a patchwork of local Lorentz frames to be linked up. What guides the rest of the course is a reformulation, the **Einstein equivalence principle** (Carroll's name for it; other books differ): in small enough regions of spacetime there is a coordinate system in which the laws of physics reduce to those of special relativity (p. 10).

As an aside, Hughes mentions the **strong equivalence principle** (pp. 10–11): gravity itself falls in a gravitational field just as mass does. Part of the mass of any bound object, as measured by orbits, is gravitational energy, and since energy and mass are equivalent, that energy must fall like any other mass. Before September 2015 this was tested with precise measurements of the Moon's orbit and with binary pulsars; now every pair of merging black holes seen by LIGO, objects made of nothing but gravitational energy, whose signals match the theoretical waveforms, is another test (p. 11).

## A local Lorentz frame always exists

Now the precise statement (p. 11). Start with coordinates $x^\alpha$ in which the metric is $g_{\alpha\beta}$. Look for new coordinates, marked with bars on the indices, in which the metric is Minkowski near a chosen event $p$. The new metric follows from the transformation matrix of partial derivatives:

<Formula label="The metric in the new coordinates" tex="g_{\bar\alpha\bar\beta} = \Lambda^\alpha{}_{\bar\alpha}\,\Lambda^\beta{}_{\bar\beta}\,g_{\alpha\beta}, \qquad \Lambda^\alpha{}_{\bar\alpha} = \frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial x^{\bar\alpha}}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 11" />

and the aim is $g_{\bar\alpha\bar\beta} = \eta_{\bar\alpha\bar\beta}$ over as large a region as possible (pp. 11–12). The trick is to compare what is given with what is free (pp. 12–13). The metric and its derivatives at $p$ are handed to us: they are constraints. The coordinate change and its derivatives at $p$ are ours to choose: they are degrees of freedom. Expand both in Taylor series about $p$ (p. 12):

<Formula label="Taylor expansion of the metric about p" tex="g_{\alpha\beta}(x) = \left(g_{\alpha\beta}\right)_p + \left(x^{\bar\gamma} - x^{\bar\gamma}_p\right)\left(\frac{\partial g_{\alpha\beta}}{\partial x^{\bar\gamma}}\right)_p + O\!\left(\partial^2 g\;(x - x_p)^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 12" />

and the same for the matrix $\Lambda$, whose derivatives are second derivatives of $x^\alpha$ (p. 13). Multiplying out gives a zeroth-order term, a term linear in the distance from $p$ with first derivatives, and a quadratic term with second derivatives. Then count, order by order, as Hughes likes to put it, maths is just counting (pp. 13–15):

- **Order zero.** The metric at $p$ is a symmetric $4\times 4$ matrix: 10 numbers to match. The matrix $\Lambda^\alpha{}_{\bar\alpha}$ at $p$ is $4\times 4$ and not symmetric: 16 free numbers. So the metric can always be made $\mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ at $p$, with 6 freedoms to spare (p. 14). Those 6 are the three rotations and three boosts of the Lorentz group, which keep the metric Minkowski, just as a student answered in class.
- **Order one.** The first derivatives $\partial_{\bar\gamma}g_{\alpha\beta}$ at $p$ are 10 components times 4 derivatives: 40 numbers. The free numbers are the second derivatives $\partial^2 x^\alpha/\partial x^{\bar\mu}\partial x^{\bar\gamma}$: 4 values of $\alpha$ times 10 symmetric pairs $(\bar\mu, \bar\gamma)$, also 40 (p. 14). A perfect match: all first derivatives of the metric can be set to zero at $p$ (pp. 14–15).
- **Order two.** The second derivatives of the metric are 10 components times 10 symmetric pairs of derivatives: 100 numbers (p. 15). The free numbers are the third derivatives $\partial^3 x^\alpha/\partial x^{\bar\mu}\partial x^{\bar\delta}\partial x^{\bar\gamma}$, fully symmetric in the three lower indices. The number of symmetric arrangements of 3 indices that each take $n$ values is $n(n+1)(n+2)/3!$, which is 20 for $n = 4$, so there are $4 \times 20 = 80$ free numbers (p. 15). (In the recording the formula is spoken with an "m" where $n$ is meant.) Twenty conditions cannot be met.

So in suitable coordinates the metric near any event is Minkowski, with no first derivatives at the event, up to terms of second order in the distance (p. 15):

<Formula label="Locally Lorentz coordinates" tex="g_{\bar\alpha\bar\beta}(x) = \eta_{\bar\alpha\bar\beta} + O\!\left(\partial^2 g\;(x - x_p)^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 15" />

The first derivatives vanish at $p$; Hughes corrects himself that "flat" is the wrong word for this, since only the slope at the point is zero (p. 15).

## What the leftover terms mean

The surviving terms are second derivatives of the metric, and the second derivative of a function measures how it curves (p. 16). General relativity makes this precise with a four-index tensor built from two derivatives of the metric. Naively it has $4^4 = 256$ components, but its symmetries leave exactly 20 independent ones, precisely the 20 that the coordinate change could not remove. This is the **Riemann curvature tensor**, coming in a couple of lectures (p. 16).

The counting also removes a weasel word (p. 16). "Sufficiently small region" now has a scale: the second derivatives of the metric have units of one over a length squared, so

<Formula label="Size of a locally inertial region" tex="L \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{\partial^2 g}}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 16–17" />

is the curvature scale of spacetime, and it sets how large a region can be treated as inertial (pp. 16–17).

## Vectors on a curved manifold

A curved manifold is one in which initially parallel lines do not stay parallel, like the surface of a sphere (p. 17). A cylinder looks curved but is not: lines drawn parallel on it stay parallel all the way round, and a cylinder can be cut and unrolled into a flat sheet without tearing, which a sphere cannot (p. 17).

The trouble starts with vectors (pp. 17–18). Vectors live in the tangent space at a point. On a flat board every point has the same tangent space, so vectors at two places are easy to compare. On a sphere the tangent plane at the North Pole is quite different from the one on the equator, so comparing, and therefore differentiating, a vector field becomes difficult. Take a curve $\gamma$ on the curved space, a point $p$ at $x^\alpha$ and a nearby point $q$ at $x^\alpha + dx^\alpha$, and a vector field $A$ (p. 18). The first guess at its derivative is the usual difference quotient, written schematically for $q$ displaced from $p$ along $x^\beta$ only:

<Formula label="The naive derivative of a vector field" tex="\frac{\partial A^\alpha}{\partial x^\beta} = \lim_{dx^\beta \to 0}\frac{A^\alpha(q) - A^\alpha(p)}{dx^\beta}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 18" />

That is a well-defined quantity, but not a tensor component (pp. 18–19). If $A$ is a vector and the derivatives obey the usual chain rule, then in new (primed) coordinates the result has one correct term plus an extra term with a derivative of the transformation matrix, which spoils the tensor law, just as for curvilinear coordinates in flat spacetime in the last lecture (p. 19). The cure: before subtracting, move one vector to the other's location, so that both live in the same tangent space (pp. 19–20). The first way of doing that is **parallel transport**: slide $A$ from $q$ back to $p$ keeping it parallel to itself, then define the derivative with this transported vector (p. 19). The way of transporting is not unique; the course will use two that the physics picks out, the subject of the next lecture (pp. 19–20).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Free fall in the expanding universe">
In cosmology, observers moving with the Hubble flow are freely falling. Each has a local inertial frame in which special relativity holds nearby, but no single inertial frame covers the expanding universe; that is why the Robertson–Walker metric of 8.286 is not the Minkowski metric, and why its curvature (second derivatives of the metric, which involve ä/a, (ȧ/a)² and k/a²) is not zero, and so cannot be transformed away, in any universe that contains matter, radiation or vacuum energy.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Schild, part two: in a global Lorentz frame the world lines of successive wave crests would be congruent, so light could not be redshifted. Since it is, gravity rules out global inertial frames.
- Freely falling frames are the new inertial frames, because gravity acts in proportion to mass. Tides make them local: falling bodies drift apart, as parallel lines meet on a sphere, a sign that spacetime is curved.
- Weak principle: free fall under gravity is locally indistinguishable from uniform acceleration (gravitational mass = inertial mass). Einstein principle: locally the laws of physics are those of special relativity. Strong principle: gravitational energy falls like any mass.
- Counting at an event: 10 vs 16 (6 Lorentz freedoms left), 40 vs 40, 100 vs 80. So $g_{\bar\alpha\bar\beta} = \eta_{\bar\alpha\bar\beta} + O(\partial^2 g\,x^2)$, and the 20 leftover second derivatives are the Riemann curvature; the inertial region has size about $1/\sqrt{\partial^2 g}$.
- On a curved manifold, vectors at different points live in different tangent spaces; the naive derivative is not a tensor, and vectors must first be transported, for example by parallel transport.
