This lecture does two things. First it finishes the mathematics of working in curved coordinates in flat spacetime: how to differentiate a vector or tensor field when the basis vectors themselves change from place to place. That leads to the Christoffel symbols and the covariant derivative, the derivative used for the rest of the course. Then Hughes takes the first step from special to general relativity, arguing that gravity cannot coexist with an inertial frame that covers all of spacetime. Throughout, the metric signature is $(-,+,+,+)$ and $c = 1$ unless $c$ is written out.

## Plane polar coordinates in a coordinate basis

The example is the simplest curvilinear system: keep $t$ and $z$, and replace $x$ and $y$ by plane polar coordinates $r$ and $\phi$ (transcript p. 1). As in the previous lecture, the basis is a **coordinate basis**: a small displacement is the coordinate differentials times the basis vectors,

<Formula label="Displacement in a coordinate basis" tex="d\vec x = dx^\alpha\,\vec e_\alpha = dt\,\vec e_t + dr\,\vec e_r + d\phi\,\vec e_\phi + dz\,\vec e_z" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 1" />

Since $d\phi$ is an angle with no length in it, $\vec e_\phi$ must carry the dimension of length. Hughes calls this "a feature, not a bug" (p. 1). The same happens in the matrix of partial derivatives that converts between the Cartesian and the polar components, built in Lecture 5: some of its entries have units of length and those of its inverse units of one over length (pp. 1–2).

Doing the matrix multiplication on the Cartesian basis vectors gives (p. 2)

<Formula label="Polar coordinate basis vectors" tex="\vec e_r = \cos\phi\,\vec e_x + \sin\phi\,\vec e_y, \qquad \vec e_\phi = -r\sin\phi\,\vec e_x + r\cos\phi\,\vec e_y" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 2" />

$\vec e_r$ is the familiar unit vector, but $\vec e_\phi$ is not a unit vector: it always points along the circle, and its length $r$ makes it span the same angle wherever it sits, so it grows with the radius (p. 2).

## The metric and the basis one-forms

The metric is still the dot product of basis vectors, now written $g_{\alpha\beta}$ instead of $\eta_{\alpha\beta}$ (p. 3). In plane polar coordinates it is diagonal with entries $(-1, 1, r^2, 1)$, and contracting it twice with the displacement gives the invariant interval:

<Formula label="Metric and interval in plane polar coordinates" tex="g_{\alpha\beta} = \vec e_\alpha\cdot\vec e_\beta = \mathrm{diag}(-1,\ 1,\ r^2,\ 1), \qquad ds^2 = g_{\alpha\beta}\,dx^\alpha dx^\beta = -dt^2 + dr^2 + r^2 d\phi^2 + dz^2" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3" />

The odd-looking basis vectors cause no trouble here: the interval, a quantity one can measure, comes out exactly as it should (p. 3). This is the general lesson. Individual components in a coordinate basis can look strange; the scalars built from them carry the physics.

The basis one-forms follow from the rule that indices must line up (p. 4):

<Formula label="Polar basis one-forms" tex="\tilde\omega^r = \cos\phi\,\tilde\omega^x + \sin\phi\,\tilde\omega^y, \qquad \tilde\omega^\phi = -\frac{\sin\phi}{r}\,\tilde\omega^x + \frac{\cos\phi}{r}\,\tilde\omega^y" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 4" />

## Derivatives of the basis vectors

So far all of this is definition. The new feature appears when something is differentiated (p. 4). In Cartesian coordinates the basis vectors are the same everywhere, so only the components of a vector field need differentiating. In polar coordinates $\vec e_r$ and $\vec e_\phi$ change with position. Differentiating the formulas above gives the four non-trivial derivatives (pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="Derivatives of the polar basis vectors" tex="\partial_r\vec e_r = 0, \qquad \partial_\phi\vec e_r = \frac{1}{r}\,\vec e_\phi, \qquad \partial_r\vec e_\phi = \frac{1}{r}\,\vec e_\phi, \qquad \partial_\phi\vec e_\phi = -r\,\vec e_r" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 4–5" />

$\vec e_t$ and $\vec e_z$ are constant, so all their derivatives vanish.

## The gradient of a vector field

Now take a vector field $\vec V = V^\alpha\vec e_\alpha$ and form its gradient, a tensor with one slot for a vector and one for a one-form (p. 5). Attaching a basis one-form for the derivative's index and using the ordinary product rule,

<Formula label="Gradient of a vector field, product rule" tex="\nabla\vec V = \partial_\beta\!\left(V^\alpha\vec e_\alpha\right)\tilde\omega^\beta = \left(\partial_\beta V^\alpha\,\vec e_\alpha + V^\alpha\,\partial_\beta\vec e_\alpha\right)\tilde\omega^\beta" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 5" />

Hughes stresses the point he most wants remembered (p. 6): the **sum** of the two terms transforms as a tensor, but neither term does on its own. The partial derivatives of the components are not tensor components.

## Christoffel symbols

The derivatives above show that the derivative of a basis vector is again a combination of basis vectors. So in general one writes (p. 6)

<Formula label="Definition of the Christoffel symbols" tex="\partial_\beta\vec e_\alpha = \Gamma^\mu_{\beta\alpha}\,\vec e_\mu" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 6" />

The table of functions $\Gamma^\mu_{\beta\alpha}$ is the **Christoffel symbol**. It has three indices and looks like a tensor, but it is not one: like the partial derivative of the components, it fails to transform as a tensor, and only the right combination of the two does (p. 6). Reading the polar values off the derivatives of the basis vectors, exactly three are non-zero (p. 6):

<Formula label="Christoffel symbols of plane polar coordinates" tex="\Gamma^\phi_{r\phi} = \Gamma^\phi_{\phi r} = \frac{1}{r}, \qquad \Gamma^r_{\phi\phi} = -r" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 6" />

These are not zero even though the spacetime is perfectly flat: they describe how the coordinates bend, not any curvature of spacetime. Working them out by differentiating basis vectors for every new coordinate system would be slow, and Hughes promises a faster method from the metric by the end of the lecture (pp. 6–7).

## The covariant derivative

Write the derivative with the Christoffel symbols in place (p. 7): $\partial_\beta\vec V = \partial_\beta V^\alpha\,\vec e_\alpha + V^\alpha\Gamma^\mu_{\beta\alpha}\vec e_\mu$. In the second term both $\alpha$ and $\mu$ are dummy indices, so they can be swapped; then both terms carry $\vec e_\alpha$, which factors out. The bracket that is left is the **covariant derivative**:

<Formula label="Covariant derivative of a vector" tex="\partial_\beta\vec V = \left(\partial_\beta V^\alpha + \Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\mu}V^\mu\right)\vec e_\alpha \equiv \left(\nabla_\beta V^\alpha\right)\vec e_\alpha" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 7–8" />

This is what the symbol $\nabla$ was being saved for (p. 8). Acting on vector components, $\nabla_\beta$ produces tensor components; $\partial_\beta$ does not. When the components are changed to another coordinate system, the partial derivative picks up an extra term that spoils the tensor transformation law, and the Christoffel symbol picks up an extra term of its own; the two extra terms cancel exactly (p. 8). Hughes promised notes working out the first half of this calculation and set the second half as homework. (In the lecture he first wrote the formula with a typo and corrected it on the board, p. 8.)

## A divergence in polar coordinates

A common use is the spacetime divergence (p. 9). Contracting the derivative index with the vector index,

<Formula label="Divergence in plane polar coordinates" tex="\nabla_\alpha V^\alpha = \partial_\alpha V^\alpha + \Gamma^\alpha_{\alpha\mu}V^\mu = \partial_t V^t + \partial_r V^r + \frac{1}{r}V^r + \partial_\phi V^\phi + \partial_z V^z" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 9" />

since $\Gamma^\phi_{\phi r} = 1/r$ is the only non-zero Christoffel symbol whose upper index equals its first lower index, as the sum $\Gamma^\alpha_{\alpha\mu}$ requires. The extra $V^r/r$ is the familiar correction to the radial term in cylindrical coordinates from electromagnetism textbooks (p. 9).

Hughes adds a sanity check on units (p. 9). A divergence should have the units of the vector divided by a length. Every term clearly does, except perhaps $\partial_\phi V^\phi$, a derivative with respect to an angle. But because $\vec e_\phi$ carries a length, $V^\phi$ has the units of the vector divided by a length, so $\partial_\phi V^\phi$ has the right units after all.

<WorkedExample title="Two divergences, two ways">
<Problem>
Find the divergence of the vector fields $\vec A = x\,\vec e_x + y\,\vec e_y$ and $\vec B = -y\,\vec e_x + x\,\vec e_y$ in Cartesian coordinates, and again from the polar formula.
</Problem>
<Step title="Cartesian">
$\nabla\cdot\vec A = \partial_x x + \partial_y y = 2$ and $\nabla\cdot\vec B = \partial_x(-y) + \partial_y x = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="Polar components">
From the basis vectors, $r\,\vec e_r = r\cos\phi\,\vec e_x + r\sin\phi\,\vec e_y = \vec A$, so $A^r = r$ and $A^\phi = 0$. And $\vec e_\phi = -r\sin\phi\,\vec e_x + r\cos\phi\,\vec e_y = \vec B$, so $B^r = 0$ and $B^\phi = 1$: the swirl has a constant angular component, because $\vec e_\phi$ itself grows with $r$.
</Step>
<Step title="Polar divergence">
For $\vec A$: $\partial_r A^r + A^r/r = 1 + 1 = 2$. For $\vec B$: $\partial_\phi B^\phi = 0$. Both agree with the Cartesian result, as a scalar must. Without the Christoffel term the first would have come out as 1, which is wrong.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Other kinds of tensors

A scalar has no basis object attached, so nothing in it changes with the basis, and its covariant derivative is its partial derivative: $\nabla_\beta\phi = \partial_\beta\phi$ (p. 10).

For a one-form there is a shortcut (pp. 10–11). Contract it with a vector to make a scalar, $p_\alpha A^\alpha$. Its covariant derivative is then a partial derivative, which obeys the product rule; replacing $\partial_\beta A^\alpha$ by $\nabla_\beta A^\alpha - \Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\mu}A^\mu$ and swapping dummy indices in the last term leaves everything proportional to $A^\alpha$. Demanding that the covariant derivative also obey the product rule, $\nabla_\beta(p_\alpha A^\alpha) = A^\alpha\nabla_\beta p_\alpha + p_\alpha\nabla_\beta A^\alpha$, then fixes

<Formula label="Covariant derivative of a one-form" tex="\nabla_\beta p_\alpha = \partial_\beta p_\alpha - \Gamma^\mu_{\beta\alpha}\,p_\mu" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 10–11" />

The minus sign is the important difference: it makes the two Christoffel terms cancel in the scalar $p_\alpha A^\alpha$. Equivalently, the basis one-forms change as $\partial_\beta\tilde\omega^\alpha = -\Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\mu}\tilde\omega^\mu$, the minus sign keeping the contraction of basis one-forms with basis vectors constant (p. 11). Some textbooks start from this and derive the rest; it makes no difference which way round one goes.

For tensors with more indices the rule follows the same pattern: each index has its own basis object, so each gets its own Christoffel correction (p. 12). For the stress-energy tensor, for instance,

<Formula label="Covariant derivatives of a two-index tensor" tex="\nabla_\gamma T^{\mu\nu} = \partial_\gamma T^{\mu\nu} + \Gamma^\mu_{\gamma\lambda}T^{\lambda\nu} + \Gamma^\nu_{\gamma\lambda}T^{\mu\lambda}, \qquad \nabla_\gamma T_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\gamma T_{\mu\nu} - \Gamma^\lambda_{\gamma\mu}T_{\lambda\nu} - \Gamma^\lambda_{\gamma\nu}T_{\mu\lambda}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 12" />

In general: one partial derivative of the whole tensor, then a Christoffel term with a plus sign for every upper index and with a minus sign for every lower index (p. 12). (A student caught a missing minus sign on the board here, which Hughes fixed.)

## Tensor equations hold in every representation

Computing Christoffel symbols from derivatives of basis vectors for every new coordinate system would be miserable, Hughes says, even with computer algebra (p. 12). The better way goes through the metric, and it rests on a principle used again and again in the course (p. 13): **a tensor equation that holds in one representation holds in all of them.** Changing coordinates changes the components of a geometric object, such as the arrow between two events, but not the object itself, and so it cannot change a relation between such objects.

A warm-up (pp. 13–14). In Cartesian coordinates the double gradient of a scalar has components $\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta\phi$, which are symmetric in $\alpha$ and $\beta$ because partial derivatives commute. In general coordinates the components are $\nabla_\alpha\nabla_\beta\phi$, and since the symmetry is a tensor statement, it must hold there too. Expanding, the inner derivative is a plain partial derivative and the outer one corrects the lower index $\beta$; the double partial derivatives cancel and what is left is

<Formula label="Symmetry of the double gradient" tex="\nabla_\alpha\nabla_\beta\phi - \nabla_\beta\nabla_\alpha\phi = -\left(\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} - \Gamma^\mu_{\beta\alpha}\right)\partial_\mu\phi = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} = \Gamma^\mu_{\beta\alpha}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 14" />

So the Christoffel symbols are symmetric in their two lower indices, as the polar values already showed ($\Gamma^\phi_{r\phi} = \Gamma^\phi_{\phi r}$).

Hughes uses the moment to introduce notation (p. 14). Round brackets around indices mean **symmetrisation** and square brackets **antisymmetrisation**:

<Formula label="Symmetrisation and antisymmetrisation" tex="T_{(\alpha\beta)} = \tfrac12\left(T_{\alpha\beta} + T_{\beta\alpha}\right), \qquad T_{[\alpha\beta]} = \tfrac12\left(T_{\alpha\beta} - T_{\beta\alpha}\right), \qquad \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} = \Gamma^\mu_{(\alpha\beta)}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 14" />

So contracting $\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}$ with anything antisymmetric in $\alpha$ and $\beta$ gives zero.

## The covariant derivative of the metric is zero

Now apply the principle to the gradient of the metric, $\nabla_\gamma g_{\alpha\beta}$ (p. 15). In Cartesian coordinates this is $\partial_\gamma\eta_{\alpha\beta}$, and the components $\eta_{\alpha\beta}$ are constants, so it is zero. A tensor that is zero in one representation is zero in all, so in any coordinates

<Formula label="Equation I: the metric has no covariant derivative" tex="\nabla_\gamma g_{\alpha\beta} = \partial_\gamma g_{\alpha\beta} - \Gamma^\mu_{\gamma\alpha}\,g_{\mu\beta} - \Gamma^\mu_{\gamma\beta}\,g_{\alpha\mu} = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 15" />

This has two Christoffel symbols, but nothing singles out this order of the indices, so Hughes writes it twice more with the indices permuted (equations II and III, p. 16), and then takes I minus II minus III. Using the symmetry of the lower indices, two pairs of Christoffel terms cancel and the remaining two are equal, so they add up to twice one term (pp. 16–17). Moving the metric derivatives to the other side,

<Formula label="Christoffel symbols from derivatives of the metric" tex="g_{\mu\gamma}\,\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} = \tfrac12\left(\partial_\alpha g_{\beta\gamma} + \partial_\beta g_{\alpha\gamma} - \partial_\gamma g_{\alpha\beta}\right) \equiv \Gamma_{\gamma\alpha\beta}, \qquad \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} = \tfrac12\,g^{\mu\gamma}\left(\partial_\alpha g_{\beta\gamma} + \partial_\beta g_{\alpha\gamma} - \partial_\gamma g_{\alpha\beta}\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 16–17" />

Here the indices of $\Gamma$ are raised and lowered with the metric by definition, even though $\Gamma$ is not a tensor (p. 17). The second form, with the inverse metric in front, is the one found in textbooks. Hughes admits that he could write down the right combination of permutations only because he knew the answer; the reasoning behind it is to arrange the terms so that the symmetry of $\Gamma$ removes all but one (p. 16).

<WorkedExample title="Polar Christoffel symbols from the metric">
<Problem>
Use the formula above with $g_{rr} = 1$ and $g_{\phi\phi} = r^2$ (the other components constant) to recover $\Gamma^r_{\phi\phi}$ and $\Gamma^\phi_{r\phi}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="The only non-zero derivative">
The only component that varies is $g_{\phi\phi} = r^2$, with $\partial_r g_{\phi\phi} = 2r$. The inverse metric is diagonal with $g^{rr} = 1$ and $g^{\phi\phi} = 1/r^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="Γ^r_φφ">
$\Gamma^r_{\phi\phi} = \tfrac12 g^{rr}(\partial_\phi g_{\phi r} + \partial_\phi g_{\phi r} - \partial_r g_{\phi\phi}) = \tfrac12(0 + 0 - 2r) = -r$.
</Step>
<Step title="Γ^φ_rφ">
$\Gamma^\phi_{r\phi} = \tfrac12 g^{\phi\phi}(\partial_r g_{\phi\phi} + \partial_\phi g_{r\phi} - \partial_\phi g_{r\phi}) = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{1}{r^2}\cdot 2r = 1/r$. Both agree with the values read off the derivatives of the basis vectors, in two lines instead of a page.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Special relativity means a global inertial frame

With the mathematics in place, Hughes turns to physics (p. 17). Everything so far, curvilinear coordinates included, has been special relativity. He now defines special relativity more sharply (p. 18): it is the theory in which inertial (Lorentz) reference frames cover the **entire** spacetime, with Lorentz transformations between them. If special relativity were the whole story, one inertial frame would describe the whole universe. Such a frame is called a **global** inertial frame, "global" in the mathematical sense of covering everything, not in the sense of the Earth.

Gravity breaks this (p. 18). With gravity there are no global inertial frames, only **local** ones, inertial coordinates that are good over a limited region of spacetime; how large that region can be is made precise a few lectures later. The argument comes in two parts and follows Alfred Schild, a mathematically minded early relativist; it is hand-waving, but it shows physically why gravity causes trouble (p. 18).

## Part one: the gravitational redshift

Part one shows that light loses frequency as it climbs (pp. 19–20). Hughes tells it as a deliberately silly story. Drop a rock of rest mass $m$ from the top of a tower of height $h$. At the bottom an ideal machine turns the rock into a single photon, conserving energy. Just before conversion, by Newtonian physics plus the rest energy, the rock has energy $m + mgh$ (that is, $mc^2 + mgh$), so the photon has

<Formula label="Photon energy at the bottom" tex="\hbar\,\omega_{\text{bottom}} = m + m g h" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 19" />

The photon flies up to the top, where a second ideal machine turns it back into a rock. If the photon still had the same frequency there, the rebuilt rock would have more energy than the one dropped; dropping it again and repeating would make a perpetual motion machine (pp. 19–20). Energy conservation therefore demands that the photon arrive at the top with energy $m$ only, and so

<Formula label="Gravitational redshift" tex="\frac{\omega_{\text{top}}}{\omega_{\text{bottom}}} = \frac{m}{m + m g h} \approx 1 - \frac{g h}{c^2}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 20" />

with $c$ restored. Light climbing out of a gravitational field gets redder. However silly the machines, the effect is real and very precisely measured: first by the Pound–Rebka experiment at Harvard, and it must be built into the Global Positioning System, whose clocks sit higher in the Earth's field than the receivers on the ground (p. 20).

<WorkedExample title="A redshift up a tall building">
<Problem>
Light is sent from the ground to the top of a 50 m building. By what fraction does its frequency drop?
</Problem>
<Step title="Use the redshift formula">
$\Delta\omega/\omega \approx g h/c^2 = (9.81\ \text{m/s}^2)(50\ \text{m})/(2.998\times 10^8\ \text{m/s})^2 \approx 5.46\times 10^{-15}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Interpret">
A shift of a few parts in $10^{15}$: tiny, which is why its measurement needed the extremely sharp gamma-ray lines that Pound and Rebka used, but far from zero.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Part two, left for the next lecture, shows that in a global inertial frame light could not get redder as it climbs (p. 20). Together the two parts say that gravity and a global inertial frame cannot both exist, and that is where general relativity begins (p. 21).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The same formula in the expanding universe">
The Christoffel formula derived here is the one Guth uses in 8.286 to simplify the geodesic equation in the Robertson–Walker metric (see the prerequisite page on Christoffel symbols). In cosmology the metric components depend on time through the scale factor, so Christoffel symbols with a time index appear, and they are what make the momentum of a freely moving particle fall off as the universe expands.
</Callout>

## Summary

- In a coordinate basis, polar basis vectors are not unit vectors: $\vec e_\phi$ has length $r$. The metric is $\mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, r^2, 1)$, and the interval comes out as it should.
- Basis vectors change from place to place: $\partial_\beta\vec e_\alpha = \Gamma^\mu_{\beta\alpha}\vec e_\mu$ defines the Christoffel symbols, which are not tensors. In polar coordinates $\Gamma^\phi_{r\phi} = \Gamma^\phi_{\phi r} = 1/r$ and $\Gamma^r_{\phi\phi} = -r$.
- The covariant derivative $\nabla_\beta V^\alpha = \partial_\beta V^\alpha + \Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\mu}V^\mu$ gives tensor components; one-forms get $-\Gamma$; each index of a tensor gets its own correction; scalars need none.
- A tensor equation true in one representation is true in all. Hence $\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} = \Gamma^\mu_{\beta\alpha}$, $\nabla_\gamma g_{\alpha\beta} = 0$, and $\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} = \tfrac12 g^{\mu\gamma}(\partial_\alpha g_{\beta\gamma} + \partial_\beta g_{\alpha\gamma} - \partial_\gamma g_{\alpha\beta})$.
- Special relativity means one inertial frame covers all of spacetime. Gravity allows only local inertial frames. Part one of Schild's argument: energy conservation forces light climbing a height $h$ to be redshifted by $gh/c^2$.
