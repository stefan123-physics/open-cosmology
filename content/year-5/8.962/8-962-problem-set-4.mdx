## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit named in the question. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `g*(1 + g*x)^2` or `exp(-x/w0)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. It treats its letters as independent. Type `x` for the coordinate $\bar x$ (no bar), `w0` for $w_0$, `th` for $\theta$, `vth` and `vph` for $v^\theta$ and $v^\phi$, `mu` for $\mu$ and `s` for $\lambda^*$; each box names its letters.

Give a number answer to 3 significant figures and keep 4 in intermediate steps; an answer with only 2 significant figures may or may not be accepted. Where a box needs more digits, its label says so.

MIT's sheet gives no points. The points here, and their split between parts, are ours. Much of this set asks you to show something. Those parts have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The conventions are those of the lessons. The speed of light is 1, except in Problems 2(d) and 2(e), where the sheet asks for $c$ to be put back. Newton's constant $G$ is kept, as on the sheet. The metric has the signature $(-, +, +, +)$: in flat spacetime it is $\eta_{\alpha\beta} = \mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$. Greek indices run over the four coordinates, Latin indices $i, j, k$ over the three space coordinates, and a repeated index, once up and once down, is summed over. $\partial_\alpha$ means $\partial/\partial x^\alpha$, and $\nabla_\alpha$ is the covariant derivative. A vector with an arrow, such as $\vec U$, is a 4-vector with components $U^\alpha$; its index is lowered with the metric, $U_\alpha = g_{\alpha\beta}U^\beta$. For a fluid, $\rho$ is the energy density and $P$ the pressure, both measured in the rest frame of the fluid. The letter $g$ has two uses, as on the sheet: with indices, $g_{\alpha\beta}$ and $g^{\alpha\beta}$ are the metric and its inverse; without indices, $g$ is an acceleration. Several answers depend on the sign convention of the metric or of the force law; the solutions say where.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The connection of Rindler spacetime" points={8} mit="Problem 1">

An astronaut moves through flat spacetime with a constant acceleration $g$, as felt on board. MIT's Problem Set 2 builds coordinates $\bar t, \bar x, \bar y, \bar z$ in which the astronaut stays at $\bar x = 0$ and $\bar t$ is the astronaut's own proper time, and finds the line element

<Formula label="Problem 1: the line element of Rindler spacetime" tex="ds^2 = -\left(1 + g\bar x\right)^2 d\bar t^2 + d\bar x^2 + d\bar y^2 + d\bar z^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, Eq. (1), p. 1" />

Spacetime described this way is called Rindler spacetime. It is still flat spacetime: only the coordinates are new. They are used for $1 + g\bar x > 0$.

On this page the bars stay on the coordinates, but an index is written without its bar: $\Gamma^x_{tt}$ means the Christoffel symbol with all three indices in the barred coordinates, and $U^t$ is the $\bar t$ component of $\vec U$. That shorthand is ours.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Compute all Christoffel symbols of this spacetime that are not zero. Type $\Gamma^t_{tx}$ and $\Gamma^x_{tt}$ in the boxes, with `g` and `x` (for $\bar x$). Then count: how many of the 64 symbols $\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}$ are not zero, if $\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}$ and $\Gamma^\mu_{\beta\alpha}$ count as two when $\alpha \ne \beta$? (The count is our addition.)

<Hint>

Only one component of the metric depends on a coordinate, and only on one coordinate. So only one derivative of the metric is not zero. Which Christoffel symbols can contain it?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Use the formula of the lesson that gives $\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}$ from derivatives of the metric. First find the symbols with all indices down, then raise the first index with the inverse metric. The metric is diagonal, so its inverse is diagonal too, with the inverse entries.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** A Christoffel symbol is built from first derivatives of the metric. Here only $\partial_x g_{tt}$ is not zero, so only symbols with two indices $t$ and one index $x$ survive.

<Step title="The metric and its one derivative">

In the coordinates $(\bar t, \bar x, \bar y, \bar z)$ the metric is diagonal:

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the Rindler metric and its inverse" tex="g_{tt} = -\left(1 + g\bar x\right)^2, \quad g_{xx} = g_{yy} = g_{zz} = 1, \qquad g^{tt} = -\frac{1}{\left(1 + g\bar x\right)^2}, \quad g^{xx} = g^{yy} = g^{zz} = 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The only derivative of a metric component that is not zero is

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the derivative of the metric" tex="\partial_x g_{tt} = -2g\left(1 + g\bar x\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The symbols with all indices down">

The lesson's formula is

<Formula label="Christoffel symbols from the metric" tex="\Gamma_{\gamma\alpha\beta} = \tfrac12\left(\partial_\alpha g_{\beta\gamma} + \partial_\beta g_{\alpha\gamma} - \partial_\gamma g_{\alpha\beta}\right), \qquad \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} = g^{\mu\gamma}\,\Gamma_{\gamma\alpha\beta}" cite="reading2" at="transcript pp. 16–17" />

Each term is a derivative $\partial_x g_{tt}$ or zero. So the three indices must be $t$, $t$ and $x$ in some order:

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the symbols with the indices down" tex="\Gamma_{xtt} = -\tfrac12\,\partial_x g_{tt} = g\left(1 + g\bar x\right), \qquad \Gamma_{ttx} = \Gamma_{txt} = \tfrac12\,\partial_x g_{tt} = -g\left(1 + g\bar x\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Raise the first index">

The inverse metric is diagonal, so raising the first index multiplies by $g^{xx} = 1$ or by $g^{tt}$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the Christoffel symbols of Rindler spacetime" tex="\Gamma^x_{tt} = g\left(1 + g\bar x\right), \qquad \Gamma^t_{tx} = \Gamma^t_{xt} = \frac{g}{1 + g\bar x}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

All other symbols are zero. Counting $\Gamma^t_{tx}$ and $\Gamma^t_{xt}$ separately, 3 of the 64 are not zero.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A second route is the geodesic equation. A geodesic makes the integral of $-\left(1 + g\bar x\right)^2\left(d\bar t/d\lambda\right)^2 + \left(d\bar x/d\lambda\right)^2$ over $\lambda$ stationary (the lesson on geodesics treats this route, transcript of Lecture 9, pp. 19 to 22). Its two Euler-Lagrange equations are

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the geodesic equations of Rindler spacetime" tex="\frac{d^2\bar x}{d\lambda^2} + g\left(1 + g\bar x\right)\left(\frac{d\bar t}{d\lambda}\right)^2 = 0, \qquad \frac{d^2\bar t}{d\lambda^2} + \frac{2g}{1 + g\bar x}\,\frac{d\bar x}{d\lambda}\,\frac{d\bar t}{d\lambda} = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Compare with the geodesic equation, $d^2x^\alpha/d\lambda^2 + \Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\gamma}\left(dx^\beta/d\lambda\right)\left(dx^\gamma/d\lambda\right) = 0$. The first gives $\Gamma^x_{tt}$. In the second the sum holds $\Gamma^t_{tx}$ and $\Gamma^t_{xt}$, so $2\Gamma^t_{tx} = 2g/(1 + g\bar x)$. Both agree. The units fit as well: with $c = 1$, $g$ is one over a length, and so is every Christoffel symbol here. For $g = 0$ all symbols vanish, as they must in inertial coordinates.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

(Our addition.) An observer stays at fixed $\bar x, \bar y, \bar z$. The 4-acceleration of any worldline with 4-velocity $\vec U$ is $a^\alpha = U^\beta\nabla_\beta U^\alpha$. Find the size $\sqrt{\vec a\cdot\vec a}$ of this observer's 4-acceleration, in terms of `g` and `x` (for $\bar x$). It is the acceleration that the observer feels.

<Hint>

The observer's 4-velocity has only a $t$ component. Its size follows from $\vec U\cdot\vec U = -1$ with the Rindler metric. It is not 1, except at $\bar x = 0$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The components $U^\alpha$ do not change along the worldline, so only the Christoffel term of $U^\beta\nabla_\beta U^\alpha$ is left.

</Hint>

    { expr: "g/(1 + g*x)^2", hint: "One power of (1 + g x) too many in the denominator. The square of the time component of the 4-velocity gives two powers, and the Christoffel symbol gives one back." }

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a worldline at rest in the coordinates the 4-acceleration is a Christoffel symbol times $(U^t)^2$.

<Step title="The 4-velocity">

With only a $t$ component, $\vec U\cdot\vec U = g_{tt}\left(U^t\right)^2 = -1$ gives

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): the 4-velocity of an observer at rest" tex="U^t = \frac{1}{1 + g\bar x}, \qquad U^x = U^y = U^z = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own addition)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The 4-acceleration">

The derivative along the worldline is the one the lessons use for transport along a curve:

<Formula label="Derivative of a vector along a curve" tex="U^\beta\nabla_\beta U^\alpha = \frac{dU^\alpha}{d\tau} + \Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\gamma}\,U^\beta U^\gamma" cite="reading5" at="transcript pp. 15–16" />

Here $dU^\alpha/d\tau = 0$, because $\bar x$ does not change along the worldline. The only term left has $\beta = \gamma = t$, and the only symbol $\Gamma^\alpha_{tt}$ that is not zero has $\alpha = x$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): the acceleration of an observer at rest" tex="a^x = \Gamma^x_{tt}\left(U^t\right)^2 = \frac{g\left(1 + g\bar x\right)}{\left(1 + g\bar x\right)^2} = \frac{g}{1 + g\bar x}, \qquad \sqrt{\vec a\cdot\vec a} = \sqrt{g_{xx}\,a^x a^x} = \frac{g}{1 + g\bar x}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own addition)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $\bar x = 0$ the size is $g$: that is the astronaut, as it should be. Observers in front of the astronaut ($\bar x > 0$) feel less, observers behind feel more, and the acceleration grows without limit as $1 + g\bar x \to 0$, where the coordinates end. The result also obeys $\vec a\cdot\vec U = 0$, which every 4-acceleration must: $\vec a$ has only an $x$ component and $\vec U$ only a $t$ component, and the metric is diagonal.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The relativistic Euler equation" points={30} mit="Problem 2">

A perfect fluid with 4-velocity $\vec U$, energy density $\rho$ and pressure $P$ has the stress-energy tensor

<Formula label="Problem 2: the stress-energy tensor of a perfect fluid" tex="\mathbf{T} = \rho\,\vec U\otimes\vec U + P\,\mathbf{h}, \qquad \mathbf{h} = \mathbf{g}^{-1} + \vec U\otimes\vec U" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1" />

Here $\mathbf{g}^{-1}$ is the inverse metric, and $\mathbf{h}$ is the tensor that projects onto the directions orthogonal to $\vec U$. In components, $T^{\mu\nu} = \rho\,U^\mu U^\nu + P\,h^{\mu\nu}$ with $h^{\mu\nu} = g^{\mu\nu} + U^\mu U^\nu$. This is the perfect fluid of the lesson, with the metric of a general spacetime in place of $\eta^{\mu\nu}$ (transcript of Lecture 5, p. 11).

<Part label="a" points={7}>

Energy and momentum are conserved locally: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{T} = 0$, in components $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$. Derive from it the relativistic Euler equation

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the relativistic Euler equation" tex="\left(\rho + P\right)\nabla_{\vec U}\vec U = -\mathbf{h}\cdot\nabla P, \qquad\text{in components}\qquad \left(\rho + P\right)U^\mu\nabla_\mu U^\nu = -h^{\nu\mu}\,\partial_\mu P" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), Eq. (2), p. 1" />

(The sheet notes that $\mathbf{T}$ and $\mathbf{h}$ are symmetric, so it does not matter which index a dot product uses. The form in components is ours.)

MIT's hint sheet suggests to look also at the part of $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{T} = 0$ that lies along $\vec U$. Work it out, and choose in the box what it says (the box is our addition).

<Hint>

This hint follows MIT's hint sheet. Writing out $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ as it stands gives an equation that is correct but mixes two statements. Call the divergence $j^\nu = \nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu}$ and split $j^\nu = 0$ in two: the part along the 4-velocity, $j^\nu U_\nu = 0$, and the part orthogonal to it, $h^\lambda{}_\nu\,j^\nu = 0$. The second is the Euler equation.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Two facts do most of the work. The covariant derivative of the metric is zero, so the metric passes through $\nabla_\mu$. And $U^\nu U_\nu = -1$ everywhere, so its derivative is zero: what does that say about $U_\nu\nabla_\mu U^\nu$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

It is easier to write $T^{\mu\nu} = \left(\rho + P\right)U^\mu U^\nu + P\,g^{\mu\nu}$ first. The projection of $U^\nu$ with $h^\lambda{}_\nu = \delta^\lambda{}_\nu + U^\lambda U_\nu$ is zero.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2a-parallel" label="The part of $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{T} = 0$ along $\vec U$ says that" choices={["the pressure gradient accelerates the fluid: it is the force law once more, written along the worldline of a fluid element instead of across it", "the 4-velocity keeps its length: it holds for any vector field of unit length and says nothing about ρ or P", "energy is conserved locally: the energy density of a fluid element changes as its volume changes, by dilution and by the work of the pressure", "the number of particles in a fluid element is conserved, whatever the pressure of the fluid is"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Take the divergence with the product rule, then separate the part along $\vec U$ from the part orthogonal to it, as MIT's hint sheet advises. The orthogonal part is the Euler equation.

<Step title="The divergence">

Write the tensor as $T^{\mu\nu} = \left(\rho + P\right)U^\mu U^\nu + P\,g^{\mu\nu}$. The covariant derivative obeys the product rule, the metric has no covariant derivative (transcript of Lecture 6, p. 15), and on a scalar $\nabla_\mu = \partial_\mu$. Following the hint sheet, call the divergence $j^\nu$:

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the divergence of the stress-energy tensor" tex="j^\nu \equiv \nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = U^\nu\,\nabla_\mu\!\left[\left(\rho + P\right)U^\mu\right] + \left(\rho + P\right)U^\mu\nabla_\mu U^\nu + g^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_\mu P = 0" cite="reading7" at="p. 1 (the definition of j; the expansion is ours)" />

That local conservation in a curved spacetime reads $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$, with the covariant derivative in place of the partial one, is the equivalence principle at work (transcript of Lecture 9, p. 13).

</Step>

<Step title="A useful fact">

A 4-velocity has $U^\nu U_\nu = -1$ at every event. Differentiate: $\nabla_\mu\left(U^\nu U_\nu\right) = 2\,U_\nu\nabla_\mu U^\nu = 0$. So

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the acceleration is orthogonal to the 4-velocity" tex="U_\nu\,\nabla_\mu U^\nu = 0, \qquad\text{and so}\qquad U_\nu\,U^\mu\nabla_\mu U^\nu = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The part along U">

Contract $j^\nu$ with $U_\nu$. The first term gets $U^\nu U_\nu = -1$, the second vanishes by the useful fact, and the third becomes $U^\mu\partial_\mu P$:

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the energy equation" tex="j^\nu U_\nu = -\nabla_\mu\!\left[\left(\rho + P\right)U^\mu\right] + U^\mu\partial_\mu P = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad U^\mu\partial_\mu\rho + \left(\rho + P\right)\nabla_\mu U^\mu = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

$U^\mu\partial_\mu\rho$ is the rate of change of the energy density along the worldline of a fluid element, and $\nabla_\mu U^\mu$ is the rate at which the volume of the element grows, per unit volume. So the equation is $d\rho/d\tau = -\left(\rho + P\right)\times$ (expansion rate): the energy density falls because the same energy fills more volume ($\rho$) and because the element does work on its surroundings ($P$). It is the first law of thermodynamics, $dE = -P\,dV$, for a fluid element without heat flow. That is the third choice in the box.

</Step>

<Step title="The part orthogonal to U">

The projection tensor with one index down is $h^\lambda{}_\nu = \delta^\lambda{}_\nu + U^\lambda U_\nu$. It removes anything along $\vec U$: $h^\lambda{}_\nu U^\nu = U^\lambda - U^\lambda = 0$. Apply it to the three terms of $j^\nu$. The first is along $U^\nu$ and drops out. The second is unchanged, by the useful fact. The third gives $h^\lambda{}_\nu\,g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu P = h^{\lambda\mu}\partial_\mu P$. So

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the Euler equation derived" tex="h^\lambda{}_\nu\,j^\nu = \left(\rho + P\right)U^\mu\nabla_\mu U^\lambda + h^{\lambda\mu}\,\partial_\mu P = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

which is the relativistic Euler equation: $\left(\rho + P\right)\nabla_{\vec U}\vec U = -\mathbf{h}\cdot\nabla P$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Read it as mass times acceleration equals force. $U^\mu\nabla_\mu U^\lambda$ is the 4-acceleration of a fluid element. The force is minus the pressure gradient, projected orthogonally to $\vec U$: in the rest frame of the element, the gradient in space. The inertia per unit volume is $\rho + P$ and not $\rho$ alone: pressure adds to inertia. For dust, $P = 0$, the equation becomes $U^\mu\nabla_\mu U^\lambda = 0$: each grain of dust moves on a geodesic (transcript of Lecture 9, p. 16), as free particles should. Both sides are orthogonal to $\vec U$: the left side by the useful fact, the right side because $h^{\lambda\mu}U_\lambda = 0$. With the signature $(+, -, -, -)$ the projection tensor is $g^{\mu\nu} - U^\mu U^\nu$ and some signs change; the content is the same.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Take a nonrelativistic fluid in flat spacetime: the pressure is much smaller than the energy density, $P \ll \rho$, and the fluid moves slowly, $U^i \ll U^t$, so that $U^i$ is close to the ordinary velocity $v^i$. <Step title="The left side">

Take the space components, $\nu = i$. With $U^\mu = \gamma\left(1, v^i\right)$ and $\gamma = 1$ up to terms of order $v^2$, the derivative along the flow is $U^\mu\partial_\mu = \partial_t + v^k\partial_k$, and

<Formula label="Problem 2(b): the left side for slow motion" tex="\left(\rho + P\right)U^\mu\partial_\mu U^i \approx \rho\left(\frac{\partial v^i}{\partial t} + v^k\,\partial_k v^i\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The condition $P \ll \rho$ turned $\rho + P$ into $\rho$. <Step title="The right side">

<Formula label="Problem 2(b): the right side for slow motion" tex="-h^{i\mu}\,\partial_\mu P = -\partial_i P - \gamma^2 v^i\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial t} + v^k\,\partial_k P\right) \approx -\partial_i P" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Formula label="Problem 2(b): the Euler equation with a connection" tex="\frac{\partial v^i}{\partial t} + v^k\,\partial_k v^i + \Gamma^i_{tt} + 2\,\Gamma^i_{tk}\,v^k + \Gamma^i_{jk}\,v^jv^k = -\frac{1}{\rho}\,g^{ij}\,\partial_j P" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="Problem 2(b): the Christoffel symbols of spherical coordinates" tex="\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = -r, \quad \Gamma^r_{\phi\phi} = -r\sin^2\theta, \quad \Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} = \Gamma^\phi_{r\phi} = \frac{1}{r}, \quad \Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi} = -\sin\theta\cos\theta, \quad \Gamma^\phi_{\theta\phi} = \frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The left side">

As in Problem 1(b), a fluid at rest has only $U^t$, and its components do not change along the worldline, so

<Formula label="Problem 3(a): the acceleration of a fluid at rest" tex="U^\mu\nabla_\mu U^\nu = \Gamma^\nu_{tt}\left(U^t\right)^2, \qquad \Gamma^\nu_{tt} = -\tfrac12\,g^{\nu\nu}\,\partial_\nu g_{tt} \quad\text{(no sum over } \nu\text{)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The second formula holds because the metric is diagonal and does not depend on $t$. Since $g_{tt} = -e^{2\Phi(r)}$ depends only on $r$, the components $\nu = \theta$, $\phi$ and $t$ are zero.

</Step>

<Step title="The right side">

With $U^\theta = U^\phi = 0$ the projection tensor has $h^{\theta\mu}\partial_\mu P = g^{\theta\theta}\partial_\theta P = \partial_\theta P/r^2$ and $h^{\phi\mu}\partial_\mu P = \partial_\phi P/\left(r^2\sin^2\theta\right)$. The Euler equation sets both to zero:

<Formula label="Problem 3(a): no angular pressure gradient" tex="\partial_\theta P = 0, \qquad \partial_\phi P = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Nothing depends on $t$ either, so $P = P(r)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The $t$ component gives no further condition: its left side is $\Gamma^t_{tt}\left(U^t\right)^2 = 0$, and its right side is $-\left(g^{tt} + U^tU^t\right)\partial_tP$, which is zero twice over: $\partial_tP = 0$, and $g^{tt} + \left(U^t\right)^2 = -e^{-2\Phi} + e^{-2\Phi} = 0$. The result is what spherical symmetry demands: a pressure that differed between two points on the same sphere would push fluid along the sphere.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Show that

<Formula label="Problem 3(b): relativistic hydrostatic equilibrium" tex="\frac{\partial P}{\partial r} = -\left(\rho + P\right)\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial r}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2" />

First choose in the box the radial component of the acceleration term, $U^\mu\nabla_\mu U^r$, for the fluid at rest (the box is our addition).

<Hint>

Find $U^t$ from $\vec U\cdot\vec U = -1$. Then $\Gamma^r_{tt}$ from the formula with $g^{rr}$ and $\partial_r g_{tt}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

On the right side the projection tensor reduces to $g^{rr}$, the inverse of the $rr$ component of the metric. Compare the factors on the two sides.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3b-acc" label="$U^\mu\nabla_\mu U^r =$" choices={["dΦ/dr", "e^(2Φ) (1 − 2GM/r) dΦ/dr", "−e^(−2Φ) dΦ/dr", "(1 − 2GM/r) dΦ/dr"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The radial acceleration term and the radial pressure gradient both carry the factor $g^{rr}$. It cancels, and the function $M(r)$ leaves the equation.

<Step title="The acceleration term">

From $g_{tt}\left(U^t\right)^2 = -1$: $U^t = e^{-\Phi}$. With $g^{rr} = 1 - 2GM/r$ and $\partial_r g_{tt} = -2\,e^{2\Phi}\,\partial_r\Phi$:

<Formula label="Problem 3(b): the radial acceleration of the fluid" tex="\Gamma^r_{tt} = -\tfrac12\,g^{rr}\,\partial_r g_{tt} = \left(1 - \frac{2GM}{r}\right)e^{2\Phi}\,\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial r}, \qquad U^\mu\nabla_\mu U^r = \Gamma^r_{tt}\left(U^t\right)^2 = \left(1 - \frac{2GM}{r}\right)\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial r}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The Euler equation">

On the right side $h^{r\mu}\partial_\mu P = g^{rr}\,\partial_rP$, because $U^r = 0$. So the $r$ component of the Euler equation reads

<Formula label="Problem 3(b): the radial Euler equation" tex="\left(\rho + P\right)\left(1 - \frac{2GM}{r}\right)\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial r} = -\left(1 - \frac{2GM}{r}\right)\frac{\partial P}{\partial r}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The line element is used where $1 - 2GM/r > 0$, so the common factor can be divided out: $\partial P/\partial r = -\left(\rho + P\right)\partial\Phi/\partial r$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For a nonrelativistic fluid, $P \ll \rho$, it becomes $dP/dr = -\rho\,d\Phi/dr$: Newtonian hydrostatic equilibrium, with $\Phi$ the Newtonian potential, as the Euler equation with gravity of Problem 2(e) gives for a fluid at rest. In general relativity the pressure adds to the inertia, and so to the weight. The equation says nothing about what $\Phi$ and $M$ are: that takes the field equation, later in the course. For constant $\Phi$ the pressure is constant: no gravity, no pressure gradient. The size of the 4-acceleration is $\sqrt{g_{rr}}\,a^r = \sqrt{1 - 2GM/r}\;\partial\Phi/\partial r$, with one factor $\sqrt{g^{rr}}$ fewer than the component $a^r$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="From a non-affine to an affine parameter" points={6} mit="Problem 4">

A curve $x^\alpha(\lambda^*)$ has the tangent $v^\alpha = dx^\alpha/d\lambda^*$, and the tangent obeys

<Formula label="Problem 4: the geodesic equation with a non-affine parameter" tex="\frac{Dv^\alpha}{d\lambda^*} = \kappa(\lambda^*)\,v^\alpha" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 2" />

where $D/d\lambda^*$ is the covariant derivative along the curve, $Dv^\alpha/d\lambda^* = v^\beta\nabla_\beta v^\alpha$. The tangent is carried along itself up to a change of length: the curve is a geodesic, but $\lambda^*$ is not an affine parameter.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Show that with a new parameter $\lambda$ the tangent $u^\alpha = dx^\alpha/d\lambda$ obeys the geodesic equation in its usual form,

<Formula label="Problem 4: the geodesic equation with an affine parameter" tex="\frac{Du^\alpha}{d\lambda} = 0 \qquad\text{provided that}\qquad \frac{d\lambda}{d\lambda^*} = \exp\left[\int\kappa(\lambda^*)\,d\lambda^*\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 2" />

<Hint>

By the chain rule the two tangents are proportional: $v^\alpha = u^\alpha\,d\lambda/d\lambda^*$. The same factor relates $D/d\lambda^*$ to $D/d\lambda$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Call the factor $f(\lambda^*) = d\lambda/d\lambda^*$. The derivative $D/d\lambda^*$ of the product $f\,u^\alpha$ obeys the product rule, and on the scalar $f$ it is an ordinary derivative.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Changing the parameter rescales the tangent. The rescaling adds a term along the tangent to the equation, and the right choice of parameter makes that term cancel $\kappa$.

<Step title="Relate the two tangents">

Let $f = d\lambda/d\lambda^*$. The chain rule gives

<Formula label="Problem 4(a): the two tangents" tex="v^\alpha = \frac{dx^\alpha}{d\lambda^*} = f\,u^\alpha, \qquad \frac{D}{d\lambda^*} = v^\beta\nabla_\beta = f\,u^\beta\nabla_\beta = f\,\frac{D}{d\lambda}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The derivative along a curve, $D/d\lambda = u^\beta\nabla_\beta$, is that of the lesson on parallel transport (transcript of Lecture 8, pp. 6 to 7).

</Step>

<Step title="Put it into the equation">

The left side of the given equation is, with the product rule,

<Formula label="Problem 4(a): the left side in the new parameter" tex="\frac{Dv^\alpha}{d\lambda^*} = \frac{D\left(f\,u^\alpha\right)}{d\lambda^*} = \frac{df}{d\lambda^*}\,u^\alpha + f\,\frac{Du^\alpha}{d\lambda^*} = \frac{df}{d\lambda^*}\,u^\alpha + f^2\,\frac{Du^\alpha}{d\lambda}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

and the right side is $\kappa\,v^\alpha = \kappa f\,u^\alpha$. So

<Formula label="Problem 4(a): the equation for the new tangent" tex="f^2\,\frac{Du^\alpha}{d\lambda} = \left(\kappa f - \frac{df}{d\lambda^*}\right)u^\alpha" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Choose the parameter">

The right side vanishes when $df/d\lambda^* = \kappa f$, that is $d\ln f/d\lambda^* = \kappa$, or

<Formula label="Problem 4(a): the condition on the new parameter" tex="f = \frac{d\lambda}{d\lambda^*} = \exp\left[\int\kappa(\lambda^*)\,d\lambda^*\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Then $Du^\alpha/d\lambda = 0$, since $f$ is not zero. The lesson states the non-affine form and names this exercise (transcript of Lecture 9, pp. 18 to 19).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The integral is fixed only up to a constant, which multiplies $f$ by a constant $a$; integrating $f$ once more to get $\lambda$ adds a second constant $b$. So $\lambda$ is fixed up to $\lambda \to a\lambda + b$: the freedom that affine parameters have (transcript of Lecture 9, p. 19). For $\kappa = 0$ the condition gives a constant $f$: $\lambda^*$ was already affine.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

(Our addition.) Let $\kappa(\lambda^*) = n/\lambda^*$ for $\lambda^* > 0$, with a constant $n$. Find $d\lambda/d\lambda^*$, with the constant of integration chosen so that $d\lambda/d\lambda^* = 1$ at $\lambda^* = 1$. Type it with `s` (for $\lambda^*$) and `n`.

<Hint>

Integrate $\kappa$ from 1 to $\lambda^*$, then take the exponential.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4b-f" label="$d\lambda/d\lambda^* =$" expr="s^n"
  vars={{ s: [0.5, 3], n: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ s: "\\lambda^*" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(n/s)", hint: "That is the exponential of κ itself. The exponent is the integral of κ over the parameter." },
    { expr: "n*ln(s)", hint: "That is the integral of κ. The rule asks for its exponential." },
    { expr: "s^(-n)", hint: "The sign of the exponent is wrong: the integral of n/s from 1 to s is +n ln s." },
    { expr: "exp(n*(s - 1))", hint: "That would follow for a constant κ = n. Here κ falls as 1 over the parameter, and its integral is a logarithm." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Apply the rule of part (a).

<Step title="The integral">

<Formula label="Problem 4(b): the rescaling for κ = n/λ*" tex="\frac{d\lambda}{d\lambda^*} = \exp\left[\int_1^{\lambda^*}\frac{n}{s}\,ds\right] = \exp\left(n\ln\lambda^*\right) = \left(\lambda^*\right)^n" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 2 (our own addition)" />

One more integration gives $\lambda = \left(\lambda^*\right)^{n+1}/(n + 1)$ plus a constant (for $n \ne -1$).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Try it on a straight line in flat space, in Cartesian coordinates, where $D/d\lambda^*$ is an ordinary derivative. The curve $x = \left(\lambda^*\right)^{n+1}/(n + 1)$, $y = z = 0$ has $v^x = \left(\lambda^*\right)^n$ and $dv^x/d\lambda^* = n\left(\lambda^*\right)^{n-1} = \left(n/\lambda^*\right)v^x$: it obeys the non-affine equation with $\kappa = n/\lambda^*$. With $\lambda = \left(\lambda^*\right)^{n+1}/(n + 1)$ the same line is $x = \lambda$, with $u^x = 1$ and $du^x/d\lambda = 0$: a straight line run through at a steady rate.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Conserved quantities with charge" points={8} mit="Problem 5">

A particle with electric charge $e$ and 4-velocity $u^\alpha$ moves in a spacetime with metric $g_{\alpha\beta}$, in which there is an electromagnetic field with vector potential $A_\mu$. Its equation of motion is

<Formula label="Problem 5: the equation of motion of a charged particle" tex="u^\beta\nabla_\beta u_\alpha = e\,F_{\alpha\beta}\,u^\beta, \qquad F_{\alpha\beta} = \nabla_\alpha A_\beta - \nabla_\beta A_\alpha" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 2" />

The sheet's equation contains no mass: read $e$ as the charge per unit mass of the particle (that reading is ours). The spacetime has a Killing vector field $\xi^\alpha$, and the potential shares the symmetry:

<Formula label="Problem 5: the symmetry of the metric and of the potential" tex="\mathcal{L}_\xi\,g_{\alpha\beta} = 0, \qquad \mathcal{L}_\xi A_\alpha = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 2" />

where $\mathcal{L}_\xi$ is the Lie derivative along $\vec\xi$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Show that the quantity $\left(u_\alpha + e\,A_\alpha\right)\xi^\alpha$ is constant along the worldline of the particle.

Then choose in the box (our addition): if the equation of motion had the other sign, $u^\beta\nabla_\beta u_\alpha = -e\,F_{\alpha\beta}u^\beta$ with the same $F_{\alpha\beta}$, which quantity would be constant?

<Hint>

The rate of change of a scalar along the worldline is $u^\beta\nabla_\beta$ of it. Apply the product rule to both products, $u_\alpha\xi^\alpha$ and $A_\alpha\xi^\alpha$: four terms.

</Hint>

<Hint>

One term is $u^\alpha u^\beta\nabla_\beta\xi_\alpha$. Killing's equation says that $\nabla_\beta\xi_\alpha$ is antisymmetric. What is an antisymmetric tensor contracted with the symmetric $u^\alpha u^\beta$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Use the equation of motion in the term with $u^\beta\nabla_\beta u_\alpha$. Half of $F_{\alpha\beta}$ cancels another term. What is left is $e\,u^\beta$ times the Lie derivative of $A_\beta$, written with covariant derivatives.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5a-sign" label="With the other sign in the equation of motion, the constant is" choices={["(u_α − e A_α) ξ^α", "(u_α + e A_α) ξ^α, as before", "u_α ξ^α alone", "e A_α ξ^α alone"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Differentiate along the worldline. Killing's equation removes one term, and the equation of motion turns the rest into the Lie derivative of the potential, which is zero.

<Step title="Four terms">

Along the worldline $d/d\tau = u^\beta\nabla_\beta$ (as in the lesson's proof for a free particle, transcript of Lecture 10, pp. 6 to 7). By the product rule,

<Formula label="Problem 5(a): the rate of change of the quantity" tex="u^\beta\nabla_\beta\left[\left(u_\alpha + eA_\alpha\right)\xi^\alpha\right] = \xi^\alpha\,u^\beta\nabla_\beta u_\alpha + u_\alpha\,u^\beta\nabla_\beta\xi^\alpha + e\,\xi^\alpha\,u^\beta\nabla_\beta A_\alpha + e\,A_\alpha\,u^\beta\nabla_\beta\xi^\alpha" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The second term: Killing's equation">

$\mathcal{L}_\xi\,g_{\alpha\beta} = 0$ is Killing's equation,

<Formula label="Killing's equation" tex="\nabla_\alpha\xi_\beta + \nabla_\beta\xi_\alpha = 0" cite="reading4" at="transcript p. 13" />

The second term is $u^\alpha u^\beta\nabla_\beta\xi_\alpha$ (the metric passes through the covariant derivative, so the index of $\xi$ can be lowered inside). It contracts the symmetric $u^\alpha u^\beta$ with the antisymmetric $\nabla_\beta\xi_\alpha$, so it is zero.

</Step>

<Step title="The first term: the equation of motion">

Put in $u^\beta\nabla_\beta u_\alpha = e\left(\nabla_\alpha A_\beta - \nabla_\beta A_\alpha\right)u^\beta$:

<Formula label="Problem 5(a): the first term" tex="\xi^\alpha\,u^\beta\nabla_\beta u_\alpha = e\,\xi^\alpha u^\beta\,\nabla_\alpha A_\beta - e\,\xi^\alpha u^\beta\,\nabla_\beta A_\alpha" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Its second piece cancels the third of the four terms. What is left of the whole sum is

<Formula label="Problem 5(a): what is left" tex="u^\beta\nabla_\beta\left[\left(u_\alpha + eA_\alpha\right)\xi^\alpha\right] = e\,u^\beta\left(\xi^\alpha\nabla_\alpha A_\beta + A_\alpha\nabla_\beta\xi^\alpha\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The Lie derivative of the potential">

The bracket is the Lie derivative of the one-form $A_\beta$ along $\vec\xi$:

<Formula label="Lie derivative of a one-form" tex="\mathcal{L}_\xi A_\beta = \xi^\alpha\nabla_\alpha A_\beta + A_\alpha\nabla_\beta\xi^\alpha" cite="reading4" at="transcript p. 11" />

It is zero by assumption. So $u^\beta\nabla_\beta\left[\left(u_\alpha + eA_\alpha\right)\xi^\alpha\right] = 0$: the quantity keeps its value along the worldline.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $e = 0$ this is the lesson's result for a free particle: $u_\alpha\xi^\alpha$ is constant along a geodesic. With a field, neither $u_\alpha\xi^\alpha$ nor $A_\alpha\xi^\alpha$ is constant, only the sum: energy or momentum moves between the particle and the field. The relative sign follows the convention. Replace $e$ by $-e$ in the equation of motion, with the same $F_{\alpha\beta}$, and the same steps give $\left(u_\alpha - eA_\alpha\right)\xi^\alpha$: the first choice in the box. The same happens if $F_{\alpha\beta}$ is defined with the opposite sign and the equation of motion is kept. One more remark: in $F_{\alpha\beta}$ the Christoffel terms of the two covariant derivatives cancel, because the symbols are symmetric in their lower indices, so $F_{\alpha\beta} = \partial_\alpha A_\beta - \partial_\beta A_\alpha$ as in flat spacetime.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

(Our addition.) Take flat spacetime with inertial coordinates $t, x, y, z$ and the potential $A_t = E\,x$, $A_x = A_y = A_z = 0$, with a constant $E$. With the sheet's conventions it describes a uniform electric field: $F_{xt} = E$, so a particle with $eE > 0$ is pushed along $+x$. The particle starts from rest at $x = 0$. Use a conserved quantity to find its Lorentz factor $\gamma = u^t$ when it is at position $x$. Type it with `e`, `E` and `x`.

<Hint>

Neither the metric nor the potential depends on $t$. Which Killing vector does that give, and what are its components?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Lower the index of $u^t$ with $\eta_{tt} = -1$, and fix the constant at the start.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5b-gamma" label="$\gamma =$" expr="1 + e*E*x"
  vars={{ e: [0.5, 2], E: [0.5, 2], x: [0.2, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1 - e*E*x", hint: "Check the sign of the lowered index: u_t = −γ. For eE > 0 the particle is pushed towards larger x and must gain energy there." },
    { expr: "e*E*x", hint: "Fix the constant at the start: at x = 0 the particle is at rest, so γ = 1 there." },
    { expr: "sqrt(1 + 2*e*E*x)", hint: "That makes γ² linear in x, as v² is for a constant Newtonian force. The conserved quantity is linear in the 4-velocity: γ itself is linear in x." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Time translation is a symmetry of both the metric and the potential, so $\left(u_\alpha + eA_\alpha\right)\xi^\alpha$ with $\xi^\alpha = (1, 0, 0, 0)$ is constant.

<Step title="The conserved quantity">

The components of $\eta_{\alpha\beta}$ and of $A_\alpha$ do not depend on $t$. For $\xi^\alpha = (1, 0, 0, 0)$ the Lie derivative of each is then just its $t$ derivative (the terms with derivatives of $\xi^\alpha$ vanish), which is zero. The conserved quantity is

<Formula label="Problem 5(b): the conserved energy" tex="\left(u_\alpha + eA_\alpha\right)\xi^\alpha = u_t + e\,A_t = -\gamma + e\,E\,x" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 2 (our own addition)" />

At the start, $x = 0$ and $\gamma = 1$, so it equals $-1$. Hence $\gamma = 1 + e\,E\,x$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Directly from the equation of motion: its $t$ component is $du_t/d\tau = e\,F_{tx}\,u^x = -e\,E\,dx/d\tau$, so $d\gamma/d\tau = e\,E\,dx/d\tau$ and $\gamma = 1 + eEx$ again. In words: the energy per unit mass, $\gamma$, grows by the work $eEx$ of the constant force. The $x$ component, $du_x/d\tau = e\,E\,\gamma$, is solved by $u^x = \sinh\left(eE\tau\right)$, $\gamma = \cosh\left(eE\tau\right)$ and $x = \left[\cosh\left(eE\tau\right) - 1\right]/(eE)$, which gives the same $\gamma(x)$. This is the motion with constant acceleration $eE$, the motion of the astronaut of Problem 1. With the signature $(+, -, -, -)$ one has $u_t = +\gamma$, and the signs of the potential terms change with it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
