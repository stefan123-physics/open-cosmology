## How to answer

Work each problem on paper (or, for Problem 3, on your computer) first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit named in the question. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `2*G*M/r^2`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. It treats its letters as independent, so each question says which letters to type: `Rstar` for $R_*$, `R0` for $R_0$, `rho0` for $\rho_0$, `rho` for $\rho$, `E` for $\hat E$, `L` for $\hat L$ and `Phi` for $\Phi$.

Give a number answer to 3 significant figures and keep 4 in intermediate steps; an answer with only 2 significant figures may or may not be accepted. Where a box needs more digits, its label says so.

MIT's set gives no points. The points here, and their split between parts, are ours. Several parts ask you to show something. Those parts have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The conventions are those of the lessons. The speed of light is 1, the metric has the signature $(-,+,+,+)$, and $d\Omega^2 = d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2$. An overdot is a derivative with respect to the time named in the problem.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Formation of a black hole" points={40} mit="Problem 1">

The Schwarzschild black hole of the lectures exists for all time. Here we see how one can form from something ordinary: a spherical star of pressureless dust that collapses. The star starts at rest with radius $R_*$ and mass $M$, and its dust is spread evenly. By Birkhoff's theorem the spacetime outside the star is the Schwarzschild spacetime:

<Formula label="Problem 1: the exterior" tex="ds^2 = -\left(1 - \frac{2GM}{r}\right)dt^2 + \frac{dr^2}{1 - 2GM/r} + r^2\,d\Omega^2 \qquad (r > R_*)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

Inside, the dust looks the same everywhere and in every direction, so a Robertson–Walker metric describes it. The star must fall in under its own weight, so we take the closed one:

<Formula label="Problem 1: the interior" tex="ds^2 = -d\tau^2 + a^2(\tau)\,R_0^2\left(d\chi^2 + \sin^2\chi\,d\Omega^2\right) \qquad (r < R_*)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

Two different times appear. Outside, $t$ is Schwarzschild time, the time of distant observers. Inside, $\tau$ is the proper time of each grain of dust.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

For a closed Robertson–Walker spacetime filled with pressureless dust, the scale factor has a closed-form solution, given with a parameter $\eta$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the parametric solution" tex="a = \frac{a_{\text{max}}}{2}\left(1 + \cos\eta\right), \qquad \tau = \frac{a_{\text{max}}R_0}{2}\left(\eta + \sin\eta\right), \qquad 0 \le \eta \le \pi" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

Show that it solves the Friedmann equations for $k = 1$ with dust. Since $a$ always appears multiplied by $R_0$, you may set $a_{\text{max}} = 1$. Then find the density $\rho_0$ at the start, $\eta = 0$, in terms of $R_0$ (type `R0` and `G`).

<Hint>

With the line element above, the curvature term of the first Friedmann equation is $1/(a^2R_0^2)$. Dust keeps $\rho a^3$ fixed, so $\rho = \rho_0/a^3$ with $a_{\text{max}} = 1$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Compute $da/d\tau$ as $(da/d\eta)/(d\tau/d\eta)$, and write $\sin^2\eta$ as $(1 - \cos\eta)(1 + \cos\eta)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1a-rho0" label="$\rho_0 =$" expr="3/(8*pi*G*R0^2)"
  vars={{ G: [0.5, 2], R0: [1, 10] }} names={{ R0: "R_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/(8*pi*G*R0^2)", hint: "The first Friedmann equation has 8πGρ/3: the factor 3 of the denominator has been lost on the way." },
    { expr: "sqrt(3/(8*pi*G))/R0", hint: "That is the square root of the answer: ρ₀ goes as 1/R₀², since Gρ has the unit of 1/time², which is 1/length² with c = 1." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the parametric $a$ and $da/d\tau$ into the first Friedmann equation. Every term then varies as $1/a$ or is constant, and both kinds match only if $\rho_0$ and $R_0$ are tied.

<Step title="The Friedmann equations">

For the closed metric written with $R_0$ the lesson's equations read

<Formula label="The Friedmann equations" tex="\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\,\rho - \frac{1}{a^2R_0^2}, \qquad \frac{\ddot a}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left(\rho + 3P\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 13" />

with $P = 0$ for dust and $\rho = \rho_0/a^3$. (The lesson writes the curvature term as $\kappa/a^2$; here $\kappa = 1/R_0^2$.)

</Step>

<Step title="The first equation">

$da/d\eta = -\frac12\sin\eta$ and $d\tau/d\eta = \frac{R_0}{2}(1 + \cos\eta) = R_0\,a$, so $\dot a = -\sin\eta/(2R_0a)$. With $\sin^2\eta = (1 - \cos\eta)(1 + \cos\eta) = 2a(1 - \cos\eta)$ and $1 - \cos\eta = 2 - 2a$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the left side" tex="\dot a^2 = \frac{\sin^2\eta}{4R_0^2a^2} = \frac{1 - a}{R_0^2\,a} = \frac{1}{R_0^2\,a} - \frac{1}{R_0^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The Friedmann equation times $a^2$ is $\dot a^2 = \frac{8\pi G\rho_0}{3a} - \frac{1}{R_0^2}$. The constant terms agree; the $1/a$ terms agree when

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): density and length scale" tex="\frac{8\pi G\rho_0}{3} = \frac{1}{R_0^2} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \rho_0 = \frac{3}{8\pi G R_0^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The second equation">

For dust the second equation follows from the first (differentiate it and use $\dot\rho = -3\rho\,\dot a/a$), but it can be checked directly. Since $\dot a = -\tan(\eta/2)/R_0$ and $\cos^2(\eta/2) = a$, $\ddot a = \frac{d\dot a/d\eta}{d\tau/d\eta} = -\frac{1}{2R_0 a}\cdot\frac{1}{R_0 a} = -\frac{1}{2R_0^2a^2}$, and $-\frac{4\pi G}{3}\rho\,a = -\frac{4\pi G\rho_0}{3a^2} = -\frac{1}{2R_0^2a^2}$. They agree.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: with $c = 1$, $G\rho$ is 1 over a time squared, which is 1 over a length squared, like $1/R_0^2$. At $\eta = 0$ the dust is at rest ($\dot a = 0$), as the problem requires, and the curvature radius $R_0$ is the one for which a closed universe of density $\rho_0$ just stops expanding there. In our calculation file the relation is checked at other values of $\eta$ with numerical derivatives of the parametric solution.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

The solution for the interior time is good only up to $\tau = \pi R_0/2$. What happens to the interior after that? The box is our addition.

<Hint>

Look at $a$ at $\eta = \pi$, and at what that does to $\rho = \rho_0/a^3$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1b-end" label="At $\tau = \pi R_0/2$:" choices={["a passes through zero smoothly, and the dust streams out through the centre to re-expand on the far side", "a stops shrinking at a small but finite size, set by how fast the dust was falling, and the star settles there", "a reaches zero: all the dust arrives at r = 0 together, and density and curvature become infinite", "a keeps shrinking but never reaches zero, which takes an infinite proper time of the dust"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At $\eta = \pi$ the scale factor reaches zero, at the same proper time for every grain.

<Step title="The crunch">

$\eta = \pi$ gives $\tau = \pi R_0/2$ and $a = 0$. The proper size of the star, $a R_0 \sin\chi$ in circumference over $2\pi$, is then zero: all the dust is at $r = 0$ at once, and $\rho = \rho_0/a^3$ is infinite. The curvature of a Robertson–Walker spacetime grows with $\rho$, so this is a true singularity, like the big crunch of a closed universe. The geodesics of the dust end there; the metric has no continuation, and the cycloid formula for $\eta > \pi$ (a re-expansion) does not describe anything physical.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The proper time to reach the crunch, $\pi R_0/2$, is finite: the dust gets there in its own time. Part (d) finds the same time from the outside, for the grains at the surface.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Take a purely radial orbit in the Schwarzschild spacetime ($\hat L = 0$) with energy per unit mass $\hat E$. Find the radius $R$ at which the radial velocity is zero, in terms of $G$, $M$ and $\hat E$ (type `E`). This defines the energy of a grain of dust at the surface of the star as it starts to fall.

<Hint>

The radial equation of the lesson for Lecture 23 holds for any $\hat L$. Set $\hat L = 0$ and $dr/d\tau = 0$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1c-turn" label="$R =$" expr="2*G*M/(1 - E^2)"
  vars={{ G: [0.5, 2], M: [1, 2], E: [0.2, 0.9] }} names={{ E: "\\hat E" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*G*M/(1 - E)", hint: "The radial equation holds the square of the energy: the turning point is where 1 − 2GM/R equals Ê²." },
    { expr: "2*G*M*(1 - E^2)", hint: "Solve 1 − 2GM/R = Ê² for R: 2GM is divided by 1 − Ê², not multiplied by it." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With $\hat L = 0$ the effective potential is $1 - 2GM/r$, and the body turns where it equals $\hat E^2$.

<Step title="The radial equation">

The lesson gives

<Formula label="Radial equation of a Schwarzschild geodesic" tex="\left(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\right)^2 = \hat E^2 - V_\text{eff}(r), \qquad V_\text{eff} = \left(1 - \frac{2GM}{r}\right)\left(1 + \frac{\hat L^2}{r^2}\right)" cite="reading4" at="transcript p. 5" />

</Step>

<Step title="The turning point">

With $\hat L = 0$, $dr/d\tau = 0$ where $1 - 2GM/R = \hat E^2$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): the radius of rest" tex="R = \frac{2GM}{1 - \hat E^2}, \qquad \hat E^2 = 1 - \frac{2GM}{R}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\hat E < 1$ gives $R > 2GM$: a bound body falling from rest outside the horizon. As $\hat E \to 1$, $R \to \infty$: a body that falls from rest at infinity has $\hat E = 1$. Far away, $\hat E^2 \approx 1 - 2GM/R$ says $\hat E \approx 1 - GM/R$: rest energy plus the Newtonian potential energy per unit mass.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={7}>

Use the radial equation and the relation of part (c), with $R = R_*$, to write the proper time $\tau$ that a grain at the surface takes to fall from $R_*$ to $r$. You should find

<Formula label="Problem 1(d): the proper time as an integral" tex="\tau = -\int_{R_*}^{r}\frac{dr'}{\sqrt{2GM/r' - 2GM/R_*}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 2" />

(the minus sign because the surface moves inward). With $r = \frac{R_*}{2}(1 + \cos\eta)$, $0 \le \eta \le \pi$, show that

<Formula label="Problem 1(d): the proper time in closed form" tex="\tau = \sqrt{\frac{R_*^3}{8GM}}\left(\eta + \sin\eta\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 2" />

Our box asks for the proper time the surface takes to reach $r = 0$ (type `Rstar` for $R_*$).

<Hint>

Express $2GM/r - 2GM/R_*$ in terms of $\eta$: it is $2GM/R_*$ times a function of $\eta$ that half-angle formulas make simple.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$1 + \cos\eta = 2\cos^2(\eta/2)$, $1 - \cos\eta = 2\sin^2(\eta/2)$ and $\sin\eta = 2\sin(\eta/2)\cos(\eta/2)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1d-tc" label="Proper time from $R_*$ to $r = 0$:" expr="pi*sqrt(Rstar^3/(8*G*M))"
  vars={{ G: [0.5, 2], M: [1, 2], Rstar: [5, 20] }} names={{ Rstar: "R_*" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "pi/2*sqrt(Rstar^3/(8*G*M))", hint: "The factor is η + sin η at η = π, which is π, not π/2: the surface reaches r = 0 at η = π." },
    { expr: "sqrt(Rstar^3/(8*G*M))", hint: "At r = 0 the parameter is η = π, so η + sin η = π: the factor π is missing." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For $\hat L = 0$ the radial equation gives $dr/d\tau$ directly. The cycloid parameter turns the integrand into $\cos^2(\eta/2)$.

<Step title="The integral">

With $\hat E^2 = 1 - 2GM/R_*$ from part (c), the radial equation with $\hat L = 0$ is $(dr/d\tau)^2 = 2GM/r - 2GM/R_*$. The surface falls, so $dr/d\tau$ is the negative root, and $d\tau = -dr/\sqrt{2GM/r - 2GM/R_*}$, which integrates to the formula of the statement.

</Step>

<Step title="The substitution">

With $r = \frac{R_*}{2}(1 + \cos\eta)$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(d): the integrand in terms of η" tex="\frac{2GM}{r} - \frac{2GM}{R_*} = \frac{2GM}{R_*}\cdot\frac{1 - \cos\eta}{1 + \cos\eta} = \frac{2GM}{R_*}\tan^2\frac{\eta}{2}, \qquad dr = -R_*\sin\frac{\eta}{2}\cos\frac{\eta}{2}\,d\eta" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

So $d\tau = \sqrt{\frac{R_*}{2GM}}\,R_*\cos^2\frac{\eta}{2}\,d\eta = \sqrt{\frac{R_*^3}{2GM}}\cdot\frac{1 + \cos\eta}{2}\,d\eta$, and integrating from $\eta = 0$ (where $r = R_*$) gives $\tau = \sqrt{R_*^3/(8GM)}\,(\eta + \sin\eta)$.

</Step>

<Step title="The collapse time">

The surface reaches $r = 0$ at $\eta = \pi$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(d): the time to collapse" tex="\tau = \pi\sqrt{\frac{R_*^3}{8GM}} \quad \text{at } r = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 2 (our own addition)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This is exactly the Newtonian free-fall time of a uniform ball, $\frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{R_*^3/(2GM)}$. Measured by the falling dust, relativity changes nothing in this time; the difference shows up in the Schwarzschild time $t$, in which the surface never quite reaches $r = 2GM$ (the lesson for Lecture 21 plots both). The same parametric form appears in that lesson for a rock dropped from rest.

</Step>

</Solution>

**The idea.** The proper times are both a constant times $(\eta + \sin\eta)$, which fixes $R_0$. The circumferences are both a constant times $(1 + \cos\eta)$, which then fixes $\sin\chi_s$.

<Step title="Proper time">

Inside, $\tau = \frac{R_0}{2}(\eta + \sin\eta)$; outside, $\tau = \sqrt{R_*^3/(8GM)}\,(\eta + \sin\eta)$. Equal for all $\eta$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(e): the length scale" tex="R_0 = 2\sqrt{\frac{R_*^3}{8GM}} = \sqrt{\frac{R_*^3}{2GM}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(e), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Circumference">

Inside, the circumference at $\chi_s$ is $2\pi\cdot\frac{1 + \cos\eta}{2}\,R_0\sin\chi_s$; outside it is $2\pi\cdot\frac{R_*}{2}(1 + \cos\eta)$. Equal for all $\eta$: $R_0\sin\chi_s = R_*$, so

<Formula label="Problem 1(e): the radius of the star in the interior coordinates" tex="\sin\chi_s = \frac{R_*}{R_0} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R_*}}, \qquad \chi_s = \arcsin\sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R_*}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(e), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Part (a) gives the density: $\rho_0 = \frac{3}{8\pi G R_0^2} = \frac{3}{8\pi G}\cdot\frac{2GM}{R_*^3} = \frac{3M}{4\pi R_*^3}$, the mass divided by $\frac{4\pi}{3}R_*^3$. (That is the volume counted with the areal radius, as $m(r)$ is in the lesson for Lecture 20; the proper volume of the curved interior is larger, and the difference is the binding energy.) $\sin\chi_s < 1$ needs $R_* > 2GM$: the star starts outside its own horizon. For $R_* \gg GM$, $\chi_s$ is small and the star is a small cap of the closed space.

</Step>

For the rest of the problem take $R_* = 5\,GM$. The horizon of a Schwarzschild black hole is a null surface, made of light rays that stay at $r = 2GM$. The horizon of a hole that forms in collapse is made of the outgoing light ray that leaves the centre of the star at just the right moment to reach the surface as the surface passes $r = 2GM$. From then on, by Birkhoff's theorem, the ray stays at $r = 2GM$.

**The idea.** The $tt$ equation fixes $\Lambda$ through $m$, exactly as for a star. The $rr$ equation holds the radial pressure, which is zero here, because no star moves radially. The pressure is all tangential.

<Step title="The tt equation">

The lesson's $tt$ equation, with $T^t{}_t = -\rho$ ($\rho$ the energy density measured by a static observer, kinetic energy of the stars included):

<Formula label="The tt field equation" tex="G^{t}{}_{t} = -\frac{1}{r^2}\,\frac{d}{dr}\left[r\left(1 - e^{-2\Lambda}\right)\right] = -8\pi G\rho" cite="reading2" at="transcript pp. 11–12" />

Integrating from $r = 0$: $r(1 - e^{-2\Lambda}) = 2Gm$, so $e^{2\Lambda} = \left(1 - 2Gm/r\right)^{-1}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The rr equation">

The lesson's $rr$ equation holds the radial pressure $P_r = T^r{}_r$:

<Formula label="The rr field equation" tex="G^{r}{}_{r} = e^{-2\Lambda}\left(\frac{2}{r}\,\frac{d\Phi}{dr} + \frac{1}{r^2}\right) - \frac{1}{r^2} = 8\pi G P_r" cite="reading2" at="transcript pp. 13–14" />

Each star moves along $\phi$ (in a plane of its own), never along $r$, so the flux of radial momentum is zero: $P_r = 0$. Then

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the field equations of the cluster" tex="e^{2\Lambda} = \frac{1}{1 - 2Gm/r}, \qquad \frac{d\Phi}{dr} = \frac{Gm}{r\left(r - 2Gm\right)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

<Step title="The crucial difference">

The TOV equation for $d\Phi/dr$ has $m + 4\pi r^3P$; here the pressure term is gone. And there is no equation $dP/dr = -(\rho + P)\,d\Phi/dr$ of hydrostatic equilibrium. What holds the cluster up is not a pressure gradient but the orbital motion: a tangential stress $P_t$. The radial part of $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ for an anisotropic stress reads $dP_r/dr = -(\rho + P_r)\Phi' + 2(P_t - P_r)/r$, which with $P_r = 0$ gives $P_t = \frac12\rho\,r\,\Phi'$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Outside the cluster $m = M$ and the two results give the Schwarzschild metric: $\frac{d}{dr}\left[\frac12\ln\left(1 - \frac{2GM}{r}\right)\right] = \frac{GM}{r(r - 2GM)}$. Far from strong fields, $d\Phi/dr \approx Gm/r^2$, Newton's field.

The lesson for Lecture 23 showed that a test body around a Schwarzschild black hole obeys

<Formula label="Problem 3: the equations of motion" tex="\left(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\right)^2 = \hat E^2 - V_\text{eff}(r), \qquad V_\text{eff} = \left(1 - \frac{2GM}{r}\right)\left(1 + \frac{\hat L^2}{r^2}\right), \qquad \frac{d\phi}{d\tau} = \frac{\hat L}{r^2}, \qquad \frac{dt}{d\tau} = \frac{\hat E}{1 - 2GM/r}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 4" />

Here you integrate these equations on a computer. The equation for $r$ has an awkward feature: at a turning point $dr/d\tau$ changes sign, and the square root would have to switch branches. The sheet's advice: take one more derivative of both sides. Use units with $GM = 1$, that is, measure $r$, $t$ and $\tau$ in units of $GM$ and $\hat L$ in units of $GM$ (with $c = 1$).

<Part label="a" points={5}>

In these units, what are the unit of time and the unit of length if $M = 10$ solar masses? Use $GM_\odot = 1.32712\times 10^{20}$ m³/s² (NASA's Sun fact sheet) and $c = 299\,792\,458$ m/s (exact, CODATA 2018). Give the time unit in microseconds and the length unit in kilometres.

Our box: the second-order equation for $r$ that the derivative gives, with $GM = 1$ (type `L` for $\hat L$).

<Hint>

The units are $GM/c^3$ and $GM/c^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Differentiate $(dr/d\tau)^2 = \hat E^2 - V_\text{eff}$ with respect to $\tau$, and divide by $dr/d\tau$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3a-t" label="Unit of time:" value="49.25" unit="μs" tolerance="1%" calc="p3a-t" />

<Answer id="p3a-l" label="Unit of length:" value="14.77" unit="km" tolerance="1%" calc="p3a-l" />

<Answer id="p3a-rdd" label="$d^2r/d\tau^2 =$" expr="-1/r^2 + L^2/r^3 - 3*L^2/r^4"
  vars={{ r: [5, 20], L: [3, 6] }} names={{ L: "\\hat L" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-2/r^2 + 2*L^2/r^3 - 6*L^2/r^4", hint: "That is −dV_eff/dr. The derivative of (dr/dτ)² is 2(dr/dτ)(d²r/dτ²): divide by 2." },
    { expr: "-1/r^2 + L^2/r^3", hint: "That is Newton's equation. The term −2L̂²/r³ of V_eff (with GM = 1) adds −3L̂²/r⁴." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $GM$ has the unit m³/s²; dividing by $c^3$ or $c^2$ turns it into a time or a length. Differentiating the energy equation removes the square root.

<Step title="The units">

$GM = 10\times 1.32712\times 10^{20}$ m³/s², so

<Formula label="Problem 3(a): the units for a ten-solar-mass hole" tex="\frac{GM}{c^3} = \frac{1.32712\times 10^{21}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}^2}{\left(2.99792458\times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}\right)^3} = 4.925\times 10^{-5}\ \text{s}, \qquad \frac{GM}{c^2} = 1.4766\times 10^{4}\ \text{m}" cite="nasasun" at="GM of the Sun; c from the CODATA 2018 values (our own arithmetic)" />

that is, 49.25 μs and 14.77 km.

</Step>

<Step title="The second-order equation">

$\frac{d}{d\tau}\left(\dot r^2\right) = 2\dot r\ddot r = -V_\text{eff}'(r)\,\dot r$, so $\ddot r = -\frac12 V_\text{eff}'$. With $V_\text{eff} = 1 - \frac{2}{r} + \frac{\hat L^2}{r^2} - \frac{2\hat L^2}{r^3}$:

<Formula label="Problem 3(a): the equation we integrate" tex="\frac{d^2r}{d\tau^2} = -\frac{1}{r^2} + \frac{\hat L^2}{r^3} - \frac{3\hat L^2}{r^4}, \qquad \frac{d\phi}{d\tau} = \frac{\hat L}{r^2}, \qquad \frac{dt}{d\tau} = \frac{\hat E}{1 - 2/r}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

This has no square root and passes smoothly through turning points. $\hat E$ now enters only through the starting value of $dr/d\tau$, $\pm\sqrt{\hat E^2 - V_\text{eff}(r_0)}$, and the energy equation becomes a check on the accuracy: $\dot r^2 + V_\text{eff}$ must stay equal to $\hat E^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The horizon radius $2GM$ is 29.5 km, as it should be for ten solar masses (about 2.95 km per solar mass). The first two terms are Newton's gravity plus the centrifugal term; the third is the relativistic term that makes the plunge possible.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="b" points={7}>

An unbound orbit: it comes in from infinity, passes near the hole and goes back out. How close can it come without being captured? The numbers are ours: take $\hat E = 1.1$, and give the largest $\hat L$ that leads to capture and the smallest radius an escaping body can reach, both in units of $GM$. The choice box is our addition.

<Hint>

The body is captured if $\hat E^2$ is above the peak of $V_\text{eff}$. The borderline case has the peak exactly at $\hat E^2$, and the peak is an unstable circular orbit.

</Hint>

<Hint>

On an unstable circular orbit, $\hat E$ is given by the formula of the lesson for Lecture 23 as a function of $r$. Solve it for $r$ with $\hat E = 1.1$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3b-lc" label="Critical $\hat L$:" value="4.725" unit="GM" tolerance="1%" calc="p3b-lc" />

<Step title="The borderline orbit">

The lesson's energy of a circular orbit is $\hat E^2 = (1 - 2/r)^2/(1 - 3/r)$ (with $GM = 1$). Setting it equal to $1.21$ gives a quadratic, $(r - 2)^2 = 1.21\,r(r - 3)$, whose root above $3$ is $r = 25/7 = 3.571$. There

<Formula label="Problem 3(b): the critical angular momentum" tex="\hat L_c^2 = \frac{r^2}{r - 3} = \frac{(25/7)^2}{4/7} = \frac{625}{28}, \qquad \hat L_c = 4.725" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

<Step title="Our numerical runs">

We started each run at $r = 200$ moving inward, with $\hat E = 1.1$:

<Part label="c" points={7}>

A bound "elliptical" orbit. You will soon see that such orbits are not closed ellipses, as in Newton's theory, but at best precessing ellipses. Try to make an orbit whose innermost and outermost radii are as far apart as possible. Our example: turning points at $r = 10\,GM$ and $r = 50\,GM$. Give $\hat E$, $\hat L$ (in units of $GM$) and the angle, in degrees, by which the innermost point advances in one radial cycle. The choice box is our addition.

<Hint>

At both turning points $V_\text{eff}(r) = \hat E^2$. Two equations, two unknowns; subtracting them gives $\hat L^2$ first.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The total angle swept from one innermost point to the next is $2\int \frac{\hat L}{r^2}\,\frac{dr}{\sqrt{\hat E^2 - V_\text{eff}}}$ between the turning points. Your integrator gives it directly; so does the integral, after a substitution that removes the square-root singularities at the ends.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3c-en" label="$\hat E$:" value="0.9839" tolerance="0.1%" calc="p3c-en" />

<Answer id="p3c-ang" label="$\hat L$:" value="4.583" unit="GM" tolerance="1%" calc="p3c-ang" />

<Answer id="p3c-adv" label="Advance per radial cycle:" value="91.3" unit="degrees" tolerance="1%" calc="p3c-adv" />

<Answer id="p3c-lim" label="For bound orbits, the innermost point can come as close as:" choices={["6GM, since a bound orbit cannot reach inside the innermost stable circular orbit", "3GM, the circular orbit of light", "4GM, approached as Ê tends to 1 and the outermost point moves away", "2GM, the horizon, approached as L̂ tends to zero"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The two turning points fix $\hat E$ and $\hat L$. The angle per radial cycle is more than $360°$ because the relativistic term pulls the body in near the innermost point.

<Step title="Energy and angular momentum">

With $p$ and $e$ defined by $r_p = p/(1 + e)$, $r_a = p/(1 - e)$ (here $p = 50/3$, $e = 2/3$), the two conditions give, with $GM = 1$,

<Formula label="Problem 3(c): energy and angular momentum of a bound orbit" tex="\hat L^2 = \frac{p^2}{p - 3 - e^2}, \qquad \hat E^2 = \frac{(p - 2)^2 - 4e^2}{p\left(p - 3 - e^2\right)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

so $\hat L = 4.583$ and $\hat E = 0.9839$. (A direct check: $V_\text{eff}(10) = V_\text{eff}(50) = 0.96807 = \hat E^2$.)

</Step>

<Step title="The advance">

With $u = 1/r = (1 + e\cos\chi)/p$ the orbit equation becomes $d\phi/d\chi = \sqrt{p/(p - 6 - 2e\cos\chi)}$, smooth at the turning points ($\chi = 0$ and $\pi$). One radial cycle is $\chi$ from $0$ to $2\pi$:

<Formula label="Problem 3(c): the angle per radial cycle" tex="\Delta\phi = \int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{p}{p - 6 - 2e\cos\chi}}\;d\chi = 451.3^\circ, \qquad \Delta\phi - 360^\circ = 91.3^\circ" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

Our numerical run (started at $r = 10$ with $dr/d\tau = 0$) gives the same advance, $91.33°$ per cycle, cycle after cycle, with turning points at 10 and 50 to eight digits. One radial cycle takes $1106\,GM$ of proper time.

</Step>

<Step title="As far apart as possible">

The outermost point can be pushed as far out as one likes by taking $\hat E$ closer to 1. The innermost point is limited: for a bound orbit ($\hat E < 1$) the peak of $V_\text{eff}$ must stay above $\hat E^2$, and the peak with height 1 sits at $r = 4GM$ ($\hat L = 4GM$). So the innermost radius can approach $4GM$, but not reach it, while the outermost tends to infinity.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For a wide orbit the weak-field advance is $6\pi GM/p$ per cycle, here $64.8°$. Our orbit comes in to $10\,GM$, where the weak-field formula underestimates: the exact $91.3°$ is larger, as expected for a strong field.

</Step>

</Solution>

A stable circular orbit. Our example: $r = 10\,GM$. Give $\hat L$ (in units of $GM$), $\hat E$, and the orbital period seen from far away, in milliseconds, for $M = 10$ solar masses.

<Hint>

Use the circular-orbit formulas of the lesson for Lecture 23, and the time unit of part (a). The period far away is $2\pi/\Omega$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3d-en" label="$\hat E$:" value="0.9562" tolerance="0.1%" calc="p3d-en" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On a circular orbit $V_\text{eff}' = 0$ and $\hat E^2 = V_\text{eff}$. With these starting values the integrator should keep $r$ fixed.

<Step title="The numbers">

$r = 10\,GM$ is outside the innermost stable orbit at $6\,GM$, so the orbit is stable. Its frequency obeys Kepler's law $\Omega^2 = GM/r^3$ exactly, in Schwarzschild time.

<Part label="e" points={7}>

An unstable circular orbit. How long can your integrator hold it at fixed $r$? If it holds it for a long time, perturb the energy or angular momentum slightly and watch what happens. Can you drive it into both outcomes of the instability, a plunge into the hole and an escape to infinity? Our example: $r = 3.5\,GM$. Give $\hat E$, and the time in which a small displacement grows by a factor $e$, in units of $GM$ of proper time. The choice box is our addition.

<Hint>

Near the circular orbit $d^2r/d\tau^2 \approx -\frac12 V_\text{eff}''(r)\,\delta r$. With $V_\text{eff}'' < 0$ the displacement grows exponentially.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Escape to infinity needs $\hat E \ge 1$. Compare $\hat E$ of circular orbits with 1.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3e-en" label="$\hat E$:" value="1.134" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p3e-en" />

<Answer id="p3e-tau" label="Growth time (factor $e$):" value="2.93" unit="GM" tolerance="1%" calc="p3e-tau" />

<Answer id="p3e-both" label="Both a plunge and an escape are possible from the unstable circular orbits with:" choices={["3GM < r ≤ 4GM, the ones with Ê ≥ 1", "4GM < r < 6GM, where Ê < 1", "every unstable radius, 3GM < r < 6GM", "no radius: an unstable orbit always plunges"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At the peak of $V_\text{eff}$ the linearized equation has an exponentially growing solution. Which way the body goes depends on the sign of the push; whether it can reach infinity depends on whether $\hat E \ge 1$.

<Step title="Energy and angular momentum">

At $r = 3.5$: $\hat L^2 = r^2/(r - 3) = 24.5$, $\hat L = 4.950$, and $\hat E = (1 - 2/3.5)/\sqrt{1 - 3/3.5} = 1.134$. Since $\hat E > 1$, a body that leaves outward has enough energy to reach infinity.

</Step>

<Step title="The growth time">

$V_\text{eff}'' = -\frac{4}{r^3} + \frac{6\hat L^2}{r^4} - \frac{24\hat L^2}{r^5} = -0.2332$ at $r = 3.5$, so $\delta\ddot r = \lambda^2\delta r$ with

<Formula label="Problem 3(e): the growth rate at the unstable orbit" tex="\lambda = \sqrt{-\tfrac{1}{2}V_\text{eff}''} = 0.3415, \qquad \frac{1}{\lambda} = 2.93\,GM" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(e), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

A displacement $\epsilon$ from rest grows as $\epsilon\cosh\lambda\tau$, so it reaches a size $\Delta$ after about $\ln(2\Delta/\epsilon)/\lambda$. Rounding errors of $10^{-16}$ in double precision would grow to order one in about $37$ e-foldings, roughly $110\,GM$ of proper time.

</Step>

<Step title="Our numerical runs">

Started exactly at $r = 3.5$ with $dr/d\tau = 0$, our integrator held the orbit for the whole run ($\tau = 400$): the computed acceleration at the starting point was zero, so there was nothing to grow. With the start moved by $\pm 10^{-6}$ (which changes $\hat E$ very slightly):

| start | outcome | $\tau$ at which $|r - 3.5|$ passes 0.1 |
|---|---|---|
| $3.5 + 10^{-6}$ | escapes (reaches $r = 100$) | 35.9 |
| $3.5 - 10^{-6}$ | plunges (crosses $r = 2.05$) | 35.6 |

The estimate $\ln(2\times 0.1/10^{-6})/\lambda = 35.7$ agrees.

</Step>

<Step title="Which orbits allow both outcomes">

Unstable circular orbits lie between $3GM$ and $6GM$. Their $\hat E$ is 1 at $r = 4GM$, above 1 inside and below 1 outside. So from $3GM < r \le 4GM$ a push outward leads to escape (at $r = 4GM$ exactly, only just) and a push inward to a plunge. From $4GM < r < 6GM$ a push inward still leads to a plunge, but a push outward cannot lead to escape: the body makes a bound excursion and comes back towards the peak (whether it then falls in depends on whether the push left its $\hat E^2$ above or below the peak).

  caption="The effective potential (1 − 2/r)(1 + L̂²/r²) with L̂² = 24.5 and GM = 1, computed from the formula. <Step title="Check the result">

</Step>
