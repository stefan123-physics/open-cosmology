The previous two lectures produced the Einstein field equation. The rest of the course solves it. This lecture begins with the simplest case, spacetimes that are nearly flat, and ends by deriving the weak-field metric that the Newtonian limit had already suggested. The page uses signature $(-, +, +, +)$, $c = 1$, and Hughes's notation: $\Box = \eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}$ is the flat-spacetime wave operator.

## Why the field equation is hard

It helps to read $G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}$ as a differential equation for the metric, given a source, just as Poisson's equation gives the potential of a mass distribution (transcript pp. 1–2). The left side is a complicated **nonlinear** differential operator on the metric: Christoffel symbols from first derivatives, the Riemann tensor from those and their derivatives, then traces with the metric. The right side can be messy too: for a perfect fluid it contains the density, pressure, four-velocity and the metric itself, and the four-velocity must be normalised with the metric as well (transcript p. 2). In general that is ten coupled nonlinear partial differential equations.

The rest of the course follows three routes (transcript pp. 2–3):

1. **Weak gravity**, spacetimes close to flat, starting today.
2. **Symmetric solutions**, static, stationary or spherical, which cut the ten equations down to one, two or three, and become very powerful when combined with perturbation theory (Hughes's own research uses this a great deal).
3. **Numerical solution** of the full equations, the subject of entire careers, introduced only briefly in the last lecture.

## Nearly Lorentz coordinates

Linearized general relativity considers spacetimes that are nearly flat: coordinates exist in which (transcript p. 3)

<Formula label="A nearly flat metric" tex="g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}, \qquad |h_{\mu\nu}| \ll 1" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3" />

These are **nearly Lorentz coordinates**, as close to a global inertial frame as the spacetime allows. In practice they are Cartesian-like on spatial slices: in spherical-like coordinates some metric components grow with radius and the condition fails, even far from the source. Every term of order $h^2$ or higher is dropped, hence *linearized* (transcript p. 3).

**Lorentz transformations.** In curved spacetime Lorentz transformations are not special, but this spacetime is nearly flat, so Hughes tries one anyway (transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="A Lorentz transformation of the nearly flat metric" tex="g_{\bar\mu\bar\nu} = \Lambda^{\mu}{}_{\bar\mu}\Lambda^{\nu}{}_{\bar\nu}\,(\eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}) = \eta_{\bar\mu\bar\nu} + h_{\bar\mu\bar\nu}, \qquad h_{\bar\mu\bar\nu} = \Lambda^{\mu}{}_{\bar\mu}\Lambda^{\nu}{}_{\bar\nu}\,h_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 4" />

The background is unchanged and the perturbation transforms like any tensor in flat spacetime. This is not flat spacetime: curvature is non-zero and initially parallel geodesics do not stay parallel. But it is a **useful fiction** to treat $h_{\mu\nu}$ as an ordinary tensor field living in the spacetime of special relativity (transcript pp. 4–5).

**Raising and lowering.** Raising an index of $h$ with the full metric gives $\eta$ plus corrections of order $h$, and those corrections give $h^2$, which is dropped. So for quantities built from $h$, indices are raised and lowered with $\eta$ (transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="Indices move with the flat metric" tex="h^{\alpha\beta} = \eta^{\alpha\mu}\eta^{\beta\nu}h_{\mu\nu}, \qquad h^{\alpha}{}_{\gamma} = \eta^{\alpha\mu}h_{\mu\gamma}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 5" />

**The inverse metric** (justifying a step of Lecture 12). Write $g^{\alpha\beta} = \eta^{\alpha\beta} + m^{\alpha\beta}$ with $m$ of order $h$ but unknown, and require $g^{\alpha\beta}g_{\beta\gamma} = \delta^\alpha{}_\gamma$. Multiplying out gives $\delta^\alpha{}_\gamma + m^\alpha{}_\gamma + h^\alpha{}_\gamma + O(h^2)$, so $m^{\alpha}{}_{\gamma} = -h^{\alpha}{}_{\gamma}$ (transcript pp. 5–6):

<Formula label="The inverse metric to first order" tex="g^{\alpha\beta} = \eta^{\alpha\beta} - h^{\alpha\beta} + O(h^2)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 6" />

It is the matrix version of $1/(1 + \epsilon) \approx 1 - \epsilon$. Hughes remarks that a wrong sign here is one of the commonest mistakes his graduate students make (transcript p. 6).

## Gauge transformations

A second kind of coordinate change matters even more: a small shift of the coordinates that varies from place to place (transcript pp. 6–7),

<Formula label="An infinitesimal coordinate shift" tex="x'^{\alpha} = x^{\alpha} + \xi^{\alpha}(x^{\beta}), \qquad \frac{\partial x'^{\alpha}}{\partial x^{\beta}} = \delta^{\alpha}{}_{\beta} + \partial_{\beta}\xi^{\alpha}, \qquad \frac{\partial x^{\beta}}{\partial x'^{\alpha}} = \delta^{\beta}{}_{\alpha} - \partial_{\alpha}\xi^{\beta}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 6–7" />

with every $|\partial_{\beta}\xi^\alpha| \ll 1$. Hughes admits that writing $x'^{\alpha}$ abuses the index notation: this relates two specific coordinate systems, not tensors. The inverse Jacobian again has the minus sign of $1/(1 + \epsilon)$. Transforming the metric with it and dropping products of small quantities (transcript pp. 7–8):

<Formula label="The gauge transformation of the perturbation" tex="h_{\mu\nu}^{\text{new}} = h_{\mu\nu}^{\text{old}} - \partial_{\mu}\xi_\nu - \partial_{\nu}\xi_\mu" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 8" />

[Hughes reads the two derivative terms as "∂_μξ_ν minus ∂_νξ_μ"; both enter with a minus sign, symmetrized.] The new spacetime is still nearly flat; only its description has changed. This is strikingly like electromagnetism, where shifting the potential by a gradient, $A_\mu \to A_\mu + \partial_{\mu}\lambda$, leaves the field tensor unchanged. Here the curvature is unchanged, as shown below, so such a shift is called a **gauge transformation** (transcript p. 8). As in electromagnetism, the freedom is used to put the equations into the most convenient form.

<WorkedExample title="A perturbation that is pure gauge">
<Problem>
Start from flat spacetime, $h_{\mu\nu} = 0$, and shift coordinates with $\xi_\mu = (0,\ \tfrac12 k x^2,\ 0,\ 0)$ for a small constant $k$ (in units of inverse length). What perturbation appears, and is the spacetime curved?
</Problem>
<Step title="The new perturbation">
Only $\partial_x\xi_x = kx$ is non-zero, so $h^{\text{new}}_{xx} = -2kx$ and every other component vanishes. For $|kx| \ll 1$, say $k = 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^{-1}$ and $|x| < 10$ m, the largest component is $2 \times 10^{-3} \times 10 = 0.02$: small, as linearized theory needs.
</Step>
<Step title="The curvature">
The linearized Riemann tensor below contains only second derivatives of $h$, and every second derivative of $-2kx$ is zero. The curvature vanishes: this "perturbation" is flat spacetime in stretched coordinates, a pure gauge.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The linearized curvature

Running the metric through the machinery of curvature is "straightforward but tedious", so Hughes gives the results (transcript pp. 8–9). To first order in $h$, the Riemann tensor is

<Formula label="Linearized Riemann tensor" tex="R_{\alpha\mu\beta\nu} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\beta} h_{\alpha\nu} + \partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\nu} h_{\mu\beta} - \partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta} h_{\mu\nu} - \partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu} h_{\alpha\beta}\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 9" />

Under a gauge transformation each of the four terms produces two terms with three derivatives of $\xi$; the eight terms cancel in pairs, so the Riemann tensor is gauge invariant (transcript pp. 9–10). Contracting with $\eta$ gives the Ricci tensor and scalar, with $h = \eta^{\mu\nu}h_{\mu\nu}$ the trace (transcript p. 10):

<Formula label="Linearized Ricci tensor and scalar" tex="R_{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\mu} h^{\alpha}{}_{\nu} + \partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\nu} h^{\alpha}{}_{\mu} - \partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu} h - \Box h_{\mu\nu}\right), \qquad R = \partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\mu} h^{\alpha\mu} - \Box h" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 10" />

and the Einstein tensor $R_{\mu\nu} - \frac12\eta_{\mu\nu}R$, six terms with an overall factor $\frac12$ (transcript p. 10):

<Formula label="Linearized Einstein tensor" tex="G_{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\mu} h^{\alpha}{}_{\nu} + \partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\nu} h^{\alpha}{}_{\mu} - \partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu} h - \Box h_{\mu\nu} - \eta_{\mu\nu}\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta} h^{\alpha\beta} + \eta_{\mu\nu}\Box h\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 10" />

Even linearized, it is a mess, which shows how bad the full nonlinear version is.

## The trace-reversed perturbation

A reorganisation cleans it up (transcript pp. 10–11). Define

<Formula label="Trace-reversed perturbation" tex="\bar h_{\mu\nu} = h_{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{2}\eta_{\mu\nu}h, \qquad \bar h = \eta^{\mu\nu}\bar h_{\mu\nu} = h - \frac{1}{2}\cdot 4\,h = -h" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 10–11" />

It carries the same information as $h_{\mu\nu}$ but with the trace reversed, and since the Einstein tensor is itself the trace-reversed Ricci tensor, substituting $h_{\mu\nu} = \bar h_{\mu\nu} - \frac12\eta_{\mu\nu}\bar h$ removes every term containing the trace (transcript p. 11):

<Formula label="Einstein tensor in terms of the trace-reversed perturbation" tex="G_{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\mu}\bar h^{\alpha}{}_{\nu} + \partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\nu}\bar h^{\alpha}{}_{\mu} - \eta_{\mu\nu}\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}\bar h^{\alpha\beta} - \Box\bar h_{\mu\nu}\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 11" />

## Lorenz gauge and the wave equation

Three of the four terms are divergences of $\bar h$ (transcript pp. 11–12). As in electromagnetism, where one can choose the divergence of the potential to vanish, can a gauge be found where $\partial^{\mu}\bar h_{\mu\nu} = 0$? That is four conditions, one for each $\nu$, and the gauge generators $\xi_\nu$ are four free functions. Under a gauge transformation the trace-reversed perturbation picks up one extra term (transcript p. 12):

<Formula label="Gauge transformation of the trace-reversed perturbation" tex="\bar h_{\mu\nu}^{\text{new}} = \bar h_{\mu\nu}^{\text{old}} - \partial_{\mu}\xi_\nu - \partial_{\nu}\xi_\mu + \eta_{\mu\nu}\partial_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 12" />

Taking the divergence, the second term gives $-\Box\xi_\nu$, and the third and fourth give $-\partial_{\nu}(\partial_{\alpha}\xi^\alpha)$ and $+\partial_{\nu}(\partial_{\alpha}\xi^\alpha)$, which cancel. [Hughes first writes the last sign wrong on the board and corrects it.] So

<Formula label="Reaching the Lorenz gauge" tex="\partial^{\mu}\bar h_{\mu\nu}^{\text{new}} = \partial^{\mu}\bar h_{\mu\nu}^{\text{old}} - \Box\xi_\nu = 0 \quad\text{if}\quad \Box\xi_\nu = \partial^{\mu}\bar h_{\mu\nu}^{\text{old}}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 12" />

a simple wave equation for $\xi_\nu$, whose solutions always exist; a spacetime too pathological to allow it would violate the weak-field assumption anyway (transcript pp. 12–13). By analogy with electromagnetism, $\partial^{\mu}\bar h_{\mu\nu} = 0$ is called the **Lorenz gauge** (Hughes calls it Lorentz gauge, the common name; the condition is due to Ludvig Lorenz). In it, $G_{\mu\nu} = -\frac12\Box\bar h_{\mu\nu}$, and the field equation becomes (transcript p. 13)

<Formula label="The linearized field equation in Lorenz gauge" tex="\Box\bar h_{\mu\nu} = -16\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 13" />

It has the same structure as the electromagnetic potential in Lorenz gauge, $\Box A^\mu = -4\pi J^\mu$ in Gaussian units (Hughes is unsure of the sign at the board; with $\Box = -\partial_t^2 + \nabla^2$ it is minus): an extra index, a different coefficient, and the same solution by a retarded Green's function, which the next lecture develops (transcript p. 13).

## The static limit: the weak-field metric

Hughes ends with the simplest source, a static, non-relativistic perfect fluid (transcript pp. 13–14). Static means all time derivatives vanish, for the source and so for the field. Non-relativistic means $\rho \gg P$, so $T_{\mu\nu} \approx \rho\,u_\mu u_\nu$ and only $T_{00} \approx \rho$ matters. The wave operator becomes the Laplacian, and the $00$ equation

<Formula label="The static 00 equation" tex="\nabla^2\bar h_{00} = -16\pi G\rho" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 14" />

is Poisson's equation, $\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho$, times $-4$. So $\bar h_{00} = -4\Phi$, and to this order every other component of $\bar h_{\mu\nu}$ vanishes. Reversing the trace again recovers $h_{\mu\nu} = \bar h_{\mu\nu} - \frac12\eta_{\mu\nu}\bar h$, with $\bar h = \eta^{00}\bar h_{00} = 4\Phi$ (transcript p. 14):

<Formula label="Back to the metric perturbation" tex="h_{00} = -4\Phi - \frac{1}{2}(-1)(4\Phi) = -2\Phi, \qquad h_{xx} = h_{yy} = h_{zz} = 0 - \frac{1}{2}(1)(4\Phi) = -2\Phi" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 14" />

<Formula label="The weak-field (Newtonian-limit) metric" tex="ds^2 = -(1 + 2\Phi)\,dt^2 + (1 - 2\Phi)\left(dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 14" />

The $g_{00}$ found in Lecture 12 from the equation of motion is now derived from the field equation, and the spatial part comes with it. Hughes notes that until about twenty years before the lecture, almost every laboratory and astronomical test of general relativity rested on this metric: gravitational lensing, post-Newtonian corrections in the solar system, and most binary systems observed in our galaxy and others (transcript p. 14). Only far more extreme systems need more than linear order.

<WorkedExample title="How big is h at the surface of the Sun, and why light sees twice as much">
<Problem>
Evaluate $h_{00} = -2\Phi$ at the Sun's surface ($GM_\odot/c^2 = 1476.6$ m, $R_\odot = 6.957\times10^8$ m). Then use the full weak-field metric to explain why light is bent twice as much as a calculation with $h_{00}$ alone suggests.
</Problem>
<Step title="Size of the perturbation">
$\Phi = -GM_\odot/(R_\odot c^2) = -1476.6/6.957\times10^8 \approx -2.12\times10^{-6}$, so $h_{00} = h_{xx} = 4.25\times10^{-6}$. Linear theory is excellent here.
</Step>
<Step title="Slow bodies versus light">
A slow body has $dx/dt \ll 1$, so its geodesic feels essentially only $h_{00}$: that is how Lecture 12 got Newton's law. For light $ds^2 = 0$, so $dx/dt = \sqrt{(1 + 2\Phi)/(1 - 2\Phi)} \approx 1 + 2\Phi$: the time and space parts contribute equally to slowing it near the Sun.
</Step>
<Step title="The deflection">
Each part contributes $2GM/(bc^2)$, giving $4GM_\odot/(bc^2) = 4 \times 1476.6/6.957\times10^8 \approx 8.49\times10^{-6}$ rad $\approx 1.75$ arcseconds for a grazing ray, the value the 1919 eclipse expeditions set out to test (their rough results favoured it; later radio measurements confirm it to better than 0.1%). With $h_{00}$ alone it would be half, about 0.88 arcseconds.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Rotating sources and gravitomagnetism

A problem set extends the calculation to a rotating body (transcript p. 15). In general relativity every flux of energy and momentum gravitates, through $T_{\mu\nu}$, so the mass currents of a rotating body add a new piece to the metric. The static metric above behaves like Newtonian gravity, which resembles the Coulomb field, so it is often called **gravitoelectric**. The rotation term is qualitatively different: it looks like a magnetic field, and moving bodies respond to it much as charges respond to the magnetic part of the Lorentz force, so it is called **gravitomagnetic** (transcript p. 15).

<Callout kind="cosmology">
The weak-field metric $-(1 + 2\Phi)dt^2 + (1 - 2\Phi)d\vec x^2$ is the starting point of cosmological perturbation theory. Put the factor $a(t)^2$ of an expanding universe in front of the spatial part and it describes the small potential wells in which galaxies and clusters grow; light crossing them is lensed, and the cosmic microwave background photons climbing out of them are redshifted. 8.902 follows the growth of those perturbations.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The field equation is ten coupled nonlinear equations for the metric; the course solves it by weak-field expansion, by symmetry, and numerically.
- In nearly Lorentz coordinates $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}$, $|h| \ll 1$, terms of order $h^2$ dropped. $h$ transforms like a special-relativity tensor under Lorentz transformations, indices move with $\eta$, and $g^{\mu\nu} = \eta^{\mu\nu} - h^{\mu\nu}$.
- A shift $x'^{\alpha} = x^\alpha + \xi^\alpha$ changes $h_{\mu\nu}$ by $-\partial_{\mu}\xi_\nu - \partial_{\nu}\xi_\mu$ and leaves the linearized Riemann tensor unchanged: a gauge transformation.
- With $\bar h_{\mu\nu} = h_{\mu\nu} - \frac12\eta_{\mu\nu}h$ and the Lorenz gauge $\partial^{\mu}\bar h_{\mu\nu} = 0$, the field equation is $\Box\bar h_{\mu\nu} = -16\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}$, a wave equation like electromagnetism's.
- A static, slow source gives $\bar h_{00} = -4\Phi$, hence $ds^2 = -(1 + 2\Phi)dt^2 + (1 - 2\Phi)(dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2)$; its spatial part doubles the bending of light.
- Mass currents add gravitomagnetic terms, the analogue of a magnetic field.
