The last lecture showed that, of the ten components of the metric perturbation, only the two in the transverse-traceless part $h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}$ are radiative in every gauge (transcript pp. 1–2). This lecture, delivered (Hughes notes) in his LIGO hat, has two goals: to understand $h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}$ in terms of what can be measured, and to compute it from a source (transcript p. 3). Signature $(-, +, +, +)$, $c = 1$ unless written out; spatial indices are written down and summed when repeated.

## A plane wave

Hughes hands himself a spacetime in which every gauge-invariant field except $h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}$ vanishes, and $h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}$ depends only on $t - z$, so it travels along $z$ at the speed of light (transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="A plane gravitational wave travelling along z" tex="h^{\text{TT}}_{ij} = \begin{pmatrix} h_+(t - z) & h_\times(t - z) & 0 \\ h_\times(t - z) & -h_+(t - z) & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3" />

with $h_+ = h^{\text{TT}}_{xx}$ and $h_\times = h^{\text{TT}}_{xy}$. Symmetry makes $h_{xy} = h_{yx}$; tracelessness makes $h_{yy} = -h_{xx}$, leaving two functions, the two polarizations. The $z$ components vanish because the wave must be transverse to its direction of travel, shown properly below. Any function of $t - z$ satisfies the wave equation (transcript pp. 3–4).

## A single body does not move (in these coordinates)

The obvious first calculation: a freely falling body at rest, $u^\mu = (1, 0, 0, 0)$, in empty flat spacetime, and at $t = 0$ the wave arrives (transcript pp. 4–5). The geodesic equation gives the initial acceleration

<Formula label="Initial acceleration of a body at rest" tex="\frac{du^{\alpha}}{d\tau} = -\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{00} = -\frac{1}{2}\eta^{\alpha\beta}\left(\partial_0 h_{\beta 0} + \partial_0 h_{0\beta} - \partial_{\beta} h_{00}\right) = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 5" />

since the wave has no time-time or time-space parts. The body starts on a geodesic and stays at fixed coordinates. Do gravitational waves do nothing? No: the calculation was, in Hughes's word, "dumb", or at least uninformative (transcript pp. 5–6). The geodesic equation only says the body is in free fall, which it is; and these coordinates move with freely falling bodies. Questions phrased in coordinates need coordinates that are understood, and these were not; better to ask about something coordinate independent. Since a freely falling frame removes gravity at a point, the effect must be sought between two separated bodies (transcript p. 6).

## Timing light between two bodies

Put body A at the origin and body B at $x = \epsilon$ along the $x$ axis, both freely falling, and bounce light between them (transcript pp. 6–7). Light has no proper time, so describe its path with an affine parameter $\lambda$; along $x$, the null condition $p^\mu p_\mu = 0$ gives

<Formula label="Light along x in the wave" tex="0 = -\left(\frac{dt}{d\lambda}\right)^2 + \left(1 + h^{\text{TT}}_{xx}\right)\left(\frac{dx}{d\lambda}\right)^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad dt = \sqrt{1 + h^{\text{TT}}_{xx}}\;dx \approx \left(1 + \frac{1}{2}h^{\text{TT}}_{xx}\right)dx" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 7" />

The gauge-invariant $h^{\text{TT}}_{xx}$ appears directly. The round-trip time measured by A's clock follows by integrating (transcript pp. 7–8):

<Formula label="Round-trip light travel time, for a slowly changing wave" tex="T_{\text{rt}} \approx 2\epsilon\left(1 + \frac{1}{2}h^{\text{TT}}_{xx}\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 7–8" />

In general one integrates $dt$ from A to B and back, $2\epsilon$ plus half the integral of $h^{\text{TT}}_{xx}$ along each leg. The time argument of $h$ changes as the light moves, so the two legs are not simply equal (a problem set works this out); when the wave changes slowly compared with the travel time they are, and the result is the formula above. Without a wave, pulses sent out every millisecond return every millisecond; a wave makes the arrival times vary (transcript p. 8). This is how LIGO works.

## Geodesic deviation: the tidal stretch and squeeze

The second view uses geodesic deviation (transcript pp. 8–9). Two nearby bodies start with the same four-velocity; any change from the wave is of order $h$. To linear order, proper time and coordinate time differ by order $h$, the Riemann tensor is itself of order $h$, and $u^\mu = (1, 0, 0, 0)$ picks out its $0\,0$ slots. The time component is trivial by antisymmetry, leaving (transcript p. 9)

<Formula label="Geodesic deviation, linearized" tex="\frac{\partial^2\xi^{j}}{\partial t^2} = -R^{j}{}_{0k0}\,\xi^{k}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 9" />

Computing the linearized Riemann tensor for the wave (only derivatives of the connection matter at this order; Hughes promises Mathematica notebooks to check such calculations), with indices raised and lowered by $\eta$ (transcript pp. 9–10):

<Formula label="Riemann components of the plane wave" tex="R_{x0x0} = -\frac{1}{2}\partial_t^2 h^{\text{TT}}_{xx}, \qquad R_{y0y0} = -\frac{1}{2}\partial_t^2 h^{\text{TT}}_{yy} = \frac{1}{2}\partial_t^2 h^{\text{TT}}_{xx}, \qquad R_{x0y0} = -\frac{1}{2}\partial_t^2 h^{\text{TT}}_{xy}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 10" />

All other components vanish or follow by symmetry. Substituting (transcript pp. 10–11):

<Formula label="The response of separated test masses" tex="\frac{\partial^2\xi^{x}}{\partial t^2} = \frac{1}{2}\partial_t^2 h^{\text{TT}}_{xx}\,\xi^{x} + \frac{1}{2}\partial_t^2 h^{\text{TT}}_{xy}\,\xi^{y}, \qquad \frac{\partial^2\xi^{y}}{\partial t^2} = \frac{1}{2}\partial_t^2 h^{\text{TT}}_{xy}\,\xi^{x} - \frac{1}{2}\partial_t^2 h^{\text{TT}}_{xx}\,\xi^{y}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 11" />

Write the separation as a constant part plus a small change of order $h$ (transcript p. 11). Integrating twice in time, a mass that starts at separation $\xi^{x}_0$, $\xi^{y}_0$ from the central one is displaced to

<Formula label="Displacement of a test mass" tex="\xi^{x} = \xi^{x}_0 + \frac{1}{2}\left(h_+\,\xi^{x}_0 + h_\times\,\xi^{y}_0\right), \qquad \xi^{y} = \xi^{y}_0 + \frac{1}{2}\left(h_\times\,\xi^{x}_0 - h_+\,\xi^{y}_0\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 11 (the page's integration of the equations above)" />

Now imagine a ring of test masses in the $xy$-plane (transcript pp. 11–12). With $h_+$ oscillating and $h_\times = 0$, the ring stretches along $x$ while it squeezes along $y$; a quarter cycle later it is a circle again, and a quarter cycle after that it is stretched along $y$ and squeezed along $x$. With $h_\times$ alone, the same happens along the diagonals at 45°. These are the two **polarizations**, named for the shape of the lines along which they stretch and squeeze: **plus** and **cross**. Polarizations are defined only relative to a chosen pair of axes.

**LIGO** looks for exactly this (transcript pp. 12–13). A full ring is impractical, so it samples it along two perpendicular arms, best aligned with the plus pattern, and times light bouncing between mirrors, as in the calculation above. The clock is the laser itself: interfering the light that has travelled down an arm and back with the incoming beam reveals tiny shifts in arrival time, with the precision needed to see $h$ of order $10^{-21}$.

## Computing the wave from a source

Given a source, how does one compute $h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}$ (transcript p. 13)? Lorenz gauge makes everything look radiative, but only $h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}$ is radiation in all gauges. So the recipe is: (a) find the metric perturbation in any convenient gauge by any convenient method; (b) extract its TT part. Start from the exact Lorenz-gauge solution of Lecture 15 (transcript pp. 13–14):

<Formula label="Lorenz-gauge solution" tex="\bar h_{\mu\nu}(t, \vec x) = 4G\int d^3x'\;\frac{T_{\mu\nu}\left(t - |\vec x - \vec x'|,\ \vec x'\right)}{|\vec x - \vec x'|}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 14" />

**Far away.** For LIGO's sources the distance is hundreds of millions to billions of light years, the source tens to hundreds of kilometres across. So replace $|\vec x - \vec x'|$ by $r$, the same for every point of the source (transcript pp. 14–15). This keeps the leading multipole; expanding $1/|\vec x - \vec x'|$ in Legendre polynomials gives the higher ones, which matter in research (much of Hughes's own) but not here. Only the spatial components will be needed: strictly it is $h_{ij}$, not $\bar h_{ij}$, that matters, which can be justified only once the calculation is finished (transcript p. 15). [Hughes first writes the trace-reversed perturbation with a tilde and corrects it to a bar.]

<Formula label="Far-field spatial components" tex="\bar h_{ij}(t, \vec x) \approx \frac{4G}{r}\int d^3x'\;T_{ij}(t - r,\ \vec x')" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 15" />

**The tensor virial theorem** (an "Easter egg" from Problem Set 2) turns the stresses into the mass distribution, using $\partial_{\mu} T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ (transcript p. 15):

<Formula label="Tensor virial theorem" tex="\int d^3x\;T^{ij} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\int d^3x\;T^{00}x^{i}x^{j}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 15–16" />

so, with the **quadrupole moment** of the source,

<Formula label="The wave in terms of the quadrupole moment" tex="\bar h_{ij} = \frac{2G}{r}\,\ddot I_{ij}(t - r), \qquad I_{ij} = \int d^3x\;T^{00}x^{i}x^{j}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 15–16" />

(Hughes marks $I_{ij}$ with an asterisk: a problem set adjusts its definition by removing its trace.)

**Transverse.** Far from the source $T = 0$, so $\Box\bar h_{ij} = 0$: expand in plane waves, $\bar h_{jl} = A_{jl}\,e^{i(\omega t - \vec k\cdot\vec x)}$, one Fourier mode with constant amplitude (indices $j, l$ to keep $k$ for the wave vector and $i$ for $\sqrt{-1}$) (transcript p. 16). The transverse condition, zero divergence, becomes $k_jA_{jl} = 0$: the wave field is orthogonal to the direction of travel, as the fields of a light wave are (transcript pp. 16–17). With $n_j = k_j/|\vec k|$, the **projection tensor**

<Formula label="Projection onto the plane transverse to n" tex="P_{ij} = \delta_{ij} - n_i n_j, \qquad h^{\text{T}}_{ij} = P_{ik}P_{jl}\,\bar h_{kl}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 17" />

removes the components along $\hat n$; it is the metric of the plane that travels with the wave, perpendicular to $\hat n$ (an earlier problem-set Easter egg).

**Traceless.** For a two-index tensor in an $N$-dimensional space with metric $g_{ij}$, the trace-free part is (transcript p. 17)

<Formula label="Removing the trace" tex="a^{\text{TF}}_{ij} = a_{ij} - \frac{1}{N}\,a_{kl}\,g^{kl}\,g_{ij}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 17" />

Here $h^{\text{T}}_{ij}$ lives in the two-dimensional transverse plane with metric $P_{ij}$, so $N = 2$. Once the trace is removed the bar no longer matters (the differences between the notions of trace involved are of second order) (transcript pp. 17–18). The result is the **quadrupole formula** for the wave field (transcript p. 18):

<Formula label="The quadrupole formula" tex="h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}(t, \vec x) = \frac{2G}{r}\left(P_{ik}P_{jl} - \frac{1}{2}P_{ij}P_{kl}\right)\ddot I_{kl}(t - r)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 18" />

Compute the quadrupole moment, take two time derivatives, project transverse and traceless with respect to the direction from source to observer, multiply by $2G$ and divide by the distance (transcript p. 18). With $c$ written out the prefactor is $2G/(c^4 r)$, with the time argument $t - r/c$. [At the board Hughes is unsure whether the power is $c^4$ or $c^2$; dimensional analysis gives $c^4$, since $\ddot I$ is a mass times a velocity squared.] The factor $G/c^4$ makes the waves tiny and very hard to detect, yet, as the next lecture shows, they carry a great deal of energy (transcript p. 19).

<WorkedExample title="The wave from a binary black hole, face-on">
<Problem>
Two black holes of $m = 30\,M_\odot$ each move on a circular orbit of separation $a = 1000$ km, in the $xy$-plane, seen from $r = 400$ Mpc along the $z$ axis. Treat them as point masses at $\pm\frac{a}{2}(\cos\Omega t, \sin\Omega t, 0)$, with $\Omega^2 = G(2m)/a^3$ from Kepler's law. Find $h_+$, its frequency and its amplitude. ($GM_\odot = 1.327\times10^{20}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$.)
</Problem>
<Step title="Quadrupole moment">
$I_{xx} = 2m(\frac{a}{2})^2\cos^2\Omega t = \frac{ma^2}{4}(1 + \cos 2\Omega t)$ and $I_{yy} = \frac{ma^2}{4}(1 - \cos 2\Omega t)$, so $\ddot I_{xx} = -ma^2\Omega^2\cos 2\Omega t = -\ddot I_{yy}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Project">
For $\hat n = \hat z$, $P_{ij}$ is the identity on the $xy$-plane, and the TT projection of the $xx$ component is $\frac12(\ddot I_{xx} - \ddot I_{yy})$. So $h_+ = -\frac{2Gma^2\Omega^2}{rc^4}\cos 2\Omega t$: the wave oscillates at **twice** the orbital frequency.
</Step>
<Step title="Numbers">
$\Omega^2 = 2 \times 30 \times 1.327\times10^{20}/(10^6)^3 \approx 7963\ \text{s}^{-2}$, $\Omega \approx 89.2$ rad/s, so the wave frequency is $2\Omega/2\pi \approx 28.4$ Hz, in LIGO's band. With $r = 400 \times 3.086\times10^{22}$ m, the amplitude is $2Gma^2\Omega^2/(rc^4) \approx 6.4\times10^{-22}$.
</Step>
<Step title="What LIGO sees">
A 4 km arm along $x$ changes by $\frac12 h_+L \approx 0.5 \times 6.4\times10^{-22} \times 4000 \approx 1.3\times10^{-18}$ m.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology">
The quadrupole formula and the TT projection are the starting point for modelling the signals measured since 2015 (real waveform templates add higher multipoles and relativistic corrections). In cosmology, a merging binary is a standard siren: its waveform gives the luminosity distance directly, independent of the distance ladder, and combined with the redshift of its host galaxy it measures the Hubble constant (first done for the neutron-star merger GW170817 in 2017, a later development). Inflation may also have left a background of primordial gravitational waves, whose stretch-and-squeeze would imprint a characteristic pattern on the polarization of the cosmic microwave background.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A plane wave along $z$ has $h^{\text{TT}}_{xx} = -h^{\text{TT}}_{yy} = h_+(t - z)$ and $h^{\text{TT}}_{xy} = h_\times(t - z)$: two polarizations.
- A single freely falling body stays at fixed TT coordinates; the physics is in the separation of two bodies.
- Light between bodies along $x$ takes $dt \approx (1 + \frac12 h^{\text{TT}}_{xx})dx$, so round-trip times vary as the wave passes.
- Geodesic deviation gives $\partial_t^2\xi^j = \frac12\partial_t^2 h^{\text{TT}}_{jk}\,\xi^k$: a ring of test masses is stretched and squeezed in a plus or cross pattern. LIGO measures this by laser interferometry.
- Recipe: solve in Lorenz gauge, approximate far away, use the tensor virial theorem, and project with $P_{ij} = \delta_{ij} - n_in_j$: $h^{\text{TT}}_{ij} = \frac{2G}{rc^4}(P_{ik}P_{jl} - \frac12 P_{ij}P_{kl})\ddot I_{kl}(t - r/c)$, with $I_{kl}$ the mass quadrupole moment $\int\rho\,x^kx^l\,d^3x$.
