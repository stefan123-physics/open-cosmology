The previous lecture reduced linearized gravity to one wave equation, $\Box\bar h_{\mu\nu} = -16\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}$ in Lorenz gauge, and solved it for a static source (transcript p. 1). This lecture keeps the time derivatives. It first solves the equation exactly for any source, then asks a subtler question: since the answer depends on a choice of gauge, which parts of it are physical, and which parts are really radiation? The page uses signature $(-, +, +, +)$ and $c = 1$; $\Box = -\partial_t^2 + \nabla^2$.

## Green's functions in brief

The linearized equation has the form "linear differential operator acting on a field equals a source", and every such equation can be solved with a **Green's function** (transcript pp. 1–2). Hughes gives a synopsis and points to Arfken's *Mathematical Methods for Physicists* (Sections 16.5–16.6 of the third edition) for more. Write the equation as $\mathcal{D}f(t, \vec x) = s(t, \vec x)$. Replace the source by a single blip at one event, the **source point** $(t', \vec x')$, and call the field it produces at the **field point** $(t, \vec x)$ the Green's function (transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Definition of the Green's function" tex="\mathcal{D}\,G(t, \vec x;\, t', \vec x') = \delta(t - t')\,\delta^3(\vec x - \vec x')" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 2" />

Integrating the source against delta functions just returns the source, a tautology. The remarkable step is that integrating the source against the Green's function returns the solution (transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="The solution from the Green's function" tex="f(t, \vec x) = \int dt'\,d^3x'\;G(t, \vec x;\, t', \vec x')\,s(t', \vec x')" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3" />

The proof is one line: $\mathcal{D}$ acts only on the unprimed coordinates, so it passes through the integral and hits only $G$, which turns into delta functions, and the integral gives back $s$ (transcript p. 3).

For the flat-spacetime wave operator the answer is the **retarded** (radiative) Green's function, found in any advanced electrodynamics text (Hughes cites Section 6.6 of the second edition of Jackson; a Fourier transform turns the wave operator into algebra) (transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Retarded Green's function of the wave operator" tex="G(t, \vec x;\, t', \vec x') = -\frac{\delta\left(t - t' - |\vec x - \vec x'|\right)}{4\pi\,|\vec x - \vec x'|}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 4" />

The delta function says that what happens at $\vec x'$ is felt at $\vec x$ only after the light-travel time $|\vec x - \vec x'|$ (divided by $c$ in ordinary units): the Green's function builds in the time it takes information about the source to travel outward (transcript p. 4).

## The exact solution of linearized gravity

Apply this to $\Box\bar h_{\mu\nu} = -16\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}$ (transcript pp. 4–5). [The letter $G$ now does double duty, Newton's constant and the Green's function, as Hughes remarks.] The $-1/4\pi$ of the Green's function meets the $-16\pi$ of the source, and the delta function does the time integral:

<Formula label="Exact solution in Lorenz gauge" tex="\bar h_{\mu\nu}(t, \vec x) = 4G\int d^3x'\;\frac{T_{\mu\nu}\left(t - |\vec x - \vec x'|,\ \vec x'\right)}{|\vec x - \vec x'|}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 5" />

Integrate over the source, evaluate each piece at its retarded time, divide by its distance, and multiply by $4G$: an exact solution of linearized gravity. For a static source it reduces to the Newtonian $\bar h_{00} = -4\Phi$ of the last lecture.

<WorkedExample title="The Sun's field at the Earth, retarded">
<Problem>
Treat the Sun as a point mass, $T_{00} = M_\odot\,\delta^3(\vec x')$, at $r = 1.496\times10^{11}$ m. Find $\bar h_{00}$ at the Earth and how old the information is. ($GM_\odot/c^2 = 1476.6$ m.)
</Problem>
<Step title="The integral">
The delta function collapses the integral: $\bar h_{00} = 4GM_\odot/(rc^2) = 4 \times 1476.6/1.496\times10^{11} \approx 3.95\times10^{-8}$, which is $-4\Phi$, as in the static limit.
</Step>
<Step title="The retarded time">
The source is evaluated at $t - r/c$: $1.496\times10^{11}/2.998\times10^8 \approx 499$ s, about 8.3 minutes earlier. For a static Sun this makes no difference; for a moving source it is the whole story.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Is everything radiation? A warning from electromagnetism

The exact solution has a problem of interpretation (transcript p. 5). Every component of $\bar h_{\mu\nu}$ comes from a wave equation, depends on the source at retarded time and falls off with distance: the whole spacetime looks radiative. Lorenz gauge was excellent for getting solvable equations, but it may be hiding the physical character of the solution (transcript p. 5).

Hughes's illustration is an electromagnetic potential that, on an oral exam, one might guess describes a point charge plus some dipole radiation: a $q/r$ term, oscillating terms in $\sin(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t)$ falling as $1/r$, and a piece falling faster (transcript p. 6). Work out the field tensor, and all that is left is a Coulomb electric field with no magnetic field. It was a point charge in a "really dumb gauge", made by applying an arbitrary gauge generator to $q/r$. The page's own version of this:

<Formula label="A Coulomb field in a strange gauge" tex="A_\mu = \left(-\frac{q}{r},\ 0,\ 0,\ 0\right) + \partial_{\mu}\lambda, \qquad \lambda = a\,\frac{\sin(kr - \omega t)}{r}, \qquad F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_{\mu} A_\nu - \partial_{\nu} A_\mu\ \text{unchanged}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 6 (the page's own version)" />

The extra pieces oscillate and fall as $1/r$, like radiation, yet $\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}\lambda - \partial_{\nu}\partial_{\mu}\lambda = 0$ leaves every field untouched.

The moral (transcript p. 6): with gauge-dependent quantities, be careful before reading physics off them. There is, Hughes says, almost a conservation of pain: the gauge that makes the equations easiest to solve is often the one that makes the answer hardest to interpret.

## Splitting the metric into scalars, vectors and tensors

The rest of the lecture is, by Hughes's own description, somewhat advanced. It follows a paper he wrote with Éanna Flanagan about 16 years earlier, building on an idea Ed Bertschinger developed for perturbations of cosmological spacetimes (transcript pp. 6–8). The punch line first (transcript pp. 7–8): linearized spacetime has

- **four** gauge-invariant degrees of freedom that are **not radiative**: they obey Poisson-type equations, like the non-radiative electric and magnetic fields of electromagnetism;
- **two** that **are radiative**: they obey wave equations, and become the two polarizations of gravitational waves, as light has two polarizations;
- and the remaining **four** of the ten metric components are eaten by the gauge freedom of the four functions $\xi^\mu$.

Treat $h_{\mu\nu}$ as a field on flat spacetime (the useful fiction of the last lecture), choose a time and space split, and sort the components by how they behave under rotations of the spatial coordinates only (transcript pp. 8–9). $h_{tt}$ is a scalar, written $-2\phi$; the three $h_{ti}$ form a vector; the six $h_{ij}$ form a symmetric tensor. Any vector field splits into a divergence-free part plus a gradient, and the tensor splits similarly (spatial indices are all written down, and repeated ones summed) (transcript p. 9):

<Formula label="Scalar-vector-tensor split of the metric perturbation" tex="h_{tt} = -2\phi, \qquad h_{ti} = \beta_i + \partial_i\gamma, \qquad h_{ij} = h^{\text{TT}}_{ij} + \frac{1}{3}H\,\delta_{ij} + \frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_i\varepsilon_j + \partial_j\varepsilon_i\right) + \left(\partial_i\partial_j - \frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}\nabla^2\right)\lambda" cite="flanagan-hughes" at="Sec. 2.2; transcript p. 9" />

with $\partial_i\beta_i = 0$ and $\partial_i\varepsilon_i = 0$. The operator on $\lambda$ is traceless: its trace is $\nabla^2 - \frac13\cdot 3\nabla^2 = 0$. The last piece, $h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}$, is **transverse and traceless** (transcript pp. 9–10):

<Formula label="Transverse-traceless conditions" tex="h^{\text{TT}}_{ii} = 0, \qquad \partial_i h^{\text{TT}}_{ij} = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 10" />

Counting (transcript pp. 9–10): $\phi$, $\gamma$, $H$, $\lambda$ are one function each; $\beta_i$ and $\varepsilon_i$ have three components minus one divergence condition, two each; $h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}$ has six components minus one trace condition minus three divergence conditions, two. In all $1 + 1 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 2 = 10$, the ten components of $h_{\mu\nu}$, just rewritten.

## Which combinations are gauge invariant

Split the gauge generator the same way: $\xi_t = A$, $\xi_i = B_i + \partial_i C$ with $\partial_iB_i = 0$ (transcript p. 11). Under $h_{\mu\nu} \to h_{\mu\nu} - \partial_{\mu}\xi_\nu - \partial_{\nu}\xi_\mu$ the new functions change (transcript p. 11):

<Formula label="Gauge transformation of the new functions" tex="\phi \to \phi + \dot A, \quad \beta_i \to \beta_i - \dot B_i, \quad \gamma \to \gamma - A - \dot C, \quad H \to H - 2\nabla^2 C, \quad \lambda \to \lambda - 2C, \quad \varepsilon_i \to \varepsilon_i - 2B_i, \quad h^{\text{TT}}_{ij} \to h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}" cite="flanagan-hughes" at="Sec. 2.2; transcript p. 11" />

The transverse-traceless part is gauge invariant: whatever looks radiative in it is radiative in every representation (transcript p. 11). For the rest, there is no recipe; one stares at these rules until combinations appear that do not change (transcript p. 12):

<Formula label="The gauge-invariant fields" tex="\Phi = \phi + \dot\gamma - \frac{1}{2}\ddot\lambda, \qquad \Theta = \frac{1}{3}\left(H - \nabla^2\lambda\right), \qquad \Xi_i = \beta_i - \frac{1}{2}\dot\varepsilon_i, \qquad h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}" cite="flanagan-hughes" at="Sec. 2.2; transcript p. 12" />

with $\partial_i\Xi_i = 0$. [The paper writes $h_{tt} = +2\phi$, so its $\phi$ has the opposite sign and its rules read $\phi \to \phi - \dot A$ and $\Phi = -\phi + \dot\gamma - \frac12\ddot\lambda$; the page follows the lecture's $h_{tt} = -2\phi$.] [On the transcript Hughes says "beta minus one half epsilon" and "one half H"; the combinations that are actually invariant use the time derivative $\dot\varepsilon_i$, and the normalisation $\frac13$ is the paper's.] Counting again: $1 + 1 + 2 + 2 = 6$ gauge-invariant functions, and $10 = 6 + 4$ with the four gauge functions (transcript p. 12).

<WorkedExample title="Checking that Φ is gauge invariant">
<Problem>
Use the transformation rules to show that $\Phi = \phi + \dot\gamma - \frac12\ddot\lambda$ does not change under a gauge transformation, and explain why $\phi$ alone is not physical.
</Problem>
<Step title="Collect the changes">
$\phi$ gains $\dot A$. $\gamma$ changes by $-A - \dot C$, so $\dot\gamma$ gains $-\dot A - \ddot C$. $\lambda$ changes by $-2C$, so $-\frac12\ddot\lambda$ gains $+\ddot C$.
</Step>
<Step title="Add them">
$\dot A - \dot A - \ddot C + \ddot C = 0$. $\Phi$ is unchanged.
</Step>
<Step title="Why φ alone is not physical">
A gauge with $A = -\int\phi\,dt$ makes $\phi$ vanish everywhere, so $h_{tt}$ by itself can be anything; only the combination $\Phi$ means something. In the static Newtonian case of the last lecture, $\gamma = \lambda = 0$ and $\Phi = \phi$ is the Newtonian potential.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The source, and the field equations

Split the stress-energy tensor the same way (transcript p. 13): $T_{tt} = \rho$, the energy density; $T_{ti} = S_i + \partial_i S$ with $\partial_iS_i = 0$, the energy flow or momentum density; and $T_{ij} = P\delta_{ij} + \sigma_{ij} + \frac12(\partial_i\sigma_j + \partial_j\sigma_i) + (\partial_i\partial_j - \frac13\delta_{ij}\nabla^2)\sigma$, an isotropic pressure, a transverse-traceless anisotropic stress $\sigma_{ij}$, and two more pieces, with $\partial_i\sigma_i = 0$. Local conservation, $\partial^{\mu} T_{\mu\nu} = 0$, ties some of these together: for example $\nabla^2 S = \partial_t\rho$, and $\sigma$ and $\sigma_i$ are similarly fixed by $P$, $S$ and $S_i$ (transcript p. 13). Only $\rho$, $P$, $S_i$ and $\sigma_{ij}$ are freely specifiable, six functions: density, pressure, energy flow and anisotropic stress.

Now the linearized Einstein tensor, written in the gauge-invariant fields (transcript p. 14): $G_{tt} = -\nabla^2\Theta$, $G_{ti} = -\frac12\nabla^2\Xi_i - \partial_i\dot\Theta$, and $G_{ij}$ contains a wave operator acting on $h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}$ plus terms in $\Phi$, $\Theta$ and $\Xi_i$. Equating like with like (divergence-free with divergence-free, and so on) gives the complete set of linearized field equations:

<Formula label="Linearized gravity in gauge-invariant form" tex="\nabla^2\Theta = -8\pi G\rho, \qquad \nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\left(\rho + 3P - 3\dot S\right), \qquad \nabla^2\Xi_i = -16\pi G\,S_i, \qquad \Box h^{\text{TT}}_{ij} = -16\pi G\,\sigma_{ij}" cite="flanagan-hughes" at="Sec. 2.2; transcript p. 14" />

Three are Poisson equations, with no time delay and no radiation: $\Phi$, $\Theta$ and $\Xi_i$, four functions in all, are non-radiative, like the Coulomb and magnetostatic fields. Only $h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}$ obeys a wave equation, and it does so in every gauge (transcript pp. 14–15). As a check, a static Newtonian source ($P = S = 0$) gives $\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho$, Poisson's equation, and $\Theta = -2\Phi$, the $(1 - 2\Phi)$ of the spatial metric.

So in linearized gravity: ten metric functions, four of them gauge, six physical; of those six, four are bound to their sources and two are radiation (transcript p. 15). In practice one still solves in a convenient gauge, such as Lorenz gauge, and then extracts the transverse-traceless part, which is what the next two lectures do.

<Callout kind="cosmology">
This scalar-vector-tensor split is the standard language of cosmological perturbation theory, going back to Bardeen's gauge-invariant formalism; Hughes credits Bertschinger's treatment of cosmological perturbations as the inspiration for the flat-spacetime version here. Its scalars carry the density perturbations that grow into galaxies and leave their imprint on the cosmic microwave background, and its tensor part is the primordial gravitational waves that inflation may have produced.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Any linear equation $\mathcal{D}f = s$ is solved by a Green's function; for the wave operator it is the retarded $G = -\delta(t - t' - |\vec x - \vec x'|)/(4\pi|\vec x - \vec x'|)$.
- In Lorenz gauge, $\bar h_{\mu\nu}(t, \vec x) = 4G\int d^3x'\,T_{\mu\nu}(t - |\vec x - \vec x'|, \vec x')/|\vec x - \vec x'|$ exactly; every component looks radiative.
- Gauge can disguise physics: a Coulomb field in a strange gauge looks like radiation, yet its $F_{\mu\nu}$ is pure Coulomb.
- Splitting $h_{\mu\nu}$ and $T_{\mu\nu}$ into scalars, vectors and tensors under spatial rotations gives ten metric functions: four gauge, and six gauge-invariant fields $\Phi$, $\Theta$, $\Xi_i$, $h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}$.
- $\Phi$, $\Theta$, $\Xi_i$ obey Poisson-type equations (non-radiative); only the two transverse-traceless functions obey $\Box h^{\text{TT}}_{ij} = -16\pi G\,\sigma_{ij}$: gravitational radiation has two polarizations.
