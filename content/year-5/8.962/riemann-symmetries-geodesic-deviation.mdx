This was Hughes's last lecture to a live audience in spring 2020, before MIT moved teaching online because of the COVID-19 pandemic; the following lectures were recorded in an empty room (pp. 1, 20). It finishes the study of curvature: the symmetries of the Riemann tensor, its traces (Ricci) and its traceless part (Weyl), the way curvature pulls nearby geodesics apart, and the Bianchi identity, which leads straight to the Einstein tensor. The signature is $(-,+,+,+)$ and the conventions are those of Carroll's textbook used by the course.

## Recap

Carrying a vector round a small parallelogram, with sides along two coordinate directions, changes it by an amount set by the Riemann tensor (pp. 1–2). Riemann is built from Christoffel symbols, which are not tensors, but their non-tensorial parts cancel; its terms with products of Christoffel symbols are where the nonlinearity of gravity enters (p. 2).

## The symmetries of Riemann

A four-index object in four dimensions seems to have 256 components, but symmetries cut this down a lot (p. 2). One is visible straight from the formula: swapping the last two indices changes the sign. Geometrically this is obvious: it means going round the loop in the opposite direction, the reverse holonomy (p. 2).

For the rest Hughes takes a shortcut (pp. 3–4). Lower the first index, $R_{\alpha\mu\lambda\sigma} = g_{\alpha\beta}R^\beta{}_{\mu\lambda\sigma}$, and work in a local Lorentz frame at a point. There the metric is $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ and the Christoffel symbols vanish, but their derivatives do not, because a little curvature remains. So the products of Christoffel symbols drop out, and writing the derivatives of $\Gamma$ in terms of the metric gives (pp. 3–4)

<Formula label="Riemann in a local Lorentz frame" tex="R_{\alpha\mu\lambda\sigma} = \tfrac12\left(\partial_\lambda\partial_\mu g_{\alpha\sigma} - \partial_\lambda\partial_\alpha g_{\mu\sigma} - \partial_\sigma\partial_\mu g_{\alpha\lambda} + \partial_\sigma\partial_\alpha g_{\lambda\mu}\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 3–4" />

Only second derivatives of the metric appear: exactly the part that could not be transformed away when building a local Lorentz frame (p. 4). Since this is a tensor relation, symmetries found in this convenient frame hold in every frame (p. 3). Staring at the formula and swapping pairs of indices shows (pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="Symmetries of the Riemann tensor" tex="R_{\alpha\mu\lambda\sigma} = -R_{\alpha\mu\sigma\lambda}, \qquad R_{\alpha\mu\lambda\sigma} = -R_{\mu\alpha\lambda\sigma}, \qquad R_{\alpha\mu\lambda\sigma} = R_{\lambda\sigma\alpha\mu}, \qquad R_{\alpha\mu\lambda\sigma} + R_{\alpha\lambda\sigma\mu} + R_{\alpha\sigma\mu\lambda} = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 4–5" />

antisymmetry in the last pair and in the first pair, symmetry under exchanging the two pairs, and a vanishing cyclic sum over the last three indices. The last can also be written as the vanishing of Riemann antisymmetrised on its last three indices, and Hughes's posted notes show that the two forms are equivalent (p. 5). (A student caught an index slip on the board while he was writing the first symmetry, p. 4.) Counting the components these leave is a small exercise in combinatorics (p. 5):

<Formula label="Independent components" tex="n^4 \;\to\; \frac{n^2\left(n^2 - 1\right)}{12}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 5" />

which is 20 for $n = 4$ (Hughes first says "$n$ equals 20" and corrects himself, p. 5).

He also writes out the metric of a freely falling frame including its second-order corrections, with a time part, an off-diagonal part and a space part, and calls them **Riemann normal coordinates**, discussed in Eric Poisson's *A Relativist's Toolkit* (p. 5). The transcript does not read out the coefficients. Coordinates built along a freely falling world line in this way are usually called Fermi normal coordinates (Riemann normal coordinates proper, built about a single event, have $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac13 R_{\mu\alpha\nu\beta}x^\alpha x^\beta$); their standard form is

<Formula label="Fermi normal coordinates of a freely falling observer (standard form)" tex="g_{00} = -1 - R_{0i0j}\,x^i x^j, \qquad g_{0i} = -\tfrac23\,R_{0jik}\,x^j x^k, \qquad g_{ij} = \delta_{ij} - \tfrac13\,R_{ikjl}\,x^k x^l" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 5 (coefficients from the standard form, not read out in the lecture)" />

so the deviations from Minkowski are quadratic in the distance and set by the curvature. With the curvature tensor in hand, Hughes says, every geometric tool needed for a relativistic theory of gravity is now available (p. 5).

## Ricci and Weyl

Traces of Riemann are taken with the metric (p. 6). Contracting the first two indices, or the last two, gives zero, since a symmetric metric meets an antisymmetric pair. Contracting the first and third (or, equivalently, the second and fourth) gives the one meaningful trace, the **Ricci tensor** (p. 6):

<Formula label="The Ricci tensor" tex="R_{\mu\nu} = R^\alpha{}_{\mu\alpha\nu} = g^{\alpha\beta}R_{\beta\mu\alpha\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 6" />

It is symmetric (pp. 6–7). Writing it out from the Christoffel symbols and using the party trick of Lecture 9, $\Gamma^\alpha_{\alpha\mu} = \partial_\mu\ln\sqrt{|g|}$,

<Formula label="Ricci in terms of Christoffel symbols" tex="R_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\alpha\Gamma^\alpha_{\mu\nu} - \partial_\mu\partial_\nu\ln\sqrt{|g|} + \Gamma^\alpha_{\alpha\beta}\Gamma^\beta_{\mu\nu} - \Gamma^\alpha_{\nu\beta}\Gamma^\beta_{\alpha\mu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 7" />

The first three terms are visibly symmetric in $\mu$ and $\nu$, and the last turns into itself on renaming the dummy indices $\alpha$ and $\beta$ (p. 7). So in four dimensions Ricci has 10 independent components, half of Riemann's 20. Its trace is the **Ricci scalar** or curvature scalar (p. 7):

<Formula label="The Ricci scalar" tex="R = g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 7" />

Where are the other 10? In the **Weyl tensor**, which Hughes writes down without derivation (pp. 7–8): Riemann minus its trace parts, in $n$ dimensions

<Formula label="The Weyl tensor" tex="C_{\alpha\mu\lambda\sigma} = R_{\alpha\mu\lambda\sigma} - \frac{1}{n-2}\left(g_{\alpha\lambda}R_{\sigma\mu} - g_{\alpha\sigma}R_{\lambda\mu} - g_{\mu\lambda}R_{\sigma\alpha} + g_{\mu\sigma}R_{\lambda\alpha}\right) + \frac{R\,\left(g_{\alpha\lambda}g_{\sigma\mu} - g_{\alpha\sigma}g_{\lambda\mu}\right)}{(n-1)(n-2)}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 7–8" />

("who ordered that?", as Rabi said of the muon). It has the same symmetries as Riemann, but all its traces vanish, which leaves 10 independent components in four dimensions (p. 8). Hughes notes that the name is pronounced "vile", which suits it (p. 8). Loosely, Riemann carries the same information as Ricci plus Weyl (p. 8).

The split matters physically (pp. 8–9). The field equations will set a slightly modified Ricci tensor equal to the stress-energy tensor, so the 10 components of the source fix the 10 components of Ricci. In vacuum, Ricci is therefore zero, yet there is still curvature (tides, gravitational effects): that is Weyl. It describes gravity in empty regions and is central to gravitational radiation (p. 9). Its role in conformal transformations is left to Carroll (p. 9).

$R_{\phi\phi} = R^\theta{}_{\phi\theta\phi} = \sin^2\theta$. For $R_{\theta\theta} = R^\phi{}_{\theta\phi\theta}$, lower and raise with the metric: $R_{\phi\theta\phi\theta} = R_{\theta\phi\theta\phi} = g_{\theta\theta}\sin^2\theta = a^2\sin^2\theta$ (antisymmetry in each index pair, used twice), so $R^\phi{}_{\theta\phi\theta} = a^2\sin^2\theta/(a^2\sin^2\theta) = 1$.

## Geodesic deviation

The defining feature of curvature is that initially parallel geodesics stop being parallel; the Riemann tensor quantifies this (p. 9). Hughes follows a more physical route than Carroll's brief one, closer to Schutz's (p. 9). Take two nearby geodesics, close enough to share a local Lorentz frame, $\gamma_u$ with tangent $u^\alpha = dx^\alpha/d\lambda$ and $\gamma_v$ with tangent $v^\alpha$, both with affine parameter $\lambda$, starting at the events A and A′ at $\lambda_0$ (p. 10). The **geodesic displacement vector** joins equal parameter values (p. 10):

<Formula label="Displacement between the geodesics" tex="\xi^\alpha(\lambda) = x^\alpha_v(\lambda) - x^\alpha_u(\lambda)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 10" />

They start parallel: $u^\alpha(\lambda_0) = v^\alpha(\lambda_0)$, so $d\xi^\alpha/d\lambda = 0$ at $\lambda_0$ (pp. 10–11). Centre the local Lorentz frame on A: there $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu}$ and all Christoffel symbols vanish. At A′ the metric is still $\eta$ to first order, but the Christoffel symbols are not zero, because A′ is a distance $\xi$ away and picks up the second-order curvature terms: $\Gamma(A') \approx \xi^\beta\partial_\beta\Gamma$ (p. 11). Subtracting the geodesic equation at A (where it is just $d^2x^\alpha/d\lambda^2 = 0$) from that at A′, with the two velocities equal at the start (pp. 11–12):

<Formula label="Relative acceleration in the local Lorentz frame" tex="\frac{d^2\xi^\alpha}{d\lambda^2} = -\,\partial_\beta\Gamma^\alpha_{\mu\nu}\;u^\mu u^\nu\,\xi^\beta" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 12" />

This already contains the key physics: second derivatives of the metric, coupled to the velocity and the displacement, push the geodesics apart (p. 12). But a derivative of a Christoffel symbol is not a tensor, so it must be "dressed in a suit and tie" (pp. 12–13). Replace $d/d\lambda = u^\beta\partial_\beta$ by the covariant $D/d\lambda = u^\beta\nabla_\beta$ and apply it twice to $\xi$ (pp. 13–14). Do not set the Christoffel symbols to zero until all derivatives are taken, since their derivatives survive. In the local Lorentz frame at A, plain Christoffel symbols then vanish, the term with the geodesic equation of $u$ vanishes, and the terms with the initial difference of the velocities vanish because the geodesics start parallel; what is left is (pp. 14–15)

<Formula label="Covariant relative acceleration" tex="\frac{D^2\xi^\alpha}{d\lambda^2} = \frac{d^2\xi^\alpha}{d\lambda^2} + \partial_\beta\Gamma^\alpha_{\mu\nu}\;u^\beta u^\mu\,\xi^\nu" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 14–15" />

Inserting the previous result gives a difference of two derivatives of $\Gamma$, which after relabelling dummy indices is exactly the Riemann tensor in a local Lorentz frame (p. 15). Since it is a tensor equation, it holds in every frame:

<Formula label="The geodesic deviation equation" tex="\frac{D^2\xi^\alpha}{d\lambda^2} = R^\alpha{}_{\mu\beta\nu}\;u^\mu u^\beta\,\xi^\nu" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 15, 17" />

(named at the start of p. 17). Doing the calculation in a special frame may feel unsatisfying; more rigorous treatments are in the textbooks, but this one shows that it is the second derivative of the metric in the freely falling frame that drives the geodesics apart (pp. 15–16).

This is the key equation for tides in general relativity (p. 16). In a freely falling frame there is no gravitational acceleration any more, so what gravity does is tidal. It gives a frame-independent description of how a gravitational-wave detector responds: two freely falling test masses, far enough apart to feel the curvature, have their separation changed by a passing wave, and analyses of detectors such as LIGO rest on this equation. It also gives the tidal stresses on an extended body that spills across its local Lorentz frame (p. 16).

## The Bianchi identity

One more identity, messy but essential (pp. 16–17). Recall that the commutator of covariant derivatives on a vector gives Riemann, $[\nabla_\lambda, \nabla_\sigma]V^\alpha = R^\alpha{}_{\beta\lambda\sigma}V^\beta$, the differential version of the holonomy, and that each index of a bigger tensor gets its own Riemann term, with a minus sign for a lower index (p. 17). Hughes writes two relations for a one-form $p_\delta$ (pp. 17–18):

<Formula label="Two commutator relations" tex="\text{(A)}\quad \left[\nabla_\alpha, \nabla_\beta\right]\nabla_\gamma\,p_\delta = -R^\mu{}_{\gamma\alpha\beta}\,\nabla_\mu p_\delta - R^\mu{}_{\delta\alpha\beta}\,\nabla_\gamma p_\mu, \qquad \text{(B)}\quad \nabla_\alpha\left[\nabla_\beta, \nabla_\gamma\right]p_\delta = -\nabla_\alpha\!\left(R^\mu{}_{\delta\beta\gamma}\,p_\mu\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 17–18" />

The "crazy" step is to antisymmetrise both on $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$ (p. 18): adding the even permutations and subtracting the odd ones, the left-hand side of (A) becomes the antisymmetrised left-hand side of (B), the commutators just slid along by one. So the antisymmetrised right-hand sides must agree too (pp. 18–19). On the right, the term with Riemann antisymmetrised on its last three indices vanishes by the cyclic symmetry of Riemann; the two terms with $R^\mu{}_{\delta\alpha\beta}\nabla_\gamma p_\mu$ are cyclic permutations of each other and cancel (p. 19). What remains is $p_\mu$ times the antisymmetrised covariant derivative of the Riemann tensor, and since nothing was assumed about $p$, that antisymmetrised derivative must vanish (p. 19). This is the **Bianchi identity**; with all indices down it reads

<Formula label="The Bianchi identity" tex="\nabla_\lambda R_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu} + \nabla_\alpha R_{\beta\lambda\mu\nu} + \nabla_\beta R_{\lambda\alpha\mu\nu} = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 19" />

## The Einstein tensor

Contract the Bianchi identity with the metric, which passes freely through covariant derivatives (p. 20). The result, derived in posted notes and in the first recorded lecture, is that a particular combination of the Ricci tensor and the Ricci scalar has zero divergence:

<Formula label="The contracted Bianchi identity" tex="\nabla^\mu G_{\mu\nu} = 0, \qquad G_{\mu\nu} = R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\,g_{\mu\nu}\,R" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 20" />

$G_{\mu\nu}$ is the **Einstein tensor** (p. 20). The plan for a theory of gravity is to replace the mass density as source by the stress-energy tensor, which is a symmetric, divergence-free two-index tensor; the other side should be built from two derivatives of the metric, that is, from curvature. A divergence-free, two-index curvature tensor is exactly what was needed, and, Hughes notes, it has Einstein's name on it (p. 20).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Curvature and the expanding universe">
For the Robertson–Walker metric of cosmology, the Einstein tensor computed from the scale factor gives, once set equal to the stress-energy tensor of a perfect fluid, the Friedmann equations of 8.286. Their time–time part involves (ȧ/a)² and k/a², and the Ricci scalar of the spatial sections is 6k/a², the three-dimensional counterpart of the 2/a² found here for a sphere.
</Callout>

## Summary

- In a local Lorentz frame $R_{\alpha\mu\lambda\sigma}$ is a combination of second derivatives of the metric. It is antisymmetric in each index pair, symmetric under exchange of the pairs, and its cyclic sum over the last three indices vanishes; $n^4$ components drop to $n^2(n^2-1)/12$, 20 in four dimensions.
- The Ricci tensor $R_{\mu\nu} = R^\alpha{}_{\mu\alpha\nu}$ is symmetric (10 components); $R = g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu}$. The traceless Weyl tensor holds the other 10 and describes curvature in vacuum.
- Geodesic deviation: $D^2\xi^\alpha/d\lambda^2 = R^\alpha{}_{\mu\beta\nu}u^\mu u^\beta\xi^\nu$, the relativistic law of tides, behind gravitational-wave detection.
- Bianchi identity: $\nabla_\lambda R_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu} + \nabla_\alpha R_{\beta\lambda\mu\nu} + \nabla_\beta R_{\lambda\alpha\mu\nu} = 0$; contracted, $\nabla^\mu G_{\mu\nu} = 0$ with $G_{\mu\nu} = R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12 g_{\mu\nu}R$, ready to be matched to the stress-energy tensor.
