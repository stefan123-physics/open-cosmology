The previous lecture reached the field equation the way Einstein did: demand the Newtonian limit, then pick the divergence-free curvature tensor. This lecture reaches it a second way, from an **action principle**. The route, developed by David Hilbert at almost the same time as Einstein's in 1915, makes clearer which choices lead to general relativity, and shows the natural way to modify it if it ever proves incomplete (transcript pp. 1–3). Most equations of this lecture were written on the board without being read aloud, so the page gives them in their standard forms and normalisations. Hughes adds that Hilbert gave Einstein priority, crediting him with the physical insight even though Hilbert's derivation is arguably the more elegant one (transcript p. 3). The page uses signature $(-, +, +, +)$ and $c = 1$.

## Recap: Einstein's route

Hughes recaps (transcript pp. 1–2). The principle of equivalence carries laws from a freely falling frame into curved spacetime in tensor form: free fall becomes parallel transport of the four-velocity along itself, and $\partial_{\mu} T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ becomes $\nabla_{\mu} T^{\mu\nu} = 0$. The field equation was found by upgrading $\rho$ in Poisson's equation to $T_{\mu\nu}$ and two derivatives of the potential to a divergence-free curvature tensor; the Newtonian limit fixed the constant at $8\pi G$, or $8\pi G/c^4$ with $c$ written out.

## Action principles in brief

Schematically, the action is a Lagrange density integrated over all of spacetime (transcript p. 3). It must be a scalar: it cannot depend on which freely falling frame one uses. The coordinate volume $d^4x$ is not a proper volume by itself, so the factor $\sqrt{-g}$ is written out, where $g$ is the determinant of the metric:

<Formula label="An action as an integral over spacetime" tex="S = \int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\mathcal{L} = \int d^4x\,\hat{\mathcal{L}}, \qquad \hat{\mathcal{L}} = \sqrt{-g}\,\mathcal{L}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3" />

Here $\mathcal{L}$ is a scalar and $\hat{\mathcal{L}}$ a scalar density. The physical field configuration makes the action stationary: varying the fields must leave $S$ unchanged to first order, for any variation, which leads to Euler–Lagrange equations (transcript p. 4).

For a single scalar field $\phi$ in flat spacetime, with $\mathcal{L}(\phi, \partial_{\mu}\phi)$, the field and its derivative are varied separately (transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Varying a scalar-field action" tex="\delta S = \int d^4x\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\phi}\,\delta\phi + \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{\mu}\phi)}\,\partial_{\mu}(\delta\phi)\right]" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 4" />

The second term is integrated by parts to move the derivative off $\delta\phi$. That produces a boundary term, which Hughes discards: the integral runs over all of spacetime, whose "boundary" is at infinity, and this is safe here, though he flags it as a step that deserves more care, and one that returns for gravity (transcript pp. 4–5). What remains is

<Formula label="Integrated by parts, and the Euler–Lagrange equation" tex="\delta S = \int d^4x\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\phi} - \partial_{\mu}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{\mu}\phi)}\right]\delta\phi = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\phi} - \partial_{\mu}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{\mu}\phi)} = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 4–5" />

A typical Lagrangian has a **kinetic term**, quadratic in the derivatives of the field, and, for a massive field, a term in $\phi^2$ that acts like a potential energy (transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="A massive scalar field" tex="\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{2}\eta^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{\mu}\phi\,\partial_{\nu}\phi - \frac{1}{2}m^2\phi^2" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 5" />

Then $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\phi = -m^2\phi$ and $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial(\partial_{\mu}\phi) = -\eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu}\phi$, whose derivative is minus the flat-spacetime wave operator acting on $\phi$. The Euler–Lagrange equation is the **massive Klein–Gordon equation** (transcript pp. 5–6):

<Formula label="Klein–Gordon equation" tex="\Box\phi - m^2\phi = 0, \qquad \Box \equiv \eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu} = -\partial_t^2 + \nabla^2" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 6" />

[Hughes says the field derivative gives "$m^2\phi$"; with this sign of the Lagrangian it is $-m^2\phi$, and the equation is the same either way.]

## Choosing the gravitational Lagrangian

Two decisions are needed (transcript pp. 6–7). First, which field to vary: here the metric is the fundamental field, and everything else (connection, curvature) is built from it. Hughes returns to this choice below. Second, the Lagrangian itself, which must

1. be a scalar (a scalar density once $\sqrt{-g}$ is included), so the action is a scalar, and
2. be built from **curvature tensors**.

The second requirement has consequences. The Lagrangian should not be something a change of frame can remove. At any event a freely falling frame makes the metric flat and the connection zero, but it cannot remove the curvature (transcript p. 6). The simplest scalar made from curvature is the Ricci scalar, so Hughes sets $\hat{\mathcal{L}} = \sqrt{-g}\,R$ and includes a factor $1/16\pi G$ chosen, with hindsight, to give the right constant (transcript p. 7). This is the **Einstein–Hilbert action**:

<Formula label="The Einstein–Hilbert action" tex="S_H = \frac{1}{16\pi G}\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,R, \qquad R = g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 7" />

Other choices are possible, and the point of the lecture is that this simplest one reproduces the Einstein equation: in this sense general relativity is the simplest geometric theory of gravity (transcript p. 7).

## Varying the metric

Varying $g^{\mu\nu}$, the integrand $\sqrt{-g}\,g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu}$ changes in three places (transcript pp. 7–8):

<Formula label="The three pieces of the variation" tex="\delta S_H = \frac{1}{16\pi G}\int d^4x\left[\delta\sqrt{-g}\;g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu} + \sqrt{-g}\;\delta g^{\mu\nu}\,R_{\mu\nu} + \sqrt{-g}\;g^{\mu\nu}\,\delta R_{\mu\nu}\right]" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 7–8" />

The middle term already has the form wanted. The first uses one of the determinant identities ("party tricks") from earlier in the course, $\delta g = g\,g^{\mu\nu}\delta g_{\mu\nu} = -g\,g_{\mu\nu}\delta g^{\mu\nu}$ (transcript p. 8):

<Formula label="Variation of the volume factor" tex="\delta\sqrt{-g} = -\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{-g}\;g_{\mu\nu}\,\delta g^{\mu\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 8" />

The third is where the work is. "By something of a miracle", the change in the Ricci tensor is a difference of two covariant derivatives of the change in the Christoffel symbols, and $\delta\Gamma$, unlike $\Gamma$, is a tensor (transcript p. 8):

<Formula label="Variation of the Ricci tensor" tex="\delta R_{\mu\nu} = \nabla_{\rho}\,\delta\Gamma^{\rho}{}_{\nu\mu} - \nabla_{\nu}\,\delta\Gamma^{\rho}{}_{\rho\mu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 8" />

Writing out $\delta\Gamma$ in terms of $\delta g^{\mu\nu}$ is lengthy, but after tracing with $g^{\mu\nu}$ most terms cancel, and what is left has no free indices and is a total divergence (transcript p. 8):

<Formula label="The traced Ricci variation is a divergence" tex="g^{\mu\nu}\,\delta R_{\mu\nu} = \nabla_{\sigma}\left(g_{\mu\nu}\nabla^{\sigma}\delta g^{\mu\nu} - \nabla_{\lambda}\,\delta g^{\sigma\lambda}\right) \equiv \nabla_{\sigma} V^{\sigma}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 8" />

These covariant derivatives do not vanish, because they act on the variation $\delta g^{\mu\nu}$, an arbitrary tensor, not on the metric itself, whose covariant derivative is always zero (transcript p. 9). [Hughes puts this as $\nabla g = 0$ holding only once the variation is finished; the point is that $\nabla_\sigma\delta g^{\mu\nu}$ need not vanish.] Putting the three pieces together, with $R = g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu}$ (transcript p. 9):

<Formula label="The varied Einstein–Hilbert action" tex="\delta S_H = \frac{1}{16\pi G}\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\left[\left(R_{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{2}R\,g_{\mu\nu}\right)\delta g^{\mu\nu} + \nabla_{\sigma} V^{\sigma}\right]" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 9" />

The Einstein tensor appears by itself. Without the divergence term, $\delta S_H = 0$ for every $\delta g^{\mu\nu}$ would give $G_{\mu\nu} = 0$, the field equation with no source.

<WorkedExample title="Checking the volume variation on an expanding metric">
<Problem>
Take $g_{\mu\nu} = \text{diag}(-1, a^2, a^2, a^2)$ with a constant $a$, so $\sqrt{-g} = a^3$. Change $a$ to $a + \delta a$. Check $\delta\sqrt{-g} = -\frac12\sqrt{-g}\,g_{\mu\nu}\delta g^{\mu\nu}$, and evaluate both sides for $a = 2$, $\delta a = 0.01$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Directly">
$\delta(a^3) = 3a^2\,\delta a$, to first order. For $a = 2$, $\delta a = 0.01$: $3 \times 4 \times 0.01 = 0.12$ (the exact change, $2.01^3 - 8 = 0.120601$, differs only at second order).
</Step>
<Step title="From the formula">
The inverse metric has $g^{ii} = 1/a^2$, so $\delta g^{ii} = -2\,\delta a/a^3$ for each of the three spatial $i$, and $g_{ii}\,\delta g^{ii} = -2\,\delta a/a$. Summing the three gives $-6\,\delta a/a$, and $-\frac12 a^3 \times (-6\,\delta a/a) = 3a^2\,\delta a = 0.12$. The two agree.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The boundary term

How to get rid of $\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\nabla_{\sigma} V^\sigma$? Carroll's textbook uses the divergence theorem: a proper volume integral of a total divergence becomes an integral over the boundary, and if the integral covers all of space and time, the boundary contributions are taken to vanish (transcript p. 9). Hughes calls this a reasonable physical argument but glib, and notes that Carroll says so himself: anyone dissatisfied is right to be (transcript pp. 9–10). There are two rigorous fixes.

- **The Palatini variation** (transcript pp. 10–11). Treat the Ricci scalar as built from the connection, not from the metric, and vary metric and connection independently. Varying the metric gives the vacuum Einstein equation, and varying the connection (taken to be torsion-free) forces it to be **metric compatible**, $\nabla_{\rho} g_{\mu\nu} = 0$. So the connection that the course chose earlier on physical grounds, the one that defines parallel transport and gives the Christoffel symbols, comes out of the variational principle by itself. Hughes had hoped to present this calculation, but the move to online teaching in the COVID-19 evacuation of the campus left no time to prepare it.
- **A careful boundary** (transcript pp. 11–12). Keep the metric variation, but define the three-dimensional boundary of the four-dimensional region carefully, as a slice through spacetime. Part of the curvature of such a slice is not intrinsic to spacetime but comes from how the slice sits in it, its **extrinsic curvature**, as a cylinder's surface is intrinsically flat (parallel geodesics on it stay parallel) yet looks curved because it is rolled up in three dimensions. Including the extrinsic curvature in the Lagrangian on the boundary produces a term that cancels the divergence. Hughes points to Appendix E of Wald's *General Relativity*, a very mathematical text, for the details. [This boundary term is now usually called the Gibbons–Hawking–York term.]

Either way, the result is $G_{\mu\nu} = 0$, the **vacuum Einstein equation**.

## Adding matter

More generally, the action is the Einstein–Hilbert action plus an action for matter and fields (transcript pp. 12–13):

<Formula label="Gravity plus matter" tex="S = \int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\left[\frac{R}{16\pi G} + \mathcal{L}_M\right]" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 12–13" />

Stationarity now gives the Einstein tensor balanced by the variation of the matter action. Defining the stress-energy tensor as (transcript p. 13)

<Formula label="Stress-energy tensor from the matter action" tex="T_{\mu\nu} = -\frac{2}{\sqrt{-g}}\,\frac{\delta\left(\sqrt{-g}\,\mathcal{L}_M\right)}{\delta g^{\mu\nu}}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 13" />

turns the result into the same equation as before:

<Formula label="The field equation again" tex="G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 13" />

The definition works in every case where the stress-energy tensor is known. For electromagnetism, take $\mathcal{L}_M = -\frac14 F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$, with $F$ the Faraday tensor, write $F^{\mu\nu} = g^{\mu\alpha}g^{\nu\beta}F_{\alpha\beta}$ to show every metric factor, and vary only the metric, holding the fields fixed (transcript pp. 13–14). The result is the standard electromagnetic stress-energy tensor (in units where the energy density is $\frac12(E^2 + B^2)$; textbooks such as Jackson give it):

<Formula label="Electromagnetic stress-energy tensor" tex="T_{\mu\nu} = F_{\mu\alpha}F_{\nu}{}^{\alpha} - \frac{1}{4}g_{\mu\nu}F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 14" />

<WorkedExample title="The stress-energy of a uniform electric field">
<Problem>
In flat spacetime, a uniform field $E$ points along $x$, so $F_{01} = -E$, $F_{10} = E$ and all other components vanish. Use the formula above to find $T_{00}$, $T_{11}$ and $T_{22}$, and give the SI energy density for $E = 10^6$ V/m.
</Problem>
<Step title="The invariant">
$F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta} = 2F_{01}F^{01} = 2(-E)(E) = -2E^2$, since raising the index 0 flips a sign.
</Step>
<Step title="The components">
$T_{00} = F_{01}F_{0}{}^{1} - \frac14(-1)(-2E^2) = E^2 - \frac12E^2 = \frac12E^2$, the energy density. $T_{11} = F_{10}F_{1}{}^{0} - \frac14(-2E^2) = -E^2 + \frac12E^2 = -\frac12E^2$: a tension along the field lines. $T_{22} = 0 + \frac12E^2$: a pressure across them.
</Step>
<Step title="In SI units">
The energy density is $\frac12\varepsilon_0E^2 = 0.5 \times 8.854\times10^{-12} \times 10^{12} \approx 4.43\ \text{J/m}^3$. Through $8\pi G/c^4$ this is a curvature of only about $9.2\times10^{-43}\ \text{m}^{-2}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Why bother: modified gravity

Is a second derivation needed? Hughes's answer is that the action makes the assumptions visible (transcript pp. 14–17). The Lagrangian had to be a scalar built from curvature, and $R$ is the simplest such scalar, not the only one. To build a theory that agrees with general relativity almost everywhere but differs in some regime, change the Lagrangian.

**Small curvature.** Suppose (transcript p. 14)

<Formula label="A correction at small curvature" tex="\hat{\mathcal{L}} = \sqrt{-g}\left(R - \frac{\alpha}{R}\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 14" />

The correction competes with $R$ when $R$ is of order $\sqrt\alpha$ or smaller. A few pages of algebra, the same variation as above, give a new field equation (transcript pp. 14–15):

<Formula label="Field equation of the 1/R theory" tex="\left(1 + \frac{\alpha}{R^2}\right)R_{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{2}\left(1 - \frac{\alpha}{R^2}\right)R\,g_{\mu\nu} + \alpha\left(g_{\mu\nu}\nabla_{\sigma}\nabla^{\sigma} - \nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\right)R^{-2} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 14–15" />

For $\alpha = 0$ it is the Einstein equation. Sean Carroll and collaborators proposed this theory in *Physical Review D* (2004) because the evidence for a cosmological constant comes from the accelerating expansion of the universe, on the largest scales, where curvature is smallest and the tests of general relativity are weakest (transcript p. 15). The theory does not fix $\alpha$: it is a knob, and tuned to the observed acceleration it worked well. But with that $\alpha$ the planets' orbits come out wrong, so the theory was falsified, a model, Hughes says, of how to propose and test a modification of gravity (transcript p. 15).

**Large curvature.** Add a term in $R^2$ (transcript p. 16):

<Formula label="A correction at large curvature" tex="S = \frac{1}{16\pi G}\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\left(R + \beta R^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 16" />

The second term dominates when $R$ exceeds about $1/\beta$. A common expectation is that this scale is where quantum gravity becomes important, with $\beta$ of order $\hbar$ in units where $G = c = 1$, that is, the Planck length squared. [Hughes says $\beta$ of order $\hbar^{-1}$; for the correction to matter at the Planck curvature, $R \sim 1/\hbar$, $\beta$ must be of order $\hbar$.] Such terms appear when general relativity is an effective theory, obtained by averaging over small-scale degrees of freedom.

**A scalar field.** Let the coupling of the metric to its source depend on a scalar field $\psi$ filling spacetime, and give $\psi$ its own action (transcript p. 16):

<Formula label="A scalar-tensor action" tex="S = \int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\left[f(\psi)\,R - \frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\psi\,\partial_{\nu}\psi - V(\psi)\right]" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 16" />

These **scalar-tensor theories** act as if the gravitational constant varied from place to place, perhaps bigger or smaller near a black hole than far away. Terms like these arise naturally in attempts to quantize gravity; Carroll's textbook discusses them, in a section Hughes calls a little out of date. A scalar field changes the dynamics, including the gravitational radiation a system emits, so in 2020 Hughes pointed to LIGO observations of merging black holes and neutron stars as among the most stringent recent limits, with no sign of a scalar so far (transcript pp. 16–17). [Solar-system tests, such as the Cassini time-delay measurement, still give the tightest bound on the simplest scalar-tensor theory, Brans–Dicke.]

<Callout kind="cosmology">
The $1/R$ theory was built to explain the accelerating expansion without a cosmological constant, and it shows how cosmology tests gravity on the largest scales. The scalar-field action is also the template for inflation: a scalar field with a kinetic term and a potential, coupled to gravity, which drives the early universe's exponential expansion.
</Callout>

## Halfway: what comes next

With this lecture the first half of the course is complete (transcript pp. 17–19). Given a metric, geodesics give the motion of bodies and the curvature gives the tides; the field equation gives the metric. Written as differential equations for the metric, the field equations are a horrible mess of coupled, nonlinear partial differential equations, and the rest of the course solves them three ways:

1. **Weak fields**: spacetime as flat plus a small correction, which linearizes the equations (the next lectures).
2. **Symmetry**: the most symmetric spacetimes describe cosmology; slightly less symmetric ones describe self-gravitating fluid balls ("stars") and, where no fluid can hold itself up, black holes.
3. **No shortcuts**: numerical relativity, putting the full equations on a computer.

## Summary

- An action is a scalar Lagrange density integrated with the proper volume element: $S = \int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\mathcal{L}$; stationarity gives Euler–Lagrange equations (for a massive scalar field, the Klein–Gordon equation).
- The gravitational Lagrangian must be a scalar built from curvature, which cannot be transformed away; the simplest is $R$: $S_H = \frac{1}{16\pi G}\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\,R$.
- Varying $g^{\mu\nu}$ uses $\delta\sqrt{-g} = -\frac12\sqrt{-g}\,g_{\mu\nu}\delta g^{\mu\nu}$ and $g^{\mu\nu}\delta R_{\mu\nu} = \nabla_{\sigma} V^\sigma$, giving $\delta S_H \propto \int\sqrt{-g}\,(G_{\mu\nu}\delta g^{\mu\nu} + \nabla_{\sigma} V^\sigma)$.
- The divergence is removed glibly by the divergence theorem, or rigorously by the Palatini variation (which also yields the metric-compatible connection) or a boundary term with extrinsic curvature (Wald, Appendix E).
- With matter, $T_{\mu\nu} = -\frac{2}{\sqrt{-g}}\,\delta(\sqrt{-g}\mathcal{L}_M)/\delta g^{\mu\nu}$ and $G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}$; electromagnetism gives its familiar stress-energy tensor.
- Other Lagrangians modify gravity: $R - \alpha/R$ (small curvature, ruled out by the solar system), $R + \beta R^2$ (large curvature), scalar-tensor theories (limited by the solar system and LIGO). General relativity is the simplest choice.
