The last lecture defined gravitational waves on flat spacetime. Near weakly curved sources such as the solar system or a galaxy, the flat analysis carries over well, because the wave varies much faster than its surroundings; but waves also travel near black holes and across an expanding universe, which are far from flat (transcript pp. 1–2). This lecture, which Hughes flags as more advanced in its details, does two things: it shows how to describe a wave on a curved background, and it uses that machinery to find the energy a wave carries, ending in a second formula also called the quadrupole formula. Signature $(-, +, +, +)$, $c = 1$ unless written out. A hat marks a background quantity.

## Wave or background?

Write the metric as a background plus a small wave (transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Background plus wave" tex="g_{\mu\nu} = \hat g_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 2" />

where $\hat g_{\mu\nu}$ may vary in space and time, and terms of order $h^2$ are dropped for now. The hard question is conceptual. A measurement (geodesic deviation, the travel time of light) is sensitive to the curvature of the whole metric. How can one tell the curvature of the wave from that of the background (transcript pp. 2–3)?

The answer, which Hughes insists is a fundamental point and not a trick, is the **separation of scales** (transcript p. 3). A wave oscillates on length and time scales much shorter than those of the background. On the ocean, a ripple with a period of a second and a wavelength of a metre or two is obviously not the curvature of an Earth some 6,000 km in radius (6,371 km, more precisely) (transcript pp. 3–4). So introduce long scales $L$ and $T$ for the background and short scales $\lambda$ and $\tau$, the wavelength and period of the wave (transcript p. 4). For LIGO, the background curvature near the Earth changes over hours (the Moon's tides, the rotating Earth) and its curvature length is enormous, about $\sqrt{c^2R_\oplus^3/GM_\oplus} \approx 2.4\times10^8$ km [Hughes guesses tens of thousands of kilometres, admitting he had not worked it out], while the waves vary in tenths of a second down to milliseconds (transcript pp. 4–5).

Then average over a region several wavelengths and periods across, but smaller than $L$ and $T$ (transcript p. 5). The background hardly changes over that region; the wave oscillates several times and averages to zero:

<Formula label="Averaging separates background and wave" tex="\langle g_{\mu\nu}\rangle = \hat g_{\mu\nu}, \qquad h_{\mu\nu} = g_{\mu\nu} - \langle g_{\mu\nu}\rangle" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 5" />

Averaging a tensor is subtle, because tensors at different points live in different tangent spaces and cannot simply be added, and an integral is a sum (transcript p. 6). Dieter Brill and James Hartle made it rigorous in 1964: integrate over a proper four-volume with a weighting function $f$, normalised to 1, which can be pictured as a Gaussian of width $l$ in every direction, peaked at the point of interest (transcript p. 6). The result is not exact: it holds up to corrections of order $\lambda/L$. If an analysis gives strange results, check the assumption: a "wave" that varies on the background's own scales, with a large amplitude, is outside the regime where the idea of radiation applies (transcript pp. 6–7).

## Linearizing about a curved background

Now repeat linearized theory about $\hat g_{\mu\nu}$ instead of $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ (transcript pp. 7–8). Indices on $h$ are raised with $\hat g$, and as before the inverse metric is $g^{\mu\nu} = \hat g^{\mu\nu} - h^{\mu\nu}$. In the connection, the purely background terms form $\hat\Gamma$, and after some staring the terms linear in $h$ combine into a shift with the same structure as the Christoffel formula, but with background covariant derivatives $\hat\nabla$ in place of partial derivatives (transcript p. 8):

<Formula label="Perturbed connection" tex="\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\mu\nu} = \hat\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\mu\nu} + \delta\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\mu\nu}, \qquad \delta\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{2}\hat g^{\alpha\beta}\left(\hat\nabla_{\mu} h_{\beta\nu} + \hat\nabla_{\nu} h_{\beta\mu} - \hat\nabla_{\beta} h_{\mu\nu}\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 8" />

Such simplifications happen at every stage, but there is no algorithm: one expands and collects terms (transcript pp. 8–9). The Riemann tensor becomes the background Riemann tensor plus a correction linear in $h$ (transcript p. 9).

To keep the lecture manageable (about a third as long, Hughes says) he assumes **vacuum**, $T_{\mu\nu} = 0$: waves far from their source. This is a simplification, not a requirement, and it does complicate some cosmological applications (transcript p. 9). Then the background is itself a vacuum solution, $\hat R_{\mu\nu} = 0$ and $\hat R = 0$, though its Riemann tensor need not vanish. The change in the Ricci tensor is (transcript pp. 9–10)

<Formula label="Perturbed Ricci tensor on a curved background" tex="\delta R_{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\hat\nabla^{\alpha}\hat\nabla_{\mu} h_{\nu\alpha} + \hat\nabla^{\alpha}\hat\nabla_{\nu} h_{\mu\alpha} - \hat\Box h_{\mu\nu} - \hat\nabla_{\mu}\hat\nabla_{\nu} h\right), \qquad \hat\Box = \hat g^{\alpha\beta}\hat\nabla_{\alpha}\hat\nabla_{\beta}, \quad h = \hat g^{\mu\nu}h_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 10" />

with $\hat\Box$ the covariant wave operator. The traces and divergences in it are the terms a gauge choice removed on flat spacetime.

## Gauge on a curved background

Repeat the infinitesimal coordinate shift $x'^{\alpha} = x^\alpha + \xi^\alpha$ (transcript pp. 10–11). Now the background metric depends on position, so it must be Taylor-expanded at the shifted point, a step Hughes warns is easy to overlook. Collecting terms, partial derivatives of the generator combine with connection coefficients into covariant derivatives, and the result looks just like the flat case with $\partial \to \hat\nabla$ (transcript pp. 11–12):

<Formula label="Generalized gauge transformation" tex="h_{\mu\nu}^{\text{new}} = h_{\mu\nu}^{\text{old}} - \hat\nabla_{\mu}\xi_\nu - \hat\nabla_{\nu}\xi_\mu" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 12" />

With the trace-reversed perturbation $\bar h_{\mu\nu} = h_{\mu\nu} - \frac12\hat g_{\mu\nu}h$ (whose trace is $-h$), the divergence can be removed by choosing $\xi$ to solve a wave equation sourced by the old divergence, the **generalized Lorenz gauge** $\hat\nabla^{\mu}\bar h_{\mu\nu} = 0$ (transcript p. 12). In vacuum the wave then obeys (transcript pp. 12–13)

<Formula label="Wave equation on a curved vacuum background" tex="\hat\Box\bar h_{\mu\nu} + 2\hat R_{\alpha\mu\beta\nu}\,\bar h^{\alpha\beta} = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 12–13" />

similar to the flat wave equation, but built from covariant derivatives and with a term that couples the wave to the curvature of the background. [Hughes says "linearizing around this flat background" at this point; he means the curved background.]

## Do gravitational waves carry energy?

Electromagnetic waves obviously carry energy: the light in a room warms the people in it and drains a power supply somewhere (transcript p. 13). For gravitational waves the question occupied the foundations of the field for decades, partly because without measurements no one could settle it by experiment (transcript pp. 13–14). The difficulty: at any event a local Lorentz frame makes the metric flat and its first derivatives zero. How can $h$ carry energy if a change of coordinates can make it vanish (transcript p. 14)?

Two observations resolve it (transcript p. 14). First, as with electromagnetism, the potentials can be made to do almost anything by a gauge choice, but the fields carry the energy; here the metric is like a potential, and the curvature, which cannot be transformed away, must carry the energy. Second, since everything looks flat at a point, the energy must be **non-local**: there is no gauge-invariant energy density of a wave at an event, only one averaged over a region larger than a wavelength but smaller than the background scales (transcript pp. 14–15).

## Second order: the wave curves the background

The electromagnetic energy flux, the Poynting vector $\vec E \times \vec B$, is quadratic in the fields, so the gravitational one should be quadratic in $h$, which needs second-order perturbation theory (transcript p. 15). Write

<Formula label="Expansion to second order" tex="g_{\mu\nu} = \hat g_{\mu\nu} + \epsilon\,h_{\mu\nu} + \epsilon^2 j_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 15" />

where $\epsilon$, actually equal to 1, only counts orders: for $h$ of order $10^{-22}$ the background terms are of order 1, the $h$ terms of order $10^{-22}$ and the $j$ terms of order $10^{-44}$ (transcript p. 15). Expanding the vacuum Einstein equation (transcript pp. 15–16):

<Formula label="The Einstein tensor order by order" tex="G_{\mu\nu}[\hat g] + \epsilon\,G^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}[h] + \epsilon^2\left(G^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}[j] + G^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}[h]\right) = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 16" />

Here $G^{(1)}$ is the linear wave operator on the curved background just found, applied to $h$ or to $j$, and $G^{(2)}[h]$ is new: a messy collection of terms quadratic in $h$ and its covariant derivatives, worked out in a paper by Richard Isaacson (transcript p. 16). Requiring the equation at each order: at order 1, the background is a vacuum solution; at order $\epsilon$, $h$ obeys the wave equation; at order $\epsilon^2$, $G^{(1)}[j] = -G^{(2)}[h]$: terms quadratic in the wave **source** the second-order field (transcript p. 16).

Now split $j$ by scale (transcript pp. 16–17): $j_{\mu\nu} = \langle j_{\mu\nu}\rangle + \delta j_{\mu\nu}$, with $\langle j\rangle$ varying only on the long scales and $\delta j$ on the short ones. The first order was defined to vary only on short scales, but nothing forces the second order to; its slowly varying part looks to any measurement like a correction to the background (transcript p. 17). Average the second-order equation. A useful fact from Isaacson's paper: the average of second derivatives equals second derivatives of the average, up to corrections of order $(\lambda/L)^2$ (transcript pp. 17–18). So the average passes through the linear operator, though not through the nonlinear one. Since $\hat g$ is a vacuum solution, the averaged equations combine into the Einstein equation for the corrected background with a source (transcript p. 18):

<Formula label="The background feels the averaged wave" tex="G_{\mu\nu}\left[\hat g + \epsilon^2\langle j\rangle\right] = -\epsilon^2\left\langle G^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}[h]\right\rangle \equiv 8\pi G\,T^{\text{GW}}_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 18" />

This defines the stress-energy of the gravitational waves, $T^{\text{GW}}_{\mu\nu} = -\frac{1}{8\pi G}\langle G^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}[h]\rangle$: as it travels, the wave acts on the background in proportion to the square of its amplitude, exactly what a stress-energy tensor should do. Working out $G^{(2)}$ for a transverse-traceless field gives a strikingly simple result, the **Isaacson stress-energy tensor** (transcript pp. 18–19):

<Formula label="Isaacson stress-energy tensor" tex="T^{\text{GW}}_{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{32\pi G}\left\langle \hat\nabla_{\mu} h^{\text{TT}}_{\alpha\beta}\;\hat\nabla_{\nu} h_{\text{TT}}^{\alpha\beta} \right\rangle" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 18–19" />

The angle brackets are essential: it is defined only through the average. Many other notions of the energy and angular momentum carried by waves exist, discussed well in Poisson and Will's *Gravity*; the Isaacson tensor is a genuine tensor, built clearly from squares of derivatives of the wave, though for some practical work it is awkward (transcript p. 19).

## The power radiated: the second quadrupole formula

In a nearly flat region far from the source, the covariant derivatives become ordinary ones and the energy density, equal to the energy flux at the speed of light, is (transcript p. 19)

<Formula label="Energy flux of the wave" tex="T^{\text{GW}}_{00} = \frac{1}{32\pi G}\left\langle \dot h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}\,\dot h^{\text{TT}}_{ij}\right\rangle, \qquad P = r^2\oint d\Omega\;T^{\text{GW}}_{00}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 19" />

Insert the wave field of Lecture 16, $h^{\text{TT}}_{ij} = \frac{2G}{r}(P_{ik}P_{jl} - \frac12 P_{ij}P_{kl})\ddot I_{kl}$, and integrate over the sphere. The projection tensors depend on direction through $n_i$, and three integrals over directions do the work (transcript pp. 19–20):

<Formula label="Integrals of direction cosines over the sphere" tex="\oint n_in_j\,d\Omega = \frac{4\pi}{3}\delta_{ij}, \qquad \oint n_i\,d\Omega = \oint n_in_jn_k\,d\Omega = 0, \qquad \oint n_in_jn_kn_l\,d\Omega = \frac{4\pi}{15}\left(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl} + \delta_{ik}\delta_{jl} + \delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 20" />

Only the quadrupole moment depends on time. The result is the **quadrupole formula** for the radiated power (transcript p. 20):

<Formula label="The quadrupole formula for the power" tex="P = \frac{G}{5}\left\langle \frac{d^3Q_{ij}}{dt^3}\,\frac{d^3Q_{ij}}{dt^3} \right\rangle, \qquad Q_{ij} = I_{ij} - \frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}I_{kk}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 20" />

with $Q_{ij}$ the trace-free quadrupole moment (the "tweak" to $I_{ij}$ of the last lecture) and $G/5c^5$ in ordinary units. Compare electromagnetism, where the leading (dipole) power is the square of the second time derivative of the dipole moment, $P = \frac23\ddot d^2$ in Gaussian units with $c = 1$ (this page's addition; the 8.07 pages write it in SI units). Gravitational radiation is quadrupolar, so one more time derivative appears (transcript p. 20).

Hughes adds the history (transcript pp. 20–21). Einstein derived a formula like this in 1916 and got it badly wrong, then nearly right in 1918, off by a factor of 2 from an algebra slip, which Hughes finds reassuring about his own mistakes. The result stayed controversial for decades, because of the conceptual problem of energy in a theory where free fall makes gravity vanish locally. Applied to two stars in orbit, the formula shows that radiation carries off energy and angular momentum, so the stars spiral together and their orbital frequency rises in a predictable way: the **chirp** that LIGO measures (transcript p. 21).

<WorkedExample title="The power of a binary black hole">
<Problem>
For two masses $m$ on a circular orbit of separation $a$ in the $xy$-plane (Lecture 16's binary), find the radiated power from the quadrupole formula. Evaluate it for $m = 30\,M_\odot$, $a = 1000$ km.
</Problem>
<Step title="Trace-free quadrupole">
From $I_{xx} = \frac{ma^2}{4}(1 + \cos 2\Omega t)$, $I_{yy} = \frac{ma^2}{4}(1 - \cos 2\Omega t)$, $I_{xy} = \frac{ma^2}{4}\sin 2\Omega t$: the time-dependent parts of $Q_{ij}$ are $\pm\frac{ma^2}{4}\cos 2\Omega t$ on the diagonal and $\frac{ma^2}{4}\sin 2\Omega t$ off it (the trace subtraction is constant).
</Step>
<Step title="Three derivatives">
$\frac{d^3Q_{xx}}{dt^3} = 2ma^2\Omega^3\sin 2\Omega t = -\frac{d^3Q_{yy}}{dt^3}$ and $\frac{d^3Q_{xy}}{dt^3} = -2ma^2\Omega^3\cos 2\Omega t$. The sum of squares over all $i, j$ (with $xy$ and $yx$) is $4m^2a^4\Omega^6(2\sin^2 + 2\cos^2) = 8m^2a^4\Omega^6$, so $P = \frac85 Gm^2a^4\Omega^6$.
</Step>
<Step title="Use Kepler's law">
With $\Omega^2 = 2Gm/a^3$: $P = \frac85 Gm^2a^4 \cdot \frac{8G^3m^3}{a^9} = \frac{64}{5}\frac{G^4m^5}{c^5a^5}$ ($c$ restored).
</Step>
<Step title="Numbers">
$Gm = 30 \times 1.327\times10^{20} = 3.98\times10^{21}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$ and $m = 5.97\times10^{31}$ kg give $P \approx 7.9\times10^{46}$ W, about $2\times10^{20}$ times the Sun's luminosity of $3.83\times10^{26}$ W. Losing energy this fast, the pair merges in about 0.37 s, from the standard leading-order result $t = \frac{5}{512}\frac{c^5a^4}{G^3m^3}$ for equal masses (worked out in a problem set, not in the lecture). At this separation the holes are only about $11\,GM/c^2$ apart ($M = 2m$, the total mass) and their relative speed is about $0.3c$, so these leading-order numbers are estimates; the last part of a real inspiral needs relativistic corrections.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology">
The same separation of scales is how gravitational waves are treated in an expanding universe: once their wavelengths are tiny compared with the Hubble length, they travel on the cosmological background, their frequencies redshift with the expansion, and their averaged energy density behaves like radiation. (The cosmological background is not a vacuum, the complication Hughes sets aside here, and primordial waves from inflation fit this picture only after they re-enter the horizon.)
</Callout>

## Summary

- On a curved background, wave and background are separated by scale: $\lambda, \tau \ll L, T$; averaging over an intermediate region (Brill and Hartle) gives $\langle g_{\mu\nu}\rangle = \hat g_{\mu\nu}$, up to errors of order $\lambda/L$.
- Linearizing about a vacuum background: $\delta\Gamma$ and $\delta R_{\mu\nu}$ take their flat forms with $\partial \to \hat\nabla$; gauge transformations become $h_{\mu\nu} \to h_{\mu\nu} - \hat\nabla_{\mu}\xi_\nu - \hat\nabla_{\nu}\xi_\mu$, and in generalized Lorenz gauge $\hat\Box\bar h_{\mu\nu} + 2\hat R_{\alpha\mu\beta\nu}\bar h^{\alpha\beta} = 0$.
- Wave energy cannot be localized at an event; it lives in the curvature and is defined by averaging.
- At second order the averaged nonlinear terms curve the background: $T^{\text{GW}}_{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{32\pi G}\langle\hat\nabla_{\mu} h^{\text{TT}}_{\alpha\beta}\hat\nabla_{\nu} h_{\text{TT}}^{\alpha\beta}\rangle$ (Isaacson).
- The radiated power is $P = \frac{G}{5c^5}\langle (d^3Q_{ij}/dt^3)^2\rangle$; for a circular binary $P = \frac{64}{5}G^4m^5/(c^5a^5)$, which drives the chirp LIGO observes.
