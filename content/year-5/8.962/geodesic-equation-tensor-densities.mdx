This lecture finishes the mathematics of tensor densities, turns it into a fast way of computing divergences, and then turns to physics: how a body moves in curved spacetime. The answer, the **geodesic equation**, is reached in two ways, from parallel transport and from maximising proper time. The signature is $(-,+,+,+)$.

## Recap: Lie derivatives and Killing vectors

Hughes recalls the last lecture (pp. 1–3). The naive derivative of a field on a curved manifold is not a tensor; a transport law, linear in the field and the displacement, with the extra demand that the metric have zero derivative, gives back the covariant derivative. Sliding vectors along a vector field instead gives the Lie derivative, a commutator for vectors; its partial derivatives may be promoted to covariant ones because the connection terms cancel. If the metric is Lie transported along $C$, then $C$ is a Killing vector and obeys Killing's equation. A metric that has a Killing vector is independent of some coordinate, and conversely; advanced books describe the symmetry as an isometry, a diffeomorphism of the spacetime that leaves the metric unchanged (pp. 2–3). (On p. 3 he says "if a vector has a Killing vector" and corrects it to "if a metric has".)

## The determinant of the metric is a tensor density

The Levi-Civita symbol $\tilde\epsilon$ transforms like a tensor apart from one factor of the Jacobian determinant: it is a tensor density of weight 1 (p. 3). Now take the metric, which is certainly a tensor, $g_{\alpha'\beta'} = \Lambda^\alpha{}_{\alpha'}\Lambda^\beta{}_{\beta'}\,g_{\alpha\beta}$, and take the determinant of both sides (pp. 3–4). Writing $g$ for the determinant of $g_{\alpha\beta}$, two factors of the Jacobian appear, and since the determinant of an inverse matrix is one over the determinant,

<Formula label="The determinant of the metric is a density of weight −2" tex="g' = \left(\det\frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial x^{\mu'}}\right)^{2} g = \left|\frac{\partial x^{\mu'}}{\partial x^\mu}\right|^{-2} g" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 3–4" />

So two "almost tensors" are available, and they can be combined into real ones (p. 4): a tensor density of weight $w$ becomes a proper tensor when multiplied by $|g|^{w/2}$. The absolute value is needed in spacetime: in an inertial frame the metric is $\mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$, so $g = -1$, and taking a square root of a negative number would go wrong; multiplying a density by $-1$ still gives a density, so using $|g|$ is allowed (pp. 4–5).

The most common use (p. 5): the Levi-Civita symbol has weight 1, so

<Formula label="The Levi-Civita tensor" tex="\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} = \sqrt{|g|}\;\tilde\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 5" />

is a tensor. With all four indices raised one gets the opposite weight, $-1$, and so one over $\sqrt{|g|}$ instead (a short homework exercise, p. 5; this page adds that in a spacetime with negative $g$ raising the indices also brings in an overall minus sign). Its main job is to build covariant volume elements (p. 5). Hughes writes the result for an orthogonal basis; in fact it holds in any coordinates:

<Formula label="Covariant four-volume element" tex="d^4V = \sqrt{|g|}\;dx^0\,dx^1\,dx^2\,dx^3" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 5" />

The same holds in any number of dimensions (pp. 5–6). In three-dimensional space in spherical coordinates (Latin indices for space only) the metric is $\mathrm{diag}(1, r^2, r^2\sin^2\theta)$ and its determinant gives the familiar $r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi$ (p. 6; a student caught a missing differential on the board).

## A party trick for contracted Christoffel symbols

Hughes names the next result after Saul Teukolsky, who liked to call such shortcuts "party tricks" (p. 6). Contract the upper index of the Christoffel symbol with a lower one, and insert the formula from Lecture 6 (p. 7):

<Formula label="Contracted Christoffel symbol" tex="\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\alpha} = \tfrac12\,g^{\mu\beta}\left(\partial_\mu g_{\beta\alpha} + \partial_\alpha g_{\beta\mu} - \partial_\beta g_{\mu\alpha}\right) = \tfrac12\,g^{\mu\beta}\,\partial_\alpha g_{\mu\beta}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 7" />

The first and third terms in the bracket are antisymmetric under exchange of $\beta$ and $\mu$ (after using the symmetry of the metric), while $g^{\mu\beta}$ is symmetric, so they cancel on contraction (p. 7). Hughes admits that after 27 years he still looks up the Christoffel formula, and recommends doing the same (p. 7). The surviving term is then (p. 8):

<Formula label="The party trick" tex="\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\alpha} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\partial_\alpha\sqrt{|g|} = \partial_\alpha\ln\sqrt{|g|}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 8" />

one derivative of a scalar function, easy to compute from the metric.

**The proof** uses a little linear algebra (pp. 8–10). For a matrix $M$ whose entries are functions, vary the logarithm of its determinant:

<Formula label="Variation of the log of a determinant" tex="\delta\ln|\det M| = \ln\left|\frac{\det(M + \delta M)}{\det M}\right| = \ln\left|\det\!\left[M^{-1}(M + \delta M)\right]\right| = \ln\left|\det\!\left(1 + M^{-1}\delta M\right)\right|" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 8" />

using that one over the determinant of $M$ is the determinant of $M^{-1}$. Here "1" is the identity matrix. For a small matrix $\epsilon$, whose entries are all much smaller than the 1s on the diagonal (p. 9),

<Formula label="Determinant of a matrix close to the identity" tex="\det\left(1 + \epsilon\right) \approx 1 + \mathrm{tr}\,\epsilon" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 9" />

the next correction being $\tfrac12[(\mathrm{tr}\,\epsilon)^2 - \mathrm{tr}(\epsilon^2)]$. (Hughes guesses that the higher coefficients depend on the dimension of the matrix; they do not, though in $n$ dimensions the terms beyond order $n$ vanish.) He says he has checked this on half a dozen examples rather than proved it; it is a standard result (p. 9). With $\epsilon = M^{-1}\delta M$ and $\ln(1 + x) \approx x$, $\delta\ln|\det M| = \mathrm{tr}(M^{-1}\delta M)$. Now let $M$ be the metric, $M^{-1}$ the inverse metric, and take the trace in the tensorial way, by contracting the two indices with the metric (p. 10). Dividing by a coordinate change and taking the limit,

<Formula label="Derivative of the log of the metric determinant" tex="\partial_\alpha\ln|g| = g^{\mu\beta}\,\partial_\alpha g_{\beta\mu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 10" />

Half of this is the contracted Christoffel symbol, and half a logarithm is the logarithm of a square root, which proves the trick (p. 10).

<WorkedExample title="The party trick in spherical coordinates">
<Problem>
For flat space in spherical coordinates, $\sqrt{g} = r^2\sin\theta$. Use the trick to find $\Gamma^\mu_{\mu r}$ and $\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\theta}$, and compare with the individual Christoffel symbols $\Gamma^\theta_{\theta r} = \Gamma^\phi_{\phi r} = 1/r$ and $\Gamma^\phi_{\phi\theta} = \cot\theta$.
</Problem>
<Step title="The trick">
$\ln\sqrt g = 2\ln r + \ln\sin\theta$, so $\Gamma^\mu_{\mu r} = \partial_r\ln\sqrt g = 2/r$ and $\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\theta} = \partial_\theta\ln\sqrt g = \cot\theta$.
</Step>
<Step title="The long way">
$\Gamma^\mu_{\mu r} = \Gamma^r_{rr} + \Gamma^\theta_{\theta r} + \Gamma^\phi_{\phi r} = 0 + 1/r + 1/r = 2/r$, and $\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\theta} = \Gamma^\phi_{\phi\theta} = \cot\theta$. The two agree, but the trick needed only one logarithmic derivative.
</Step>
<Step title="A numerical check of the identity">
For $\epsilon$ with rows $(0.01, 0.02)$ and $(0.03, -0.02)$: $\det(1 + \epsilon) = 1.01 \times 0.98 - 0.02 \times 0.03 = 0.9892$, while $1 + \mathrm{tr}\,\epsilon = 0.99$. The difference, $0.0008$, is of second order in $\epsilon$: it is exactly $-\tfrac12[(\mathrm{tr}\,\epsilon)^2 - \mathrm{tr}(\epsilon^2)] = -\tfrac12(0.0001 - 0.0017)$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Divergences and Gauss's theorem

The trick pays off for the divergence of a vector (p. 11). Writing out the covariant derivative and relabelling dummy indices,

<Formula label="Covariant divergence of a vector" tex="\nabla_\mu V^\mu = \partial_\mu V^\mu + \Gamma^\mu_{\mu\alpha}V^\alpha = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\partial_\mu\!\left(\sqrt{|g|}\,V^\mu\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 11" />

Normally one would have to compute all 40 independent Christoffel symbols of a general four-dimensional metric (four values of the upper index times ten symmetric pairs of lower ones) and sum them; here one takes a determinant, a square root and a partial derivative (p. 11). It also gives Gauss's theorem in a curved manifold (p. 12): integrating the divergence over a four-volume with the covariant volume element, the $\sqrt{|g|}$ factors cancel,

<Formula label="Gauss's theorem in curved spacetime" tex="\int \nabla_\mu V^\mu\,\sqrt{|g|}\;d^4x = \int \partial_\mu\!\left(\sqrt{|g|}\,V^\mu\right)d^4x" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 12" />

an ordinary divergence of a vector density, which the usual theorem turns into an integral over the three-dimensional surface bounding the region.

**Tensors are different** (pp. 12–13). For the divergence on the first index of a two-index tensor,

<Formula label="Divergence of a two-index tensor" tex="\nabla_\mu A^{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\,\partial_\mu\!\left(\sqrt{|g|}\,A^{\mu\nu}\right) + \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\lambda}\,A^{\mu\lambda}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 12–13" />

the correction of the first index fits the trick, but the second does not, and one is back to computing Christoffel symbols. The exception: if $A^{\mu\nu}$ is antisymmetric, the last term is a symmetric object contracted with an antisymmetric one and vanishes (p. 13).

Even when the trick works, it helps less for tensors (pp. 13–14). The divergence of a vector is a scalar, simple to compare between frames. The divergence of the stress-energy tensor is a four-vector. The equivalence principle turns special relativity's $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ into $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$, but integrating that four-vector over a volume means adding vectors that live in different tangent spaces; doing so needs a transport law, and over macroscopic regions transport depends on the path because of curvature. So $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ expresses **local** conservation of energy and momentum, which in general relativity cannot be promoted to a global conservation law (pp. 13–14).

<WorkedExample title="An inverse-square field has no divergence">
<Problem>
In flat space in spherical coordinates, take $V^r = 1/r^2$ with the other components zero. Find $\nabla_\mu V^\mu$ for $r > 0$, and compare with $V^r = r$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Inverse-square field">
$\sqrt g\,V^r = r^2\sin\theta \cdot r^{-2} = \sin\theta$, which does not depend on $r$, so $\nabla_\mu V^\mu = 0$: the field of a point charge has no sources away from the charge.
</Step>
<Step title="Linear field">
$\sqrt g\,V^r = r^3\sin\theta$, so $\nabla_\mu V^\mu = (1/(r^2\sin\theta))\cdot 3r^2\sin\theta = 3$: a uniform source, as for the field $x\,\vec e_x + y\,\vec e_y + z\,\vec e_z$ in Cartesian coordinates.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Free fall: the geodesic equation

Now physics (p. 14). Following Einstein's reading of the weak equivalence principle, go into a freely falling frame with locally Lorentz coordinates, and consider the simplest body, a **test body**: no charge, no size, no spin, nothing but mass at a point, an idealised pure monopole (p. 14). Nothing couples to it but gravity, so in that frame it moves on an inertial trajectory: a straight line with respect to its own clock (pp. 14–15). Geometrically, its tangent at one moment is parallel to its tangent a moment later, as long as both fit in one local Lorentz frame: the body **parallel transports its own tangent vector** (p. 15).

Let the path be $x^\alpha(\lambda)$, with $\lambda$ accumulating uniformly along the world line, and tangent $U^\alpha = dx^\alpha/d\lambda$ (p. 15). The parallel-transport condition, also written $\nabla_U U = 0$ or $DU/d\lambda = 0$, expands to

<Formula label="The tangent is parallel transported" tex="U^\beta\,\nabla_\beta U^\alpha = U^\beta\,\partial_\beta U^\alpha + \Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\gamma}\,U^\beta U^\gamma = \frac{dU^\alpha}{d\lambda} + \Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\gamma}\,U^\beta U^\gamma = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 15–16" />

since $U^\beta\partial_\beta$ is the total derivative along the curve. Writing $U$ out gives the second-order form Hughes boxes (p. 16):

<Formula label="The geodesic equation" tex="\frac{d^2x^\alpha}{d\lambda^2} + \Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\gamma}\,\frac{dx^\beta}{d\lambda}\,\frac{dx^\gamma}{d\lambda} = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 16" />

Its solutions are **geodesics**. They describe anything moving under gravity alone, such as orbits, and Hughes estimates that about 65% of his own papers centre on this equation for black-hole spacetimes (p. 16).

It is only the leading description (pp. 16–17). A charged body in an electromagnetic field is pushed off parallel transport: a force replaces the zero on the right-hand side. A body large enough to feel the second-order, curvature terms of its freely falling frame gets extra terms coupling its size to the curvature; a spinning body must have size, so spins couple to curvature too, which matters for binaries. Gravity is the weakest force but has only one sign, so it adds up, and the monopole coupling dominates; the corrections are small enough to add on, and adding them is what modern research does (p. 17).

<WorkedExample title="A straight line is a geodesic of the polar plane">
<Problem>
In the flat plane in polar coordinates ($\Gamma^r_{\phi\phi} = -r$, $\Gamma^\phi_{r\phi} = \Gamma^\phi_{\phi r} = 1/r$) the geodesic equations are $\ddot r - r\dot\phi^2 = 0$ and $\ddot\phi + (2/r)\dot r\dot\phi = 0$, dots meaning $d/d\lambda$. Check them for the straight line $x = \lambda$, $y = 2$ at $\lambda = 1$.
</Problem>
<Step title="The path in polar coordinates">
$r = \sqrt{\lambda^2 + 4}$ and $\phi = \arctan(2/\lambda)$. At $\lambda = 1$: $r = \sqrt5 \approx 2.236$, $\dot r = \lambda/r \approx 0.4472$, $\ddot r = 4/r^3 \approx 0.3578$; $\dot\phi = -2/(\lambda^2 + 4) = -0.4$, $\ddot\phi = 4\lambda/(\lambda^2 + 4)^2 = 0.16$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
$\ddot r - r\dot\phi^2 \approx 0.3578 - 2.236 \times 0.16 = 0.3578 - 0.3578 = 0$, and $\ddot\phi + (2/r)\dot r\dot\phi \approx 0.16 + (0.8944)(0.4472)(-0.4) = 0.16 - 0.16 = 0$. A straight line, with $\lambda$ proportional to distance, solves the geodesic equations, even though $r$ and $\phi$ change in a complicated way along it.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Affine parameters

What is $\lambda$ (pp. 17–18)? Parallel transport keeps the components fixed in the local Lorentz frame. Suppose instead the tangent kept its direction but its length was allowed to change as it slid along, by a factor $\kappa$ (p. 18):

<Formula label="A non-affinely parameterised geodesic" tex="\frac{DU^\alpha}{d\lambda^*} = \kappa(\lambda^*)\,U^\alpha" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 18" />

This looks like a more general law, but it describes the same motion with a poorly chosen parameter $\lambda^*$: a homework exercise shows that relabelling, with $\lambda$ obtained from $\lambda^*$ by an integral, brings the right-hand side back to zero (p. 18). A parameter for which the right-hand side is zero is called **affine** (p. 18). Affine parameters tick uniformly in the local Lorentz frame; for timelike paths the proper time is the natural choice (p. 19). The only changes that keep a parameter affine are linear ones,

<Formula label="Affine reparameterisations" tex="\lambda' = a\,\lambda + b, \qquad a, b\ \text{constant}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 19" />

which for proper time just move the zero of the clock or change its units (p. 19).

## Geodesics as paths of extremal aging

A second route, which Hughes left to posted notes because the course was behind schedule, connects with classical mechanics (pp. 19–22). On a globe the "straightest" path between two points is the shortest one; working such paths out on the sphere, for instance the direction to Mecca (the qibla), occupied Muslim astronomers long ago (pp. 20–21). In spacetime, take two events $p$ and $q$ and all the timelike paths between them that an observer could ride (p. 20). Most involve acceleration, and accelerated observers age less; the freely falling path is the one along which an observer ages **most**. It is a maximum rather than a minimum because of the minus sign in the time part of the interval (p. 21). Each path's proper time is

<Formula label="Proper time along a path" tex="\Delta\tau = \int_0^{\lambda_q}\sqrt{-g_{\alpha\beta}\,\frac{dx^\alpha}{d\lambda}\,\frac{dx^\beta}{d\lambda}}\;d\lambda" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 21" />

which serves as an action. Varying the path, $x^\mu \to x^\mu + \delta x^\mu$, and demanding that the action be stationary for every variation, about two-thirds of a page of algebra leads to an expression in which a Christoffel symbol (with all indices down) appears; the bracket multiplying $\delta x^\mu$ must vanish, and multiplying by the inverse metric gives the geodesic equation back (pp. 21–22). So geodesics are both the generalised straight lines of a curved spacetime and the paths of extremal aging (p. 22).

<Plot variable="v" domain={[0, 0.95]} range={[0, 11]} xLabel="speed v (c = 1)" yLabel="τ (years)"
  curves={[{ expr: "10*sqrt(1 - v^2)", label: "out and back" }]}
  points={[{ x: 0, y: 10, label: "free fall" }, { x: 0.6, y: 8, label: "v = 0.6" }]}
  caption="Proper time between two events 10 years apart at the same place in flat spacetime, for a traveller who goes out at speed v and comes back at the same speed: τ = 10√(1 − v²). It is largest, 10 years, for the unaccelerated path v = 0; at v = 0.6 it is 8 years." />

Two pieces were left for the next lecture (pp. 22–23). The analysis so far assumed timelike paths with a proper time; light moves on null paths, where proper time cannot be used and the affine parameter needs a little more thought. And combining Killing's equation with the geodesic equation gives conserved quantities, such as energy in a time-independent spacetime.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Geodesics in the expanding universe">
In 8.286 (Lectures 13 and 14) Guth derives the same geodesic equation by varying the length of a path. Applied to the Robertson–Walker metric, its Christoffel symbols with a time index make the momentum of a freely moving massive particle fall off as one over the scale factor, the counterpart for matter of the cosmological redshift of light.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The metric determinant is a tensor density of weight $-2$; multiplying a density of weight $w$ by $|g|^{w/2}$ gives a tensor. So $\sqrt{|g|}\,\tilde\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}$ is a tensor and $\sqrt{|g|}\,d^4x$ the covariant volume element.
- From $\det(1 + \epsilon) \approx 1 + \mathrm{tr}\,\epsilon$: $\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\alpha} = \partial_\alpha\ln\sqrt{|g|}$, hence $\nabla_\mu V^\mu = (1/\sqrt{|g|})\,\partial_\mu(\sqrt{|g|}V^\mu)$ and Gauss's theorem in curved spacetime. For tensors it works only when antisymmetric; $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ is local, not global, conservation.
- A test body parallel transports its four-velocity: $d^2x^\alpha/d\lambda^2 + \Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\gamma}(dx^\beta/d\lambda)(dx^\gamma/d\lambda) = 0$, the geodesic equation. Forces, size and spin add corrections.
- The parameter must be affine (uniform in the local Lorentz frame, e.g. proper time); only $\lambda' = a\lambda + b$ keeps it so.
- Geodesics also maximise the proper time between two events.
