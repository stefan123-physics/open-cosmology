This lecture was given as MIT's teaching was about to be disrupted (in spring 2020, by the COVID-19 pandemic, which the next lecture names); Hughes opens with a few words about looking after one another. It does three things. It squeezes more out of the geodesic equation: a form that works for light, conserved quantities, and the Newtonian limit. Then it makes "curvature" precise by carrying a vector round a closed loop, which produces the **Riemann curvature tensor**. The signature is $(-,+,+,+)$ and $c = 1$.

## The geodesic equation in momentum form

Geodesics parallel transport their own tangent, and they are the paths of free fall, motion under gravity alone (pp. 1–2). Hughes revises his earlier estimate: after checking, about 75% of his papers, not 65%, rest on solving this equation (p. 2). For a body of rest mass $m$ on a timelike path, multiply the equation by $m$ twice to replace the four-velocity by the four-momentum $p^\alpha = m\,u^\alpha$ (p. 2). This amounts to an affine parameter with $\Delta\lambda = \Delta\tau/m$, allowed because affine parameters can be rescaled by a constant (p. 3):

<Formula label="Geodesic equation in momentum form" tex="p^\alpha = \frac{dx^\alpha}{d\lambda}, \qquad p^\beta\,\nabla_\beta\,p^\alpha = 0, \qquad \frac{dp^\alpha}{d\lambda} + \Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\gamma}\,p^\beta p^\gamma = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 2–3" />

Now let $m \to 0$ while $\Delta\tau \to 0$ in such a way that $\Delta\tau/m$ stays fixed (p. 3). Nothing in the equation misbehaves, so it also describes light, for which proper time is not defined; one simply picks the parameter along the null world line that makes the tangent equal to the momentum, with $p^\alpha p_\alpha = -m^2 = 0$ (pp. 3–4). Many of the most important tests of general relativity concern light moving through curved spacetime (p. 3).

## Conserved components of the momentum

Lower the free index (p. 4). That looks like index gymnastics, but is it allowed? It amounts to multiplying by $g_{\beta\gamma}$, and since the covariant derivative of the metric is zero, the metric can be moved inside the derivative freely; Hughes stresses that this trick will be used constantly. Expanding $p^\alpha\nabla_\alpha p_\beta = 0$ (the lower index enters its Christoffel term with a minus sign), using $p^\alpha\partial_\alpha = m\,d/d\tau$, and writing the Christoffel symbol with all indices down (pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="The geodesic equation with the index down" tex="m\,\frac{dp_\beta}{d\tau} = \Gamma_{\gamma\beta\alpha}\,p^\alpha p^\gamma = \tfrac12\left(\partial_\beta g_{\gamma\alpha} + \partial_\alpha g_{\gamma\beta} - \partial_\gamma g_{\beta\alpha}\right)p^\alpha p^\gamma = \tfrac12\,\partial_\beta g_{\alpha\gamma}\,p^\alpha p^\gamma" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 5" />

The product $p^\alpha p^\gamma$ is symmetric in $\alpha$ and $\gamma$, while the last two terms in the bracket swap into each other with a minus sign, so they cancel; only one derivative of the metric survives (p. 5). The consequence is powerful: **if the metric does not depend on some coordinate $x^\beta$, the lower component $p_\beta$ is a constant of the motion** (p. 5). For a complicated spacetime, noticing that it is independent of time or of an angle immediately gives a conserved $p_t$ or $p_\phi$ (p. 6).

The same comes from Killing vectors, without choosing coordinates (pp. 6–7). If the metric is independent of a coordinate there is a Killing vector $C$; follow the scalar $C^\beta p_\beta$ along the geodesic (with a factor $m$ inserted for symmetry):

<Formula label="A Killing vector gives a conserved quantity" tex="m\,\frac{d}{d\tau}\left(C^\beta p_\beta\right) = p^\alpha\nabla_\alpha\left(C^\beta p_\beta\right) = C^\beta\,p^\alpha\nabla_\alpha p_\beta + p^\alpha p^\beta\,\nabla_\alpha C_\beta = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 6–7" />

The first term vanishes because $p$ obeys the geodesic equation. For the second, split any two-index object into symmetric and antisymmetric parts (p. 7),

<Formula label="Symmetric plus antisymmetric parts" tex="M_{\alpha\beta} = M_{(\alpha\beta)} + M_{[\alpha\beta]}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 7" />

The antisymmetric part of $\nabla_\alpha C_\beta$ dies against the symmetric $p^\alpha p^\beta$, and the symmetric part is zero by Killing's equation (p. 7). So $C^\beta p_\beta$ is conserved. The two results complement each other: the statement about $p_\beta$ depends on having chosen coordinates in which the metric does not depend on $x^\beta$; the Killing statement holds in any representation (p. 7).

If the metric is independent of time there is a timelike Killing vector and $p_t$ is conserved; $-p_t$ is called the **energy** (p. 8). The minus sign is natural in spacetimes that are **asymptotically flat**, looking like special relativity far from the source, where lowering the time index of the momentum gives minus the energy; but $p_t$ is conserved even right next to a rapidly rotating black hole (p. 8). An angle-independent metric likewise gives a conserved angular momentum (p. 8).

## The Newtonian limit

An example, using a spacetime to be derived later (pp. 8–9):

<Formula label="A weak, static metric" tex="ds^2 = -(1 + 2\Phi)\,dt^2 + (1 - 2\Phi)\left(dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 8" />

with $\Phi(x, y, z)$ small, so that terms of order $\Phi^2$ can be dropped, and independent of time. Consider slow motion: the energy is close to the mass and much larger than the spatial momentum, $p^0 \approx m \gg |p^i|$ (p. 9). In the geodesic equation the time–time term then dominates, since the other terms are smaller by factors of $v/c$ (p. 9):

<Formula label="Slow-motion geodesic equation" tex="m\,\frac{dp^\beta}{d\tau} = -\Gamma^\beta_{00}\,\left(p^0\right)^2 \approx -\Gamma^\beta_{00}\,m^2" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 9" />

The $\beta = 0$ part says nothing new: the metric is static, so energy is conserved (p. 9). For a spatial index only a spatial gradient of $g_{00} = -(1 + 2\Phi)$ survives (p. 10):

<Formula label="The relevant Christoffel symbol" tex="\Gamma^i_{00} = -\tfrac12\,g^{ij}\,\partial_j g_{00} = \frac{\partial_i\Phi}{1 - 2\Phi} \approx \partial_i\Phi" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 10" />

and the equation of motion becomes (pp. 10–11)

<Formula label="Newton's law from the geodesic equation" tex="\frac{dp^i}{d\tau} = -m\,\partial_i\Phi" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 10–11" />

Ignoring the difference between proper time and coordinate time, this is $d\vec p/dt = -m\nabla\Phi$: the motion in a potential $\Phi$ (p. 10). (On the board Hughes first lost a factor of $m$; a student pointed out the $m$ and $m^2$, p. 11.) Right after the spring break the field equations will show that $\Phi$ is the Newtonian gravitational potential (p. 11). Hughes previews the two derivations to come: Einstein's own, which finds tensors with the right symmetries and then fixes an overall constant by demanding the Newtonian limit, and a more sophisticated one (pp. 11–12). He recalls seeing Einstein's working papers in the archive in Jerusalem, using a spacetime like this one for the perihelion of Mercury, full of crossings-out and "Nein nein nein!" in the margin (p. 12).

<WorkedExample title="How small is Φ at the Earth's surface?">
<Problem>
With $c$ restored, the Newtonian potential at the Earth's surface is $\Phi = -GM_\oplus/(R_\oplus c^2)$. Using $GM_\oplus = 3.986\times 10^{14}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$ and $R_\oplus = 6.371\times 10^6$ m, check that terms of order $\Phi^2$ can be dropped, and recover $g$ from the gradient.
</Problem>
<Step title="The size of Φ">
$GM_\oplus/(R_\oplus c^2) = 3.986\times 10^{14}/(6.371\times 10^6 \times 8.988\times 10^{16}) \approx 6.96\times 10^{-10}$. Its square, about $5\times 10^{-19}$, is utterly negligible.
</Step>
<Step title="The acceleration">
$-c^2\,\partial_r\Phi = -GM_\oplus/R_\oplus^2 \approx -9.82\ \text{m/s}^2$: the familiar pull towards the centre.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Curvature from parallel transport round a loop

"Curvature" has been used loosely so far: the way initially parallel trajectories stop being parallel (p. 12). To make it precise, carry a vector by parallel transport round a closed figure (p. 12). On the flat blackboard, take a triangle and a vector pointing from one corner to the next; transported all the way round, it comes back pointing exactly as before, and the triangle's angles add up to 180° (p. 12).

Now a triangle on a sphere (p. 13). Start at the North Pole, A, and walk as straight as the surface allows (a straight line for a two-dimensional being on the sphere; Hughes says "one-dimensional" here, a slip) down to the equator, meeting it at a right angle at B. Walk along the equator to C, then straight north back to the pole. All three angles are right angles. Carry a vector that starts at the pole pointing south: it keeps pointing south down to the equator, stays pointing the same way along the equator, and comes back up to the pole turned by 90° (p. 13). For a surface of constant curvature, Hughes says, the vector turns by the angle sum minus 180° (pp. 13–14; by the Gauss–Bonnet theorem this holds for a geodesic triangle on any surface, the turning angle being the curvature integrated over the triangle); stretching the equatorial leg makes the rotation as large as one likes. This operation is called **holonomy** (p. 14).

<WorkedExample title="Holonomy on a sphere">
<Problem>
A geodesic triangle on a sphere of radius $a$ has its apex at the North Pole with angle 60°, and its other two corners on the equator. By how much does a vector carried round it turn? Check against the triangle's area.
</Problem>
<Step title="Angle excess">
The angles are 60°, 90° and 90°, which add to 240°: an excess of 60°, so the vector turns by 60°.
</Step>
<Step title="Area">
The triangle is $60/360 = 1/6$ of the northern hemisphere, whose area is $2\pi a^2$, so its area is $\pi a^2/3$. Divided by $a^2$ that is $\pi/3$ radians, which is 60°: on a sphere the turning angle is the area divided by the radius squared, so it measures the curvature enclosed.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The Riemann tensor

Now the calculation (pp. 14–19). Take a small coordinate parallelogram with corners A, B, C, D (Hughes sketches it, using one figure for all the steps): along one pair of sides a coordinate $x^\lambda$ is constant, at $x^\lambda$ and $x^\lambda + \delta x^\lambda$; along the other pair $x^\sigma$ is constant, at $x^\sigma$ and $x^\sigma + \delta x^\sigma$ (p. 14). Carry a vector $V^\alpha$ from A to B (increasing $x^\sigma$), B to C (increasing $x^\lambda$), C to D and back to A. On the first leg the transport condition is $\nabla_\sigma V^\alpha = 0$, which integrates to (p. 15)

<Formula label="Transport along the first side" tex="\partial_\sigma V^\alpha = -\Gamma^\alpha_{\sigma\mu}V^\mu, \qquad V^\alpha(B) = V^\alpha(A) - \int_{1}\Gamma^\alpha_{\sigma\mu}V^\mu\,dx^\sigma" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 15" />

and similarly for the other three sides, with the sign of the integral flipped on the two sides traversed backwards (pp. 15–16). The change $\delta V^\alpha = V^\alpha_{\text{final}} - V^\alpha(A)$ is a sum of four integrals, which Hughes groups in pairs along opposite, slightly offset sides (p. 16). Each pair is the same integrand evaluated on two parallel paths a small distance apart, so to first order it is one integral of a derivative (pp. 16–17). For small sides the integrals are just products of the side lengths, leaving terms with derivatives of $\Gamma$ times $V$ and terms with $\Gamma$ times derivatives of $V$ (p. 17). Since $V$ is being parallel transported, its derivatives are themselves $-\Gamma V$, which turns the second kind into products of two Christoffel symbols (p. 18). (Hughes enlivens the slog by quoting the "The end is near" line from a review article by his future PhD supervisor, p. 18.) Those products are where the **nonlinearity** of general relativity comes from: derivatives of the metric multiplied by derivatives of the metric (p. 18). Relabelling dummy indices, the result is linear in the vector and in both sides (p. 19). Carried out with the orientation above (first along $x^\sigma$, then along $x^\lambda$), it reads

<Formula label="Change of a vector carried round a small loop" tex="\delta V^\alpha = -R^\alpha{}_{\mu\sigma\lambda}\,V^\mu\,\delta x^\sigma\,\delta x^\lambda, \qquad R^\alpha{}_{\mu\sigma\lambda} = \partial_\sigma\Gamma^\alpha_{\lambda\mu} - \partial_\lambda\Gamma^\alpha_{\sigma\mu} + \Gamma^\alpha_{\sigma\nu}\Gamma^\nu_{\lambda\mu} - \Gamma^\alpha_{\lambda\nu}\Gamma^\nu_{\sigma\mu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 18–19" />

(The overall sign depends on the direction round the loop and on the index convention; the one written here is this page's own working, in the convention of Carroll's textbook used by the course.) The four-index object is the **Riemann curvature tensor** (p. 19). Although the Christoffel symbols are not tensors, their non-tensorial pieces cancel in this combination, so $R$ is a true tensor.

An equivalent definition, the one in Carroll, uses the commutator of covariant derivatives (Hughes says "partial derivatives" here, a slip); the loop calculation is its geometric meaning (p. 19):

<Formula label="Riemann tensor from commutators of covariant derivatives" tex="\left[\nabla_\mu, \nabla_\nu\right]V^\rho = R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}\,V^\sigma, \qquad \left[\nabla_\mu, \nabla_\nu\right]p_\sigma = -R^\lambda{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}\,p_\lambda" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 19" />

For a one-form the sign is minus. Hughes warns that the first edition of Schutz's textbook has this sign wrong (around p. 171), backed by an intuitive argument that is also wrong, a reminder that sometimes one just has to calculate (p. 19).

<WorkedExample title="The Riemann tensor of a sphere">
<Problem>
On a sphere of radius $a$, $ds^2 = a^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)$, the non-zero Christoffel symbols are $\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi} = -\sin\theta\cos\theta$ and $\Gamma^\phi_{\theta\phi} = \Gamma^\phi_{\phi\theta} = \cot\theta$. Find $R^\theta{}_{\phi\theta\phi}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Write out the definition">
$R^\theta{}_{\phi\theta\phi} = \partial_\theta\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi} - \partial_\phi\Gamma^\theta_{\theta\phi} + \Gamma^\theta_{\theta\nu}\Gamma^\nu_{\phi\phi} - \Gamma^\theta_{\phi\nu}\Gamma^\nu_{\theta\phi}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Evaluate">
$\partial_\theta(-\sin\theta\cos\theta) = \sin^2\theta - \cos^2\theta$. The second term is zero (nothing depends on $\phi$), and so is the third ($\Gamma^\theta_{\theta\nu} = 0$). The last is $-\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi}\Gamma^\phi_{\theta\phi} = \sin\theta\cos\theta\cot\theta = \cos^2\theta$. In total $R^\theta{}_{\phi\theta\phi} = \sin^2\theta$.
</Step>
<Step title="Interpret">
It is not zero, so the sphere is curved, while a plane (where all the Christoffel symbols can be made to vanish everywhere) gives zero. In two dimensions this one component fixes the whole tensor; lowering the index, $R_{\theta\phi\theta\phi} = a^2\sin^2\theta$, and dividing by the determinant of the metric, $a^4\sin^2\theta$, leaves $1/a^2$, the same at every point: the single number that measures the curvature of a sphere.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## What the Riemann tensor does, and how many components it has

Riemann also tells how bodies with structure feel spacetime (pp. 19–20). Geodesics describe pure monopoles; a body with size or any multipole structure fills part of its local Lorentz frame, couples to the curvature and is pushed off the geodesic, and the Riemann tensor describes that coupling. It is the relativistic description of tides: in Newtonian theory the precession of the equinoxes comes from the tides of the Sun and Moon on the Earth's bulge (Hughes says "the Earth and the moon"), and in general relativity the Riemann tensor carries the same information (p. 20).

A four-index object in four dimensions seems to have $4^4 = 256$ components (p. 20). Its symmetries, to be worked through next time, reduce the count in $n$ dimensions from $n^4$ to (p. 20)

<Formula label="Independent components of the Riemann tensor" tex="\frac{n^2\left(n^2 - 1\right)}{12}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 20–21" />

For $n = 1$ this is 0: in one dimension there is no loop to carry a vector round, so no holonomy and no curvature; a pedant can insist that a line cannot be curved (p. 20). For $n = 2$ it is 1: a single number at each point, the radius of curvature of the sphere that best fits the surface there (p. 21). For $n = 4$ it is $16 \times 15/12 = 20$, exactly the number of second derivatives of the metric that could not be removed when building a local Lorentz frame (p. 21).

What comes next (pp. 21–22): the symmetries behind that count; contractions of Riemann with the metric to get two-index curvature tensors, needed because the source of gravity, the stress-energy tensor, has two indices; and a combination of derivatives of the curvature that vanishes, giving a divergence-free curvature tensor. The stress-energy tensor is divergence-free too, and asking whether the two are related is, in a nutshell, how Einstein found general relativity (p. 22).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Curvature in cosmology">
The spatial sections of a Robertson–Walker universe are spaces of constant curvature, like the sphere here: a closed universe is a three-sphere whose curvature is set by $k/a^2$. A triangle of light rays in a closed universe has angles adding to more than 180°, in an open one to less; this is the idea behind measuring the geometry of the universe from the apparent angular size of objects of known size, such as the spots in the cosmic microwave background.
</Callout>

## Summary

- With momentum as tangent, $p^\beta\nabla_\beta p^\alpha = 0$ also covers light ($m \to 0$, $p^\alpha p_\alpha = 0$).
- $m\,dp_\beta/d\tau = \tfrac12\partial_\beta g_{\alpha\gamma}p^\alpha p^\gamma$: a coordinate the metric does not depend on gives a conserved $p_\beta$; any Killing vector gives a conserved $C^\beta p_\beta$; $-p_t$ is the energy.
- For $g_{00} = -(1 + 2\Phi)$ and slow motion, $\Gamma^i_{00} \approx \partial_i\Phi$ and $dp^i/d\tau = -m\,\partial_i\Phi$: Newton's law.
- Parallel transport round a loop (holonomy) measures curvature: on a sphere a vector turns by the triangle's angle excess.
- Round a small parallelogram, $\delta V$ is linear in $V$ and in both sides, with coefficient the Riemann tensor $R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\Gamma^\rho_{\nu\sigma} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma} + \Gamma^\rho_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\sigma} - \Gamma^\rho_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\sigma}$; equivalently $[\nabla_\mu, \nabla_\nu]V^\rho = R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}V^\sigma$. It has $n^2(n^2-1)/12$ independent components: 1 in two dimensions, 20 in four.
