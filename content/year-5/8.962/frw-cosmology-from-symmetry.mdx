Up to Lecture 17, 8.962 solved Einstein's equation by expanding around a known background, mostly flat spacetime. Lecture 18 starts a new kind of solution: one built from **symmetry**. No field is assumed to be weak. Instead Hughes asks what spacetime must look like if, on very large scales, it obeys certain symmetries, and finds that the symmetry reduces the full nonlinear field equations to two ordinary differential equations for one function of time. The result is the metric of the universe.

Readers of 8.286 have met these results before: Guth reached the [Friedmann equation](/courses/8-286/friedmann-equation) from a Newtonian expanding sphere and built the [Robertson–Walker metric](/courses/8-286/robertson-walker-spacetime) geometrically, and the 8.902 page [The Friedmann equations from general relativity](/courses/8-902/friedmann-equations-density-parameters) quotes the field-equation result. This page follows Hughes's own route, which uses the tools of the earlier 8.962 lectures (Killing vectors, the Riemann and Ricci tensors, the perfect fluid). This page uses $c = 1$ and the signature $(-,+,+,+)$, as Hughes does.

## Maximally symmetric spaces

The background comes from section 3.9 of Carroll's textbook, which Hughes asks the class to read. A **maximally symmetric space** is one with the largest possible number of Killing vectors (the directions along which the Lie derivative of the metric vanishes, so that flowing along them leaves the geometry unchanged). In $n$ dimensions that number is

<Formula label="Killing vectors of a maximally symmetric space" tex="N_\text{Killing} = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 2" />

Such a space is as homogeneous as possible (the same at every point) and as isotropic as possible (the same in every direction). Two examples:

- Three-dimensional Euclidean space: $3 \times 4/2 = 6$ Killing vectors, the 3 rotations and 3 translations.
- Four-dimensional Minkowski spacetime: $4 \times 5/2 = 10$: 3 rotations, 3 boosts and 4 translations (three in space, one in time). [Hughes says "3 translations and 4 boosts"; there are 3 boosts and 4 translations.]

Maximal symmetry means that, viewed from a local Lorentz frame, the curvature must look the same under every rotation and boost. In both examples the Riemann tensor vanishes, which is trivially invariant. In general, Carroll shows, the Riemann tensor is forced into one simple form (transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Riemann tensor of a maximally symmetric space" tex="R_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu} = \frac{R}{n(n-1)}\left(g_{\alpha\mu}\,g_{\beta\nu} - g_{\alpha\nu}\,g_{\beta\mu}\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3" />

Here $R$ is the Ricci scalar. Contracting on $\alpha$ and $\mu$ gives a factor $n$ from the trace of the metric in the first term, while the second term just returns the metric:

<Formula label="Its Ricci tensor" tex="R_{\beta\nu} = \frac{R}{n(n-1)}\left(n\,g_{\beta\nu} - g_{\beta\nu}\right) = \frac{R}{n}\,g_{\beta\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3" />

and one more trace confirms that the $R$ in front is the Ricci scalar. So the Einstein tensor of a maximally symmetric spacetime is proportional to the metric. Hughes notes that the only solutions of the field equations with $G_{\mu\nu}$ proportional to $g_{\mu\nu}$ are empty flat spacetime and a spacetime filled with a cosmological constant. Neither is our universe.

## Symmetric in space, not in time

Observation says the universe is homogeneous and isotropic on large **spatial** scales. Spacetime as a whole is not: looking far away means looking into the past, and the past universe was much denser. So Hughes builds a spacetime whose spatial slices are maximally symmetric while time is allowed to change things.

How large is "large"? The cosmic microwave background, emitted roughly 13.7 billion years ago in Hughes's rough figure, is uniform to about one part in 100,000, with a great deal of physics in that small deviation. Nearer and later, matter clumps into galaxy clusters, and homogeneity fails on scales of several tens of megaparsecs (a parsec is about 3.26 light years). Cosmology therefore averages over anything smaller: a cluster of a few hundred galaxies is treated as a point.

## The metric in comoving coordinates

Hughes chooses the line element

<Formula label="The cosmological line element" tex="ds^2 = -dt^2 + R^2(t)\,\gamma_{ij}\,dx^i\,dx^j" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 5" />

where $R(t)$ is the **scale factor**. (It is the same letter as the Ricci scalar, an unfortunate clash; context tells them apart.) Setting $g_{tt} = -1$ and $g_{ti} = 0$ fixes four of the ten metric functions, much as choosing a gauge did in linearised theory. These are **comoving coordinates**: an observer at rest in them, with four-velocity $U^\mu = (1, 0, 0, 0)$, simply rides along with whatever the spacetime does.

The Earth is not comoving. It spins, orbits the Sun, which orbits the Galaxy, which falls towards the Virgo Cluster. Cosmological measurements must correct for this, and the clearest sign of it is the **dipole anisotropy** of the microwave background: a Doppler shift from our motion relative to the comoving frame (transcript pp. 5–6).

The spatial metric $\gamma_{ij}$ is taken to be maximally symmetric. The coordinates $x^i$ are dimensionless; every length is carried by $R(t)$. In three dimensions the Riemann tensor of $\gamma_{ij}$ then has the form above with a constant $k$ (not the index $k$):

<Formula label="Curvature of each spatial slice" tex="{}^{3}\!R_{ijkl} = k\left(\gamma_{ik}\gamma_{jl} - \gamma_{il}\gamma_{jk}\right), \qquad {}^{3}\!R_{jl} = 2k\,\gamma_{jl}, \qquad {}^{3}\!R = 6k" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 6" />

so $k$ is simply related to the scalar curvature of a slice of constant time.

## Solving for the spatial metric

An isotropic three-dimensional space looks the same in every direction, so it is spherically symmetric about any point. With a dimensionless radius $\bar r$, the spatial metric must be

<Formula label="Spherically symmetric spatial metric" tex="\gamma_{ij}\,dx^i\,dx^j = e^{2\beta(\bar r)}\,d\bar r^2 + \bar r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 7" />

where writing the unknown function of $\bar r$ as an exponential defines $\beta$. Now compute the curvature twice:

- From the metric, by the usual recipe (Christoffel symbols, then Riemann, then Ricci), ignoring maximal symmetry, the radial component is $R_{\bar r\bar r} = (2/\bar r)\,d\beta/d\bar r$.
- From maximal symmetry, ${}^{3}\!R_{\bar r\bar r} = 2k\,\gamma_{\bar r\bar r} = 2k\,e^{2\beta}$.

Setting the two equal:

<Formula label="Equating the two Ricci components" tex="\frac{2}{\bar r}\,\frac{d\beta}{d\bar r} = 2k\,e^{2\beta} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d}{d\bar r}\left(e^{-2\beta}\right) = -2k\,\bar r" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 7" />

With the boundary condition $\beta = 0$ at $\bar r = 0$ (space looks flat near the origin), integration gives $e^{-2\beta} = 1 - k\bar r^2$, and so

<Formula label="Robertson–Walker line element" tex="ds^2 = -dt^2 + R^2(t)\left[\frac{d\bar r^2}{1 - k\bar r^2} + \bar r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right]" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 7" />

This is the **Robertson–Walker** spacetime, written down, Hughes says, in the 1920s (Robertson 1929; Walker's papers followed in the 1930s) on just this kind of symmetry argument (Hughes names it on transcript p. 12, apologising for not doing so earlier). Geometry alone cannot say what $k$ and $R(t)$ are; that needs a source.

## Only the sign of k matters

One thing can be said already. Rescale all three quantities by a positive number $\alpha$:

<Formula label="A rescaling that leaves the metric unchanged" tex="k \to \frac{k}{\alpha}, \qquad \bar r \to \sqrt{\alpha}\,\bar r, \qquad R \to \frac{R}{\sqrt{\alpha}}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 8" />

Then $k\bar r^2$, $R\,d\bar r$ and $R\,\bar r$ are all unchanged, and so is the line element. [In the transcript $k$ is multiplied by $\alpha$; with the other two rescalings the metric stays the same only if $k$ is divided by it.] The size of $k$ can therefore be absorbed into the scale factor, and only three cases are distinct: $k = -1$, $0$ or $+1$.

## Three spatial geometries

A new radial coordinate $\chi$ makes the three cases explicit:

<Formula label="The radial coordinate χ" tex="d\chi = \frac{d\bar r}{\sqrt{1 - k\bar r^2}}, \qquad \bar r = \sin\chi\ (k = +1), \quad \bar r = \chi\ (k = 0), \quad \bar r = \sinh\chi\ (k = -1)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 9" />

- **$k = +1$, closed:** $ds^2 = -dt^2 + R^2(t)\left[d\chi^2 + \sin^2\chi\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right]$. Each spatial slice is a **3-sphere**, the set of points at unit distance from a centre in four-dimensional space. Distances between points grow until $\chi = \pi/2$ and then shrink again; at $\chi = \pi$ you are back where you started. Each slice therefore has a finite total volume, even though it has no edge.
- **$k = 0$, flat:** $ds^2 = -dt^2 + R^2(t)\left[d\chi^2 + \chi^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right]$. Each slice is Euclidean space. Hughes warns that "flat" refers only to the spatial slices in comoving coordinates: the spacetime is curved. Current evidence favours this case.
- **$k = -1$, open:** $ds^2 = -dt^2 + R^2(t)\left[d\chi^2 + \sinh^2\chi\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right]$. Each slice is hyperbolic and, like the flat one, infinite, but the area of a sphere of radius $\chi$ grows exponentially rather than as $\chi^2$.

(Transcript pp. 9–10; the 8.286 pages on [the 3-sphere](/courses/8-286/sphere-metrics) and [the open universe](/courses/8-286/open-universe-geometry) explore these geometries at length.)

## The dimensionless scale factor

A second notation is common, and Hughes uses both. Choose a reference time, usually today, call the scale factor then $R_0$, and define

<Formula label="Normalised scale factor and curvature" tex="a(t) = \frac{R(t)}{R_0}, \qquad r = R_0\,\bar r, \qquad \kappa = \frac{k}{R_0^2}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 10–11" />

so that $a = 1$ today and the coordinate $r$ carries dimensions of length. The line element becomes

<Formula label="Robertson–Walker line element with a(t)" tex="ds^2 = -dt^2 + a^2(t)\left[\frac{dr^2}{1 - \kappa r^2} + r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right]" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 11" />

The price is that $\kappa$ is no longer restricted to three values; only its sign tells $k$. The gain is that $a$ compares every length at any epoch with its size today.

## The source: a perfect fluid

To connect geometry to physics, Hughes takes the default source of general relativity, a **perfect fluid**. At rest in comoving coordinates it respects homogeneity and isotropy automatically:

<Formula label="Perfect fluid at rest in comoving coordinates" tex="T_{\mu\nu} = (\rho + P)\,U_\mu U_\nu + P\,g_{\mu\nu}, \qquad T^{\mu}{}_{\nu} = \mathrm{diag}\left(-\rho,\ P,\ P,\ P\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 11" />

Before solving the field equations, check local energy conservation, the time component of $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu}{}_{\nu} = 0$. In the Robertson–Walker metric it becomes remarkably simple:

<Formula label="Local energy conservation" tex="\dot\rho = -3\,\frac{\dot a}{a}\left(\rho + P\right) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dt}\left(\rho R^3\right) = -P\,\frac{d}{dt}\left(R^3\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 12" />

Up to a number, $R^3$ is the volume of a piece of a spatial slice, so the second form says: the change of energy in a comoving volume equals minus the pressure times the change of volume, $dU = -P\,dV$. It is the first law of thermodynamics.

## The Friedmann equations

Rather than $G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}$ itself, Hughes uses the equivalent trace-reversed form, in which $T = T^{\mu}{}_{\mu} = -\rho + 3P$ is the trace of the stress-energy tensor:

<Formula label="Field equation in trace-reversed form" tex="R_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\left(T_{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{2}\,T\,g_{\mu\nu}\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 12–13" />

For the metric with $a(t)$, the Ricci tensor has $R_{tt} = -3\ddot a/a$ and $R_{ij} = \left(a\ddot a + 2\dot a^2 + 2\kappa\right)\tilde\gamma_{ij}$, where $\tilde\gamma_{ij}$ is the spatial metric in the square bracket. (Hughes leaves this "little bit of work" to the class; we state the components so the steps can be followed.) The $tt$ component gives the acceleration; a spatial component, combined with the $tt$ one to remove $\ddot a$, gives the expansion rate:

<Formula label="The Friedmann equations" tex="\text{F1:}\ \ \left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\,\rho - \frac{\kappa}{a^2}, \qquad \text{F2:}\ \ \frac{\ddot a}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left(\rho + 3P\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 13" />

These are the **Friedmann equations**, which Hughes labels F1 and F2; solutions of them give the Friedmann–Robertson–Walker (FRW) metrics. Robertson–Walker is the geometry; Friedmann's equations tie it to the source.

F1 is Guth's Friedmann equation, now with $\kappa$ coming out of the field equations rather than as an energy constant of a Newtonian sphere; F2 adds that **pressure gravitates** through $\rho + 3P$. Compare the 8.286 pages on [the Newtonian derivation](/courses/8-286/friedmann-equation) and [radiation and pressure](/courses/8-286/radiation-dominated-universe).

## Hubble parameter and critical density

The ratio $H = \dot a/a$ is the **Hubble parameter**: it says how fast the length scale of the spatial slices is changing. Its value today is $H_0$. The scanned course notes, written some eleven or twelve years before the 2020 lecture, give $73 \pm 3$ km/s/Mpc; Hughes points out that this is out of date in an interesting way. Local measurements cluster around 72 to 73 in these units but disagree with other ways of inferring $H_0$ (the "Hubble tension", under active debate in 2020; as a later development, it has persisted in the measurements since then). $H$ has units of inverse time; astronomers quote it as a velocity per distance because comoving objects in an expanding universe recede from us.

Setting $\kappa = 0$ in F1 and solving for $\rho$ defines the **critical density**, and every density is measured against it:

<Formula label="Critical density and density parameter" tex="\rho_\text{crit} = \frac{3H^2}{8\pi G}, \qquad \Omega = \frac{\rho}{\rho_\text{crit}}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 14" />

Since $H$ can be measured and the rest are constants, $\rho_\text{crit}$ is measurable. F1 then reads

<Formula label="Density decides the geometry" tex="\Omega - 1 = \frac{\kappa}{H^2a^2}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 14" />

and since $H^2a^2 > 0$:

- $\Omega < 1$: $\kappa < 0$, $k = -1$, an open universe;
- $\Omega = 1$: $\kappa = 0$, a spatially flat universe;
- $\Omega > 1$: $\kappa > 0$, $k = +1$, a closed universe. [The transcript says "kappa is greater than 1" here; it is the sign, $\kappa > 0$, that matters.]

Measuring whether the universe holds more, exactly as much, or less than the critical density tells us whether space is finite, "simply infinite" or, in Hughes's phrase, ridiculously infinite.

A piece of notation seen in the literature puts curvature on the same footing as the real densities. Define a **curvature density** and its parameter,

<Formula label="Curvature density" tex="\rho_\text{curv} = -\frac{3\kappa}{8\pi G\,a^2}, \qquad \Omega_\text{curv} = \frac{\rho_\text{curv}}{\rho_\text{crit}}, \qquad \Omega + \Omega_\text{curv} = 1" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 15" />

It is not a real density, only a useful bookkeeping device. [The transcript describes $\rho_\text{curv}$ in words, as $\kappa$, $G$ and $a$ combined to have the dimensions of a density; the explicit form is ours, fixed by $\Omega + \Omega_\text{curv} = 1$.]

<WorkedExample title="How big could a closed universe be?">
<Problem>
Suppose measurements gave $\Omega = 1.02$ today, with $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc. If space is a 3-sphere, what is its radius of curvature today, $1/\sqrt{\kappa}$?
</Problem>
<Step title="Solve for κ">
Today $a = 1$, so $\kappa = H_0^2(\Omega - 1)$ and $1/\sqrt{\kappa} = (c/H_0)/\sqrt{\Omega - 1}$, restoring $c$.
</Step>
<Step title="Numbers">
$c/H_0 = 299\,792/70 = 4283$ Mpc, and $\sqrt{0.02} = 0.141$, so the radius of curvature is about $30\,300$ Mpc, some seven times the Hubble length.
</Step>
<Step title="What it means">
A density only 2% above critical already makes space finite, though very large. Measurements can only ever bound $|\Omega - 1|$, never prove it zero; as a later development, the Planck satellite's 2018 results combined with galaxy surveys bound it at a few tenths of a percent, so a 2% excess would in fact have been seen.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Equations of state

The Friedmann equations involve both $\rho$ and $P$, so to go further one needs an **equation of state**, $P$ as a function of $\rho$. Cosmology usually takes the linear form $P = w\rho$. Hughes stresses that this is very restrictive (for stars, the next topic of the course, it is not used) but works well for what dominates the universe on the largest scales.

Suppose one species $i$, with $P_i = w_i\rho_i$, dominates. Dividing the energy-conservation law by $R_0^3$ and integrating gives

<Formula label="How a species dilutes" tex="\frac{\rho_i}{\rho_{i,0}} = \left(\frac{a}{a_0}\right)^{-3(1 + w_i)}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 16" />

with $a_0 = 1$ if the reference is today. Three cases:

- **Matter** ($w = 0$): pressureless "dust". The dust grains are galaxies and clusters, which hardly interact on cosmological scales. $\rho_m = \rho_{m,0}\,a^{-3}$: the number of particles is fixed and the volume grows, so doubling $a$ cuts the density by 8 (transcript pp. 16–17).
- **Radiation** ($w = 1/3$, the photon-gas result from statistical mechanics, with $P = \rho/3$): $\rho_r \propto a^{-4}$. Doubling $a$ divides the density by 16: 8 from the volume and another 2 because each photon is redshifted as the universe grows, a point Lecture 19 derives properly (transcript pp. 17–18). [Hughes says $\rho$ "scales with the scale factor to the fourth power"; it scales as its inverse fourth power.]
- **Cosmological constant** ($w = -1$, $P = -\rho$): $\rho \propto a^0$, constant. The energy density never changes while the volume grows, so the energy in a comoving volume rises. Total energy cannot be defined covariantly, and local conservation still holds, but this fluid is, as Hughes says, a little weird.

## One-species flat universes

To see what the solutions look like, Hughes takes a spatially flat universe ($\kappa = 0$) with a single species. F1 becomes

<Formula label="Flat, one species" tex="\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\,\rho_0\,a^{-3(1+w)} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot a \propto a^{1 - 3(1+w)/2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a \propto t^{2/[3(1+w)]}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 19" />

(Hughes abbreviates the exponent $3(1 + w)$ as $n$; we write it out to keep the letter free.)

- **Matter-dominated:** $a \propto t^{2/3}$. It expands, but more and more slowly.
- **Radiation-dominated:** $a \propto t^{1/2}$.
- **Cosmological constant:** the exponent blows up, so go back to the equation. With $\rho = \rho_0 = \Lambda/(8\pi G)$ constant, $(\dot a/a)^2 = \Lambda/3$, and the solution is exponential (Hughes first drops the square here and then corrects himself):

<Formula label="Cosmological-constant universe" tex="\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G\rho_0}{3} = \frac{\Lambda}{3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a \propto \exp\!\left(\sqrt{\Lambda/3}\;t\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 19" />

The real universe is not one species. It contains matter and radiation and something that looks very much like a cosmological constant (Hughes, in 2020: the jury is still out), and need not be exactly flat. One builds models with a mixture, finds epochs where each dominates (a $\sqrt t$ era giving way to a $t^{2/3}$ era as radiation fades), and asks whether the observables of the model match the sky. ## Summary

- A maximally symmetric space of dimension $n$ has $n(n+1)/2$ Killing vectors, and its Riemann tensor is $R(g_{\alpha\mu}g_{\beta\nu} - g_{\alpha\nu}g_{\beta\mu})/[n(n-1)]$. A maximally symmetric spacetime solves Einstein's equation only if empty and flat or filled with a cosmological constant.
- The universe is maximally symmetric in space but not in time: $ds^2 = -dt^2 + R^2(t)\gamma_{ij}dx^idx^j$ in comoving coordinates. Comparing two computations of the Ricci tensor gives the Robertson–Walker metric, with $d\bar r^2/(1 - k\bar r^2)$; only the sign of $k$ matters (closed 3-sphere, flat, open hyperbolic).
- With $a = R/R_0$ and $\kappa = k/R_0^2$, a perfect-fluid source and the trace-reversed field equation give $\ddot a/a = -\tfrac{4\pi G}{3}(\rho + 3P)$ and $(\dot a/a)^2 = \tfrac{8\pi G}{3}\rho - \kappa/a^2$. Energy conservation is the first law of thermodynamics.
- $\rho_\text{crit} = 3H^2/8\pi G$ and $\Omega - 1 = \kappa/(H^2a^2)$: the density decides whether space is open, flat or closed.
- For $P = w\rho$, $\rho \propto a^{-3(1+w)}$: matter $a^{-3}$, radiation $a^{-4}$, cosmological constant constant. Flat one-species universes grow as $t^{2/3}$, $t^{1/2}$ and exponentially.
