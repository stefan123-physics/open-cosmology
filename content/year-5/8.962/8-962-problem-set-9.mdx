## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit named in the question. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `2/(H0*sqrt(1 + z))` or `(1 - 1/Omega1)*a2/a1`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. It treats its letters as independent, so each question says which letters to type: `H0` for $H_0$, `z` for a redshift, `Omega1` for $\Omega_1$, `a1` and `a2` for $a_1$ and $a_2$, `sqrt` for a square root.

Give a number answer to 3 significant figures and keep 4 in intermediate steps; an answer with only 2 significant figures may or may not be accepted. Where a box needs more digits, its label says so.

MIT's set gives no points. The points here, and their split between parts, are ours. Several parts ask you to show something. Those parts have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The conventions are those of the lessons. The speed of light is 1 in the formulas, and is put back for numbers. The universe has the scale factor $a(t)$, with $a = 1$ today, at the time $t_0$. A redshift $z$ belongs to the scale factor $a = 1/(1 + z)$. The expansion rate is $H = \dot a/a$, with the value $H_0$ today. A density $\rho$ is an energy density (with $c = 1$ the same as a mass density), and $\Omega = \rho/\rho_\text{crit}$ is the total density divided by the critical density $3H^2/(8\pi G)$. The curvature of space is $\kappa = k/R_0^2$, where $k$ is $+1$, 0 or $-1$. A coordinate distance is a distance in comoving coordinates; with $a = 1$ today it is the distance between the two places measured today.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Time scale of a microlensing event" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

A dark body in our Galaxy passes in front of a distant star. Its gravity bends the star's light and forms two images. In **microlensing** the two images are too close together to be seen apart, and only their total brightness is measured. The brightness rises while the lens comes close to the line of sight, and falls again as it moves away.

Take the sheet's case. The source is a star in the Large Magellanic Cloud, at the distance $D_S = 100$ kpc. The lens is a dark body of about one solar mass in the Galaxy, at the distance $D_L = 10$ kpc. It moves across the line of sight at a typical orbital speed in the Galaxy, $v = 200$ km/s (the sheet's numbers, with $1\ \text{pc} = 3.1\times 10^{13}$ km). Treat the space between as flat and static, and ignore the motion of the source and of the Earth.

When source, lens and observer are exactly in line, the image is a ring around the lens, the Einstein ring. Its angular radius $\theta_E$ is the Einstein angle, and $r_E = D_L\,\theta_E$ is the Einstein radius, its size in the plane of the lens. The brightness changes markedly while the lens is within about one Einstein radius of the line of sight, so the time scale of the light curve is

<Formula label="Problem 1: the time scale" tex="t_E = \frac{r_E}{v}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own definition)" />

Find $t_E$ in days. For the mass use $GM/c^2$ of the Sun with $GM = 1.32712\times 10^{20}$ m³/s² (NASA). Then (our addition) find the Einstein angle $\theta_E$ in milliarcseconds.

<Hint>

A ray that passes a mass $M$ at the distance $b$ is bent towards it by the angle $4GM/(c^2 b)$ (Problem 2 of Problem Set 5). Draw the straight line from the observer through the lens to the source, and a ray that leaves the source, is bent once in the plane of the lens, and reaches the observer.

</Hint>

<Hint>

All angles are tiny. A ray that reaches us at the angle $\theta$ from the lens passes it at $b = D_L\theta$. For a source exactly behind the lens, the bent ray must meet the source: the angle $\theta$ seen from us, times $D_S$, equals the bending angle times the distance $D_{LS} = D_S - D_L$ from the lens to the source.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-te" label="$t_E =$" value="74.3" unit="days" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-te" />

<Answer id="p1-theta" label="$\theta_E =$" value="0.854" unit="milliarcseconds" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-theta" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Find the Einstein radius from the bending angle and the geometry of a straight line of sight. The time scale is the time the lens needs to cross it.

<Step title="The bending angle">

In the weak field of the lens the metric is the one of the lesson,

<Formula label="The weak-field metric" tex="ds^2 = -\left(1 + 2\Phi\right)dt^2 + \left(1 - 2\Phi\right)\left(dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2\right), \qquad \Phi = -\frac{GM}{r}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 14" />

and Problem 2 of Problem Set 5 found from it the bending angle of a ray that passes at the distance $b$:

<Formula label="Problem 1: the bending angle" tex="\alpha = \frac{4GM}{c^2\,b}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution; the angle is found in Problem Set 5)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The lens equation">

Let $\beta$ be the angle of the source from the line through the lens, and $\theta$ the angle of an image, both seen from us. Measured across the line of sight at the distance of the source, the ray's offset is $\theta D_S$ without bending, and the bend at the lens moves it back by $\alpha D_{LS}$. The ray meets the source if

<Formula label="Problem 1: the lens equation" tex="\beta\,D_S = \theta\,D_S - \alpha\,D_{LS}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{4GM}{c^2\,D_L\,\theta}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

For a source exactly behind the lens, $\beta = 0$, and every direction around the lens at the same $\theta$ works: the image is a ring. Its angle and its radius in the lens plane are

<Formula label="Problem 1: the Einstein angle and radius" tex="\theta_E^2 = \frac{4GM}{c^2}\,\frac{D_{LS}}{D_L\,D_S}, \qquad r_E = D_L\,\theta_E = \sqrt{\frac{4GM}{c^2}\,\frac{D_L\,D_{LS}}{D_S}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

For $\beta \ne 0$ the ring breaks into two images, on either side of the lens, at angles of the order of $\theta_E$ as long as $\beta$ is not much larger than $\theta_E$. That is when the total brightness is raised.

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

$GM/c^2 = 1.32712\times 10^{20}/(299\,792\,458)^2 = 1476.6$ m, so $4GM/c^2 = 5906$ m. With $D_{LS} = 90$ kpc, $D_L D_{LS}/D_S = 9$ kpc $= 2.79\times 10^{20}$ m. Then

<Formula label="Problem 1: the Einstein radius and the time scale" tex="r_E = \sqrt{5906 \times 2.79\times 10^{20}}\ \text{m} = 1.28\times 10^{12}\ \text{m}, \qquad t_E = \frac{1.28\times 10^{12}\ \text{m}}{2\times 10^{5}\ \text{m/s}} = 6.42\times 10^{6}\ \text{s} = 74.3\ \text{days}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The Einstein radius is about 8.6 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun. The lens takes about $2t_E$, some five months, to pass from one Einstein radius before the line of sight to one after it, so the star brightens and dims over a few months. The Einstein angle is

<Formula label="Problem 1: the Einstein angle" tex="\theta_E = \frac{r_E}{D_L} = \frac{1.28\times 10^{12}\ \text{m}}{3.1\times 10^{20}\ \text{m}} = 4.14\times 10^{-9}\ \text{rad} = 0.854\ \text{milliarcseconds}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own addition)" />

The two images are less than a thousandth of an arcsecond from the lens, which is why only their total brightness is seen.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $GM/c^2$ is a length, so $r_E$ is the square root of a length times a length. The Einstein radius grows as $\sqrt M$: a lens of a hundredth of a solar mass gives an event ten times shorter, about a week. The [dark-matter course's lesson on microlensing](/courses/fundamentals-of-dark-matter/weak-lensing-and-machos) makes the same estimate for other masses and distances, and shows the shape of the light curve.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The proper-motion distance" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

The lesson met three ways to measure a distance, defined as in flat space (Lecture 19 transcript, pp. 11–12):

<Formula label="Luminosity, angular-diameter and proper-motion distances" tex="F = \frac{L}{4\pi d_L^2}, \qquad \delta\theta = \frac{\delta\ell}{d_A}, \qquad \dot\theta = \frac{v_\perp}{d_M}" cite="reading3" at="transcript pp. 11–12" />

The last one is the proper-motion distance. In words (ours, since the sheet points to a textbook for it): a source moves across the line of sight with the speed $v_\perp$, measured at the source with a clock that moves with the source, and we see its direction change at the rate $\dot\theta$, measured with our own clock. The proper-motion distance is $d_M = v_\perp/\dot\theta$.

In a Robertson–Walker universe of any curvature, with the line element of the lesson,

<Formula label="FRW line element with χ" tex="ds^2 = -dt^2 + a^2(t)R_0^2\left[d\chi^2 + S_k^2(\chi)\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right]" cite="reading3" at="transcript p. 10" />

where $S_k(\chi)$ is $\sin\chi$, $\chi$ or $\sinh\chi$ for $k = +1$, 0 or $-1$, compute $d_M$ as a function of the redshift $z$ of the source. Write it in the form that the lesson found for $d_A$, and show that

<Formula label="Problem 2: the rule for the three distances" tex="d_L(z) = (1 + z)\,d_M(z) = (1 + z)^2\,d_A(z)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1" />

The lesson found the angular-diameter distance $d_A = R_0 S_k(\chi)/(1 + z)$, so you may use it, and derive $d_L$ yourself.

The two boxes are our additions. In a flat universe that holds only matter, find $d_M$ in terms of `H0` and `z`. Then find, as a decimal, the redshift at which the angular-diameter distance of that universe is largest.

<Hint>

Put us at $\chi = 0$. The source is at the coordinate $\chi$ and sends the light at the time $t_\text{em}$, when the scale factor was $a_\text{em} = 1/(1 + z)$. A step $\delta\ell$ across the line of sight at the source covers the angle $\delta\theta = \delta\ell/(a_\text{em}R_0 S_k(\chi))$, as in the lesson's derivation of $d_A$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The two clocks run at different rates as seen through the light: a time $\delta t_\text{em}$ at the source arrives spread over $\delta t_0 = (1 + z)\,\delta t_\text{em}$ here, for the same reason that a wavelength is stretched by $1 + z$. For $d_L$, count how the energy of each photon and the rate at which photons arrive change, and over what area the light is spread today.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-dm" label="Flat, matter only: $d_M =$" expr="2*(1 - 1/sqrt(1 + z))/H0"
  vars={{ H0: [0.5, 2], z: [0.1, 5] }} names={{ H0: "H_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*(1 - 1/sqrt(1 + z))/(H0*(1 + z))", hint: "That is the angular-diameter distance, d_M divided by 1 + z. The proper-motion distance has no factor 1/(1 + z)." },
    { expr: "2*(1 + z)*(1 - 1/sqrt(1 + z))/H0", hint: "That is the luminosity distance, (1 + z) times d_M." },
    { expr: "z/H0", hint: "That is Hubble's law, d = z/H0, which holds only for small z. Integrate dz/E(z) with E(z) = (1 + z)^(3/2)." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-zmax" label="$d_A$ is largest at $z =$" value="1.25" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-zmax" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The angle that the source moves through is the angle of the lesson's derivation of $d_A$. The time over which we see it move is stretched by $1 + z$. The two factors $1/(1 + z)$ cancel, and $d_M$ is the coordinate size $R_0S_k(\chi)$ itself.

<Step title="The angle and the time">

In the time $\delta t_\text{em}$ the source moves across the line of sight by $\delta\ell = v_\perp\,\delta t_\text{em}$. At the time of emission the sphere around us through the source has the radius $a_\text{em}R_0S_k(\chi)$ in its angular part, so the direction changes by

<Formula label="Problem 2: the angle" tex="\delta\theta = \frac{v_\perp\,\delta t_\text{em}}{a_\text{em}\,R_0\,S_k(\chi)} = \frac{(1 + z)\,v_\perp\,\delta t_\text{em}}{R_0\,S_k(\chi)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Light leaving at $t_\text{em}$ and at $t_\text{em} + \delta t_\text{em}$ travels the same $\chi$. For radial light $dt = a R_0\,d\chi$, so $\int dt/a$ is the same for both, and the two ends give $\delta t_\text{em}/a_\text{em} = \delta t_0/1$: we see the motion over the time $\delta t_0 = (1 + z)\,\delta t_\text{em}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The proper-motion distance">

$\dot\theta = \delta\theta/\delta t_0 = v_\perp/(R_0 S_k(\chi))$, so

<Formula label="Problem 2: the proper-motion distance" tex="d_M = R_0\,S_k(\chi) = (1 + z)\,d_A, \qquad \chi = \frac{1}{R_0H_0}\int_0^z\frac{dz'}{E(z')}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

with $\chi$ from the lesson (Lecture 19 transcript, pp. 14–15). With $R_0 = 1/(H_0\sqrt{|\Omega_\text{curv}|})$ from the lesson, this is

<Formula label="Problem 2: the proper-motion distance in terms of the redshift" tex="d_M = \frac{1}{H_0}\times\begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{|\Omega_\text{curv}|}}\,\sin\!\left(\sqrt{|\Omega_\text{curv}|}\displaystyle\int_0^z\frac{dz'}{E(z')}\right), & k = +1 \\[2ex] \displaystyle\int_0^z\frac{dz'}{E(z')}, & k = 0 \\[2ex] \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{|\Omega_\text{curv}|}}\,\sinh\!\left(\sqrt{|\Omega_\text{curv}|}\displaystyle\int_0^z\frac{dz'}{E(z')}\right), & k = -1 \end{cases}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

It is the lesson's $d_A$ without the factor $1/(1 + z)$ in front, as Hughes says in the lecture (Lecture 19 transcript, p. 16). Here $E(z)$ is the lesson's

<Formula label="The expansion rate as a function of redshift" tex="H(z) = H_0\,E(z), \qquad E(z) = \sqrt{\sum_i \Omega_i\left(1 + z\right)^{n_i}}" cite="reading3" at="transcript p. 13" />

with the curvature among the species.

</Step>

<Step title="The luminosity distance">

The source sends out the power $L$. Today its light is spread over a sphere around the source that passes through us. In comoving coordinates it has the radius $\chi$, so today its area is $4\pi R_0^2S_k^2(\chi)$. Each photon arrives with its energy divided by $1 + z$, and the photons sent out in the time $\delta t_\text{em}$ arrive over $(1 + z)\,\delta t_\text{em}$. So

<Formula label="Problem 2: the flux and the luminosity distance" tex="F = \frac{L}{4\pi R_0^2S_k^2(\chi)\,(1 + z)^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d_L = (1 + z)\,R_0S_k(\chi) = (1 + z)\,d_M = (1 + z)^2\,d_A" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

which is the rule of the statement.

</Step>

<Step title="A flat universe of matter (our addition)">

With only matter and no curvature, $E(z) = (1 + z)^{3/2}$, and

<Formula label="Problem 2: the distance in a flat universe of matter" tex="d_M = \frac{1}{H_0}\int_0^z\frac{dz'}{(1 + z')^{3/2}} = \frac{2}{H_0}\left(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + z}}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own addition)" />

The proper-motion distance grows with $z$ towards $2/H_0$. The angular-diameter distance $d_A = d_M/(1 + z)$ does not: write $s = 1/\sqrt{1 + z}$, so that $H_0 d_A = 2s^2(1 - s)$. Its derivative $2s(2 - 3s)$ vanishes at $s = 2/3$, which is $1 + z = 9/4$, $z = 1.25$. Beyond that redshift a ruler of fixed size looks larger the farther away it is.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For small $z$, $\int_0^z dz'/E \approx z$ and $S_k(\chi) \approx \chi$, so all three distances go to $z/H_0$: Hubble's law, where the three agree as in flat space. In the flat universe of matter, $2(1 - 1/\sqrt{1 + z}) \approx z$ for small $z$. Hughes adds that $d_M$ is of little use in practice: even a source moving across the sky at nearly the speed of light would turn by a negligible angle in a human lifetime (Lecture 19 transcript, pp. 16–17).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Particle horizons in the early universe" points={20} mit="Problem 3">

The early universe was hot and dense enough to be a plasma, and light scattered again and again off its free charges. At **recombination**, at the redshift $z_r \approx 1200$ (the sheet's value), it became cool enough for electrons and protons to form atoms. From then on light streams freely: the microwave background is that light.

Take a flat universe ($k = 0$) that is dominated by radiation before recombination, at the time $t_r$, and by matter after it. (The sheet notes that matter and radiation had equal densities somewhat earlier, at $z_\text{eq} \approx 3000$. Taking the two times as one is good enough here.) Make the model precise this way: the density falls as $a^{-4}$ before $t_r$ and as $a^{-3}$ after, and it does not jump at $t_r$. (This is our choice: the sheet leaves the join open. Joining $a \propto t^{1/2}$ to $a \propto t^{2/3}$ at one time instead gives a horizon distance larger by $4/3$.)

The coordinate distance that light can travel from the big bang to $t_r$ is the **comoving particle horizon** at recombination, $r_H(t_r)$. Two places are in causal contact only if each is within the other's particle horizon. Let $r_\text{obs}(t_r)$ be the coordinate distance from the place where light left at recombination to us. Patches of the microwave background on opposite sides of the sky are then $2r_\text{obs}(t_r)$ apart.

<Part label="a" points={8}>

Find $r_H(t_r)$ and $r_\text{obs}(t_r)$. Type each in terms of `H0` and `z` (for $z_r$). Then (our addition) find the ratio $r_H/r_\text{obs}$ for $z_r = 1200$, as a decimal.

</Hint>

<Hint>

<Solution>

<Solution>

**The idea.** A uniform temperature is what equilibrium gives, and equilibrium needs contact. Patches more than $r_H$ apart had no contact at all before the light left them.

<Step title="The argument">

</Step>

A ratio close to 1 or larger would not be puzzling: then each patch we see would have been within the horizon of the others, and contact could have smoothed the temperature. That is why the sheet says that a ratio that is not small means a mistake in part (a).

How does inflation solve the flatness problem? If the universe goes through just the $N_e$ e-foldings that Problem 4(b) found for the horizon problem, is it likely to be flat enough today?

Two boxes (our additions). First, using the exact relation $\Omega - 1 = \kappa/(aH)^2$, find the ratio of $\Omega - 1$ today to $\Omega - 1$ at the start of inflation, $t_1$, for the $N_e$ of Problem 4(b). Second, if $|\Omega - 1| = 1$ at $t_1$, how many e-foldings beyond that $N_e$ are needed for $|\Omega - 1| \le 0.01$ today?

<Hint>

$\kappa$ is the same at all times, so the ratio is $(a_1H_1)^2/(a_0H_0)^2$, with $a_0 = 1$ today, $H_1 = \sqrt{\Lambda/3}$ and $a_1 = a(t_2)\,e^{-N_e}$. Use the condition of Problem 4(b) for $e^{N_e}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Each extra e-folding multiplies $a_1H_1$ by $e^{-1}$, and so $\Omega - 1$ today by $e^{-2}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5d-ratio" label="$(\Omega_0 - 1)/(\Omega_1 - 1) =$" value="0.273" unit="(a pure number)" tolerance="10%" calc="p5d-ratio" />

<Answer id="p5d-extra" label="Extra e-foldings:" value="1.65" tolerance="0.05" calc="p5d-extra" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Both problems are about the same quantity, the comoving length $1/(aH)$. The horizon problem needs it to have been as large at the start of inflation as the distance to the microwave background is now. Then $\Omega - 1 = \kappa/(aH)^2$ was about as large at the start of inflation as it is today.

<Step title="The ratio">

From $\Omega - 1 = \kappa/(aH)^2$ with constant $\kappa$,

<Formula label="Problem 5(d): Ω − 1 today and at the start of inflation" tex="\frac{\Omega_0 - 1}{\Omega_1 - 1} = \left(\frac{a_1H_1}{H_0}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{a_2H_1\,e^{-N_e}}{H_0}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{1 - e^{-N_e}}{2 - 3\,a_r^{1/2}}\right)^2 \approx \frac{1}{1.913^2} = 0.273" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

The third step uses the condition of Problem 4(b). The result depends neither on $T_\text{vac}$ nor on $H_0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The extra e-foldings">

If $|\Omega - 1| = 1$ at $t_1$, it is 0.273 today with the minimum $N_e$. To bring it to 0.01 it must shrink by a further $0.273/0.01 = 27.3$, and each e-folding gives $e^2$: $\Delta N = \tfrac12\ln 27.3 = 1.65$. So about 61 e-foldings solve both problems at once.

</Step>

<Step title="How inflation solves the problem">

During inflation $|\Omega - 1|$ falls as $e^{-2N}$, and in the following eras it grows again as $a^2$ and $a$. Inflation does not need fine tuning: whatever $\Omega$ was at $t_1$, enough e-foldings make it as close to 1 as we like. The horizon problem already demands about 59 e-foldings, which leaves $\Omega - 1$ today at about a quarter of its value at $t_1$; nothing makes inflation stop at exactly that number, and a couple more e-foldings bring the universe within the observed 1%. So yes, an inflation long enough to cure the horizon problem is likely to leave the universe flat enough, and one only a little longer leaves it far flatter than any measurement could tell. Hughes makes the same point: once inflation has driven the curvature close enough to 0, the growth afterwards never catches up (Lecture 19 transcript, p. 21).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The factor $e^{-2N_e} = 3.0\times 10^{-52}$ of part (c) is larger than the bound $6.62\times 10^{-55}$ of part (b) by a factor of about 450. That fits: the bound of part (b) uses the radiation model of Problem 3 back to $a_2$, whose expansion rate there is 4.07 times the $H$ of inflation (Problem 4(b)), and $4.07^2 \times 27.3 = 450$.

</Step>
