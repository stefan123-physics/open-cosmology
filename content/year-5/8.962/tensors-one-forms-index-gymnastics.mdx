The last lecture ended with a first definition of a tensor. This one completes it. Hughes shows how to get a tensor's components and how they transform, introduces the one-form (a tensor with one slot) and its most important example, the gradient, and then uses the metric to move freely between vectors and one-forms. At the end the distinction between them almost disappears, and tensors of any type are defined at once. As before, $c = 1$ and the metric signature is $(-, +, +, +)$.

## Tensors as machines with slots

The scalar product of two 4-vectors is built from the metric of special relativity, which in rectilinear coordinates (Cartesian coordinates plus time) is (transcript pp. 1–2)

<Formula label="The metric of special relativity" tex="\eta_{\alpha\beta} = \vec e_\alpha\cdot\vec e_\beta = \mathrm{diag}(-1,\ 1,\ 1,\ 1)" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 1–2" />

The symbol $\eta$ is kept for this metric in these coordinates. Recall the definition: a **tensor of type (0, n)** is a mapping of $n$ vectors into Lorentz-invariant scalars that is linear in each argument (transcript p. 2); the scalar product was defined to copy the structure of the invariant interval. The scalar product is one: multiply a vector by a number $\gamma$ and the result is multiplied by $\gamma$; put a sum of vectors into a slot and the result is the sum, and so on for every rule of linearity. (On the board Hughes first used $\alpha$ for both a prefactor and an index, and renamed the prefactor; transcript p. 2.)

Books such as Misner, Thorne and Wheeler (MTW) and Carroll picture a tensor as a machine with slots, writing it in bold; Hughes writes two bars over the symbol on the blackboard (transcript p. 3). With both slots of the metric filled,

<Formula label="The metric as a machine with two slots" tex="\eta(\vec A, \vec B) = \vec A\cdot\vec B = \eta_{\alpha\beta}\,A^\alpha B^\beta" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3" />

The vectors are frame-independent geometric objects and so is the number that comes out. So the machine itself must be a frame-independent geometric object too (transcript p. 3). Some tensors are easy to picture, like the one coming soon that describes the flow of energy and momentum, whose two indices say which component of 4-momentum flows in which direction; others are harder, but all of them give a Lorentz-invariant number when every slot is filled with a geometric object (transcript p. 4).

## Components and how they transform

To get an observer's components, put that observer's basis vectors into the slots (transcript p. 4). For the metric,

<Formula label="Components from basis vectors" tex="\eta(\vec e_\alpha, \vec e_\beta) = \eta_{\alpha\beta}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 4" />

A second observer $\bar O$, rushing through the room at three quarters of the speed of light, puts in their own basis vectors. These are related to the first observer's by Lorentz matrices, and since every slot is linear the matrices come straight out as numbers (transcript pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="Transformation of the components of a (0, 2) tensor" tex="\eta_{\bar\alpha\bar\beta} = \eta(\vec e_{\bar\alpha}, \vec e_{\bar\beta}) = \Lambda^{\mu}{}_{\bar\alpha}\,\Lambda^{\nu}{}_{\bar\beta}\;\eta(\vec e_\mu, \vec e_\nu) = \Lambda^{\mu}{}_{\bar\alpha}\,\Lambda^{\nu}{}_{\bar\beta}\;\eta_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 5" />

Any (0, n) tensor works the same way: one Lorentz matrix per index, with the indices lined up (transcript p. 5). Hughes admits that the metric is a dull example. Feed $\mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ through the most horrible Lorentz transformation you like and you get $\mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ back: the metric of special relativity has the same components for every inertial observer, which is one of its defining properties (transcript pp. 5–6). (Here Hughes says "curvilinear coordinates" but means the rectilinear coordinates of inertial frames; in curvilinear coordinates the components change, as Lecture 2 showed.) The recipe, though, is general. In his words, learn the lesson and overlook the example (transcript p. 6).

<WorkedExample title="The metric is the same for a moving observer">
<Problem>
For a boost with $v = 0.6$ along $x$, the inverse Lorentz matrix has $\Lambda^0{}_{\bar 0} = \Lambda^1{}_{\bar 1} = \gamma = 1.25$ and $\Lambda^0{}_{\bar 1} = \Lambda^1{}_{\bar 0} = \gamma v = 0.75$. Compute $\eta_{\bar 0\bar 0}$ and $\eta_{\bar 0\bar 1}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="The time-time component">
$\eta_{\bar 0\bar 0} = \Lambda^\mu{}_{\bar 0}\Lambda^\nu{}_{\bar 0}\,\eta_{\mu\nu} = (1.25)^2(-1) + (0.75)^2(+1) = -1.5625 + 0.5625 = -1$.
</Step>
<Step title="The time-space component">
$\eta_{\bar 0\bar 1} = \Lambda^0{}_{\bar 0}\Lambda^0{}_{\bar 1}(-1) + \Lambda^1{}_{\bar 0}\Lambda^1{}_{\bar 1}(+1) = -1.25 \times 0.75 + 0.75 \times 1.25 = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="Conclusion">
The moving observer finds the same $-1$ and $0$; the other components work out the same way, so $\eta_{\bar\alpha\bar\beta} = \mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$, as it must.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## One-forms

The (0, 1) tensors, with a single slot, are called **one-forms**, or dual vectors for a reason that comes shortly (transcript p. 6). Hughes marks them, for this lecture only, with a tilde. A one-form $\tilde p$ takes one vector and returns a scalar, $\tilde p(\vec A)$. Its components come, as always, from the basis vectors, and they carry their index downstairs (transcript p. 6):

<Formula label="Components of a one-form" tex="p_\alpha = \tilde p(\vec e_\alpha)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 6" />

Writing $\vec A = A^\alpha\vec e_\alpha$ and using linearity (transcript p. 7):

<Formula label="A one-form acting on a vector: contraction" tex="\tilde p(\vec A) = \tilde p(A^\alpha\,\vec e_\alpha) = A^\alpha\,\tilde p(\vec e_\alpha) = p_\alpha A^\alpha" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 7" />

Summing upstairs components against downstairs ones like this is called **contraction**. A one-form is a tensor, so its components transform by the general rule, one Lorentz matrix lined up with the index (transcript p. 7):

<Formula label="Transformation of one-form components" tex="p_{\bar\alpha} = \Lambda^{\beta}{}_{\bar\alpha}\,p_\beta" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 7" />

Just as vectors have basis vectors, one-forms have **basis one-forms** $\tilde\omega^\alpha$, chosen so that every one-form is its components times them, with an honest equals sign (transcript pp. 7–8). Requiring $\tilde p(\vec A) = p_\beta\,\tilde\omega^\beta(A^\alpha\vec e_\alpha) = p_\beta A^\alpha\,\tilde\omega^\beta(\vec e_\alpha)$ to equal $p_\alpha A^\alpha$ fixes their defining property:

<Formula label="Basis one-forms" tex="\tilde p = p_\alpha\,\tilde\omega^\alpha, \qquad \tilde\omega^\alpha(\vec e_\beta) = \delta^\alpha_{\ \beta}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 7–8" />

For a particular observer, $\tilde\omega^0$ has components $(1, 0, 0, 0)$, $\tilde\omega^1$ has $(0, 1, 0, 0)$, and so on, which looks like a mere repeat of the basis vectors (transcript p. 8). The word "dual" explains the difference. In linear-algebra language the basis vectors are columns and the basis one-forms rows, and an invariant number only comes from combining objects of the two kinds (transcript p. 8). One can perfectly well compute $\sum_\alpha A^\alpha B^\alpha$ in some frame, but it means nothing physically, because it does not use the metric, the structure that makes the manifold what it is; $\eta_{\alpha\beta}A^\alpha B^\beta$, by contrast, is the same for everyone (transcript pp. 8–9). Hughes's analogy from quantum mechanics: you could integrate the product $\psi(x)\phi(x)$ of two wave functions over all space, but the meaningful combination is the inner product $\int \psi^*\phi\,dx$, with the complex conjugate (transcript p. 9).

## The gradient is a one-form

The best example is the gradient (transcript pp. 9–11). An observer moves through spacetime with 4-velocity $\vec u = d\vec x/d\tau$, through a field $\phi$ that depends on all four coordinates. How fast does $\phi$ change along the observer's world line? In ordinary 3-space the answer is the directional derivative, $d\phi/dt = (dx/dt)\,\partial\phi/\partial x + \dots = \vec v\cdot\nabla\phi$ (with partial derivatives on the right; transcript p. 10). In spacetime, time is a coordinate, and the parameter along the path is the proper time:

<Formula label="Rate of change of a field along a world line" tex="\frac{d\phi}{d\tau} = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x^\alpha}\,\frac{dx^\alpha}{d\tau} = u^\alpha\,\partial_\alpha\phi" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 10" />

The left side is a scalar that every observer agrees on, and the $u^\alpha$ are the components of a vector. So the $\partial_\alpha\phi$ must be the components of a one-form, the **gradient one-form** $\tilde d\phi$ (transcript pp. 10–11). Hughes notes two other notations: the familiar $\nabla\phi$ (with the warning that $\nabla$ will soon get a more general meaning, which reduces to this one in special relativity), and $\nabla_{\vec u}\phi$ for the derivative along $\vec u$, the gradient one-form contracted with $\vec u$ (transcript p. 11).

<WorkedExample title="A field seen by a moving observer">
<Problem>
A field is $\phi = 3t + 2x$ (units with $c = 1$). An observer moves along $x$ at $v = 0.6$. How fast does $\phi$ change per unit of the observer's proper time?
</Problem>
<Step title="The two sets of components">
The gradient one-form has components $\partial_\alpha\phi = (3, 2, 0, 0)$. With $\gamma = 1.25$ the 4-velocity is $u^\alpha = (1.25, 0.75, 0, 0)$.
</Step>
<Step title="Contract">
$d\phi/d\tau = u^\alpha\partial_\alpha\phi = 1.25 \times 3 + 0.75 \times 2 = 3.75 + 1.5 = 5.25$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check along the world line">
The observer's path is $t = 1.25\,\tau$, $x = 0.75\,\tau$, so $\phi = 3(1.25\,\tau) + 2(0.75\,\tau) = 5.25\,\tau$. Note there is no metric and no minus sign: a one-form meets a vector directly.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## What a one-form looks like

The gradient also explains the basis one-forms (transcript pp. 11–12). The derivative of a coordinate with respect to a coordinate is a Kronecker delta, $\partial x^\alpha/\partial x^\beta = \delta^\alpha_{\ \beta}$, exactly the defining property of the basis one-forms. So the basis one-forms are the gradients of the coordinates:

<Formula label="Basis one-forms are gradients of the coordinates" tex="\tilde\omega^\alpha = \tilde d x^\alpha" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 12" />

For a picture, Hughes uses a height field $h(x, y)$ on a topographic map (transcript pp. 12–13). The change in height along a small displacement $\Delta\vec x$ is $\tilde d h(\Delta\vec x)$, the gradient one-form with the displacement in its slot (for a longer step, the one-form taken at the middle of the step; for a quadratic $h$ like the one below that is exact). The same displacement gives a large change where the contour lines are crowded and a small one where they are sparse. So the natural picture of a one-form is not an arrow but the set of level surfaces itself: the number it gives a vector is how many surfaces the vector pierces. The basis one-form $\tilde\omega^x$ is a stack of sheets normal to the $x$ axis, one unit apart, filling all of space (MTW draws them; transcript p. 13). Such a stack is made for catching fluxes: everything that flows in the $x$ direction passes through the sheets of $\tilde\omega^x$ (transcript pp. 13–14). If this feels odd, Hughes says, it is fine to calculate first; intuition comes with practice, as with a musical instrument (transcript p. 14).

## The metric turns vectors into one-forms

Now back to the metric (transcript p. 14). With both slots filled it gives a number. Fill only one slot with a vector $\vec A$ and leave the other empty: the result takes one vector and returns a number, so it is a one-form, $\tilde A = \eta(\vec A, \ )$. Its components follow by putting in a basis vector and using the symmetry of the metric:

<Formula label="Lowering an index" tex="A_\alpha = \tilde A(\vec e_\alpha) = \eta(\vec A, \vec e_\alpha) = \eta_{\alpha\beta}\,A^\beta" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 14" />

The metric converts vectors into one-forms by **lowering the index**. The step can be undone with the **inverse metric**, defined with its indices upstairs (transcript p. 15):

<Formula label="The inverse metric and raising an index" tex="\eta^{\alpha\beta}\,\eta_{\beta\gamma} = \delta^\alpha_{\ \gamma}, \qquad A^\alpha = \eta^{\alpha\beta}A_\beta" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 15" />

In special relativity with rectilinear coordinates $\eta^{\alpha\beta}$ is again $\mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$, but that will not be true in general (transcript p. 15). Now the scalar product can be written in many equivalent ways (transcript p. 15):

<Formula label="Equivalent forms of the scalar product" tex="\vec A\cdot\vec B = \eta_{\alpha\beta}A^\alpha B^\beta = A_\alpha B^\alpha = A^\alpha B_\alpha = \eta^{\alpha\beta}A_\alpha B_\beta" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 15" />

## Tensors of type (m, n)

With the metric able to swap vectors and one-forms, there is no reason to treat tensors only as things that act on vectors: a vector can just as well be seen as something that acts on one-forms. The complete definition (transcript p. 15):

> A **tensor of type (m, n)** takes $m$ one-forms and $n$ vectors, is linear in each of them, and returns a Lorentz-invariant number (Hughes's definition).

A (0, 1) tensor is a one-form and a (1, 0) tensor is a vector (transcript p. 15). The scaffolding of separate names can now be kicked away: the metric changes the kind of a slot (transcript p. 16). Lowering an index turns an $(m, n)$ tensor into an $(m - 1, n + 1)$ tensor, and raising does the reverse, for example

<Formula label="Lowering and raising an index of a tensor" tex="R_{\mu\beta\gamma\delta} = \eta_{\mu\alpha}\,R^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\gamma\delta}, \qquad S^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\gamma} = \eta^{\alpha\mu}\,S_{\mu\beta\gamma}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 16" />

where the first tensor goes from one one-form slot and three vector slots to four vector slots (transcript p. 16).

## Basis objects for tensors

Does every tensor need a basis object of its own, one for two indices, one for three, and so on up to seventeen? No: basis vectors and basis one-forms are enough (transcript p. 16). A tensor with two downstairs indices is written with the outer product of two basis one-forms, one attached to each slot (transcript pp. 16–17):

<Formula label="A two-index tensor and its basis" tex="\eta = \eta_{\alpha\beta}\;\tilde\omega^\alpha\otimes\tilde\omega^\beta" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 16–17" />

Each index carries a direction of its own. The coming **stress-energy tensor** $T^{\alpha\beta}$ is the example: its $\alpha\beta$ component is the flux of 4-momentum component $\alpha$ in the $\beta$ direction, so it points in two directions at once, one saying which momentum and one saying where it flows (transcript p. 17).

The basis objects matter most for derivatives (transcript pp. 17–18). For a tensor field along an observer's world line in special relativity with rectilinear coordinates, the basis objects are constant and drop out. In curvilinear coordinates or curved spacetime they change from point to point, and a proper derivative has to account for that. The complicated divergence and curl formulas of electromagnetism in curvilinear coordinates are exactly this effect, in an orthonormal rather than a coordinate basis (transcript p. 18).

Hughes closes with the point he most wants remembered: the metric raises and lowers indices. That is why he avoids the words covariant and contravariant: once there is a metric, upstairs and downstairs components carry the same information, and neither is more physical than the other (transcript p. 19).

<Callout kind="cosmology">
The stress-energy tensor previewed here is how general relativity describes the contents of the universe. For the homogeneous cosmic fluid of 8.286 it holds just two numbers in the fluid's rest frame, the energy density and the pressure, and its conservation gives the equation that tells how the density of matter, radiation or vacuum energy changes as the universe expands.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A **(0, n) tensor** is a linear machine with $n$ slots that turns vectors into a Lorentz-invariant number; it is a frame-independent geometric object. Its components are its values on basis vectors, and they transform with one Lorentz matrix per index: $\eta_{\bar\alpha\bar\beta} = \Lambda^\mu{}_{\bar\alpha}\Lambda^\nu{}_{\bar\beta}\eta_{\mu\nu}$, which leaves $\mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ unchanged.
- A **one-form** $\tilde p$ is a (0, 1) tensor with components $p_\alpha = \tilde p(\vec e_\alpha)$; acting on a vector it gives the contraction $p_\alpha A^\alpha$. Basis one-forms satisfy $\tilde\omega^\alpha(\vec e_\beta) = \delta^\alpha_{\ \beta}$.
- The **gradient** is a one-form: $d\phi/d\tau = u^\alpha\partial_\alpha\phi$. Basis one-forms are gradients of the coordinates; a one-form is pictured as a stack of level surfaces, suited to catching fluxes.
- The metric lowers indices, $A_\alpha = \eta_{\alpha\beta}A^\beta$, and the inverse metric raises them; $\vec A\cdot\vec B = A_\alpha B^\alpha$.
- A **tensor of type (m, n)** takes $m$ one-forms and $n$ vectors; vectors are (1, 0) and one-forms (0, 1) tensors. Raising and lowering change the type, and basis vectors and basis one-forms, combined by outer products, serve as basis objects for every tensor.
