## How to answer

Work each exercise on paper first (Exercises 3.2, 3.3 and 3.5 also need a small numerical calculation: a calculator with a root finder, a spreadsheet or a few lines of Python will do). Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it; type decimals, for example `0.25` or `1.2e5`, in the unit the box names. A formula box takes an expression in the letters it names, for example `a*b/c` or `sqrt(a)/b^2`; type `^` for powers.

The book gives no points: the points are ours, 100 in all (20, 20, 15, 20 and 25 for the five exercises), and so is every split between parts. Parts that ask you to show or discuss something have no box: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons. $r$ is the distance from the centre of a halo, $r_S$ its scale radius, $x = r/r_S$, $r_h$ the halo radius, $c = r_h/r_S$ the concentration, $\rho$ the density and $\gamma = d\ln\rho/d\ln r$ its logarithmic slope, $M(r)$ the mass inside radius $r$ and $v_c$ the circular velocity. $G$ is the gravitational constant.

Constants and cosmology (our choice where the book leaves it open): $G = 6.67430 \times 10^{-11}$ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻² (CODATA 2018), the solar mass $M_\odot = 1.98841 \times 10^{30}$ kg (from the IAU 2015 nominal value of $GM_\odot$, Resolution B3, divided by $G$), 1 Mpc $= 3.0857 \times 10^{22}$ m, and the values the book uses in this chapter: $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc and $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.3$.

## Exercise 3.1

<Exercise id="e1" title="How far does a Milky Way halo extend?" points={20} mit="Exercise 3.1">

A dark matter halo of $10^{12}\,M_\odot$ hosts a galaxy like the Milky Way. Use the result of spherical collapse that the mean density inside the halo is about 180 times the mean (background) matter density of the universe at the time the halo collapsed. Estimate the halo's radius, compare it with the size of the luminous part of our Galaxy, and discuss what you assumed about the cosmological model and the redshift of collapse (book, Exercise 3.1, p. 52).

<Part label="A" points={8}>

Suppose the halo collapsed today, at redshift $z_c = 0$ (our choice). What is its radius $R$, in kpc (tolerance 2%)?

<Hint>

The mean matter density today is $\Omega_{m,0}$ times the critical density $3H_0^2/8\pi G$. Working in solar masses and Mpc keeps the numbers small.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The halo is a sphere of mass $M$ whose mean density is 180 times that background: solve $M = \frac43\pi R^3 \times 180\,\bar\rho$ for $R$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e1-r0" label="$R$ in kpc:" value="319" tolerance="2%" calc="e1-r0" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The halo's mass and mean density fix its volume, and the mean density is a fixed multiple of the background density.

<Step title="The background density">

$H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc gives the critical density $\rho_c = 3H_0^2/8\pi G = 1.360 \times 10^{11}\,M_\odot$ per Mpc³, so the mean matter density today is $\bar\rho_0 = 0.3\rho_c = 4.080 \times 10^{10}\,M_\odot$ per Mpc³.

</Step>

<Step title="The radius">

<Formula label="Radius of a halo that collapsed at redshift z_c" tex="R = \left[\frac{3M}{4\pi \times 180\,\bar\rho_0\,(1 + z_c)^3}\right]^{1/3}" cite="mit" at="Exercise 3.1, p. 52 (our own solution)" />

For $z_c = 0$: $R = [3 \times 10^{12}/(4\pi \times 180 \times 4.080 \times 10^{10})]^{1/3}$ Mpc $= 0.319$ Mpc $= 319$ kpc.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The lesson's worked example has a $3 \times 10^{11}\,M_\odot$ halo at 200 times today's mean density and finds 0.206 Mpc (rounded there to 210 kpc). Our halo is 3.33 times heavier and sits at 180 rather than 200 times the background: $206 \times (3.33 \times 200/180)^{1/3}$ kpc $= 319$ kpc, the same.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="B" points={8}>

Now suppose the halo collapsed earlier, at $z_c = 1$ (our choice). What is its radius in kpc (tolerance 2%)? And (our addition) at what collapse redshift $z_c$ would its radius be 200 kpc, the upper end of the range the book gives for the Milky Way's halo (tolerance 2%)?

<Hint>

The mean matter density grows as $(1 + z)^3$ into the past. How does $R$ scale with $1 + z_c$ at fixed mass?

</Hint>

<Answer id="e1-r1" label="$R$ in kpc for $z_c = 1$:" value="160" tolerance="2%" calc="e1-r1" />

<Answer id="e1-zc" label="$z_c$ for $R = 200$ kpc:" value="0.596" tolerance="2%" calc="e1-zc" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At fixed mass, $R \propto \bar\rho^{-1/3} \propto (1 + z_c)^{-1}$: a halo that collapsed when the universe was half its present size is half as large.

<Step title="Collapse at z_c = 1">

$R = 319\ \text{kpc}/(1 + 1) = 160$ kpc.

</Step>

<Step title="The collapse redshift for 200 kpc">

$1 + z_c = 319/200 = 1.596$, so $z_c = 0.596$, about 0.6.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At $z_c = 1$ the background matter density is $8 \times 4.080 \times 10^{10} = 3.264 \times 10^{11}\,M_\odot$ per Mpc³, and $[3 \times 10^{12}/(4\pi \times 180 \times 3.264 \times 10^{11})]^{1/3}$ Mpc $= 0.160$ Mpc, as above.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="C" points={4}>

Compare these radii with the extent of the Milky Way's luminous disc (about 10 to 20 kpc, as the book and lesson say), and discuss the assumptions: the collapse redshift, the cosmological model, and the factor 180 itself. Mark yourself against the solution.

<Solution>

**The idea.** The estimate is robust to a factor of about two; whatever the details, the halo is ten or more times larger than the disc.

<Step title="Halo against disc">

Between $z_c = 0$ and $z_c = 1$ the radius runs from 319 to 160 kpc, the same scale as the book's 100 to 200 kpc for the Milky Way's halo (an earlier collapse fits that range better). The luminous disc reaches 10 to 20 kpc: the halo is roughly 10 to 30 times larger in radius ($160/15 \approx 11$, $319/15 \approx 21$), and thousands of times larger in volume. That is the picture of the chapter: gas cooled and sank to the centre of a much larger dark halo.

</Step>

<Step title="The collapse redshift">

$R \propto (1 + z_c)^{-1}$, so the redshift matters most. A halo has no single moment of collapse: it grows by mergers and accretion, so "the" collapse redshift is a convention (for example, when half the present mass was in place). Halos that collapsed earlier are denser and smaller, as the lesson says.

</Step>

<Step title="The cosmological model">

Only the background **matter** density enters, $\bar\rho = \Omega_{m,0}\rho_c(1 + z_c)^3$, and it depends on $\Omega_{m,0}h^2$, not on the cosmological constant. In an Einstein–de Sitter universe ($\Omega_{m,0} = 1$, same $H_0$), the background today is $1/0.3$ times denser and $R$ is $0.3^{1/3} = 0.67$ times smaller: 214 kpc for $z_c = 0$. The cosmological model enters in two more places. It sets **when** a halo of this mass typically collapses (with a cosmological constant, structure growth slows at late times). And the factor 180 is the Einstein–de Sitter result of spherical collapse ($18\pi^2 \approx 178$); with a cosmological constant, the same calculation gives a larger factor for halos collapsing at late times, which makes them somewhat smaller (our remark; $R$ changes only as the cube root of that factor). The lesson also notes that other conventions (200 times the critical density, for example) give different radii.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Exercise 3.2

<Exercise id="e2" title="The logarithmic slope of the Einasto profile" points={20} mit="Exercise 3.2">

The Einasto profile is

<Formula label="Einasto profile" tex="\rho(x; \alpha) = \rho_S\,\exp\left[-\frac{2}{\alpha}\left(x^\alpha - 1\right)\right], \qquad x = \frac{r}{r_S}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.3), p. 56" />

and the logarithmic slope of a density profile is

<Formula label="Logarithmic slope" tex="\gamma(r) = \frac{d\ln\rho}{d\ln r}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.1), p. 55" />

which is $-2$ at every radius for the isothermal sphere. Show that the Einasto profile reaches this isothermal slope at the scale radius for any $\alpha$, and check numerically that its slope behaves like that of the NFW profile, $\rho = \rho_0/[x(1+x)^2]$, for $\alpha$ near 0.2 (book, Exercise 3.2, p. 56).

<Part label="A" points={8}>

Find the logarithmic slope $\gamma$ of the Einasto profile as a function of $x$, and show that it equals $-2$ at $r = r_S$ for every $\alpha$. (The lesson quotes the result; derive it.)

<Hint>

Since $d\ln r = d\ln x$, you need $d\ln\rho/d\ln x = x\,d\ln\rho/dx$. Take the logarithm of $\rho$ first.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e2-gamma" label="$\gamma =$" expr="-2*x^a"
  vars={{ x: [5, 10], a: [0.1, 0.5] }}
  names={{ a: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-2*x^(a - 1)", hint: "That is the derivative of ln ρ with respect to x. The slope is with respect to ln r, which needs one more factor of x." },
    { expr: "-(2/a)*x^a", hint: "Differentiate the power too: the derivative of x to the power α brings down a factor α, which cancels the 1/α in front." },
    { expr: "-(2/a)*(x^a - 1)", hint: "That is ln(ρ/ρ_S) itself. You still need its derivative with respect to ln r." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For an exponential profile the logarithm is simple, and the slope is one derivative of it.

<Step title="The slope">

$\ln\rho = \ln\rho_S - \frac{2}{\alpha}(x^\alpha - 1)$, and $d(x^\alpha)/d\ln x = \alpha x^\alpha$, so

<Formula label="Slope of the Einasto profile" tex="\gamma = \frac{d\ln\rho}{d\ln x} = -\frac{2}{\alpha}\,\alpha x^\alpha = -2x^\alpha" cite="mit" at="Exercise 3.2, p. 56 (our own solution)" />

At $r = r_S$, $x = 1$ and $x^\alpha = 1$ for any $\alpha$, so $\gamma = -2$: the isothermal value.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The exponent of the profile was built for this: the factor $2/\alpha$ in front is what makes the slope at $x = 1$ equal to $-2$ whatever $\alpha$ is. As $x \to 0$ the slope goes to 0 (a flat centre, unlike the NFW cusp), and it steepens without limit as $x$ grows.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="B" points={12}>

Find the logarithmic slope of the NFW profile. Then (our choice of comparison) find the value of $\alpha$ for which the Einasto slope equals the NFW slope at $x = 0.1$, and the value for which they are equal at $x = 10$ (tolerance 1% each). Is "$\alpha \approx 0.2$" a fair summary?

<Hint>

For NFW, $\ln\rho = \ln\rho_0 - \ln x - 2\ln(1 + x)$. Differentiate with respect to $\ln x$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Setting $-2x^\alpha$ equal to a number $\gamma_\text{NFW}$ at a given $x$ gives $\alpha = \ln(\gamma_\text{NFW}/(-2))/\ln x$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e2-a01" label="$\alpha$ matching at $x = 0.1$:" value="0.228" tolerance="1%" calc="e2-a01" />

<Answer id="e2-a10" label="$\alpha$ matching at $x = 10$:" value="0.149" tolerance="1%" calc="e2-a10" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Both profiles have slope $-2$ at $r_S$ by construction; the question is how fast they move away from it on either side.

<Step title="The NFW slope">

$x\,d/dx$ of $-\ln x - 2\ln(1 + x)$ gives

<Formula label="Slope of the NFW profile" tex="\gamma_\text{NFW} = -1 - \frac{2x}{1 + x} = -\frac{1 + 3x}{1 + x}" cite="mit" at="Exercise 3.2, p. 56 (our own solution)" />

It runs from $-1$ at the centre to $-3$ far out, and is $-2$ at $x = 1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Matching at x = 0.1 and x = 10">

At $x = 0.1$: $\gamma_\text{NFW} = -1.3/1.1 = -1.182$, and $-2(0.1)^\alpha = -1.182$ gives $\alpha = \ln(0.591)/\ln(0.1) = 0.228$.

At $x = 10$: $\gamma_\text{NFW} = -31/11 = -2.818$, and $-2(10)^\alpha = -2.818$ gives $\alpha = \log_{10}(1.409) = 0.149$.

</Step>

<Step title="Is α ≈ 0.2 fair?">

Over the two decades from $0.1\,r_S$ to $10\,r_S$, where halos are best measured, the matching $\alpha$ lies between 0.15 and 0.23: "comparable for $\alpha \approx 0.2$" is a fair summary, as the book says. With $\alpha = 0.2$ exactly, the Einasto slope is $-1.26$ at $x = 0.1$ (NFW $-1.18$) and $-3.17$ at $x = 10$ (NFW $-2.82$). Far from $r_S$ the two differ in kind: NFW tends to the fixed slopes $-1$ and $-3$, while the Einasto slope keeps changing (0 at the centre, steeper and steeper outside). The lesson's graph of the slopes lets you move $\alpha$ and see this.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Exercise 3.3

<Exercise id="e3" title="Enclosed mass and characteristic radii of the NFW and Hernquist profiles" points={15} mit="Exercise 3.3">

In the double power law $\rho = \rho_0/[x^\alpha(1 + x)^\beta]$ with $x = r/r_S$ (book, Eq. 3.2, p. 55), the NFW profile has $(\alpha, \beta) = (1, 2)$ and the Hernquist profile $(1, 3)$. Show that the enclosed mass of the NFW profile diverges logarithmically at large radius, while that of the Hernquist profile tends to a finite total mass, and find in both cases the radius $r_{v,\text{max}}$ where the circular velocity peaks (book, Exercise 3.3, p. 56). The book's exercise (part A here) is worked in the lesson [Dark matter halos](/courses/fundamentals-of-dark-matter/dark-matter-halos), worked example "Mass and rotation curve of an NFW halo".

<Part label="A" points={5}>

Do the book's exercise, then give $r_{v,\text{max}}/r_S$ for the NFW profile (tolerance 0.5%) and for the Hernquist profile (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

$M(r) = 4\pi\int_0^r\rho\,r'^2\,dr'$; with $r' = r_S x'$ the integrand becomes $x'/(1 + x')^\beta$. The circular velocity peaks where $M(r)/r$ does.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e3-nfw" label="NFW, $r_{v,\text{max}}/r_S$:" value="2.163" tolerance="0.5%" calc="e3-xmax-nfw" />

<Answer id="e3-hern" label="Hernquist, $r_{v,\text{max}}/r_S$:" value="1" tolerance="1%" calc="e3-xmax-hern" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The outer slope decides: $r^{-3}$ puts the same mass in every factor of $r$ (a logarithm), $r^{-4}$ puts less and less (a finite total).

<Step title="The enclosed masses">

With $M_0 = 4\pi\rho_0 r_S^3$:

<Formula label="Enclosed mass, NFW and Hernquist" tex="M_\text{NFW}(x) = M_0\left[\ln(1 + x) - \frac{x}{1 + x}\right], \qquad M_\text{H}(x) = \frac{M_0}{2}\,\frac{x^2}{(1 + x)^2}" cite="mit" at="Exercise 3.3, p. 56 (our own solution)" />

As $x \to \infty$ the NFW bracket grows like $\ln x - 1$: without limit, but slowly. The Hernquist mass tends to $M_0/2 = 2\pi\rho_0 r_S^3$.

</Step>

<Step title="The peaks">

$v_c^2 = GM(r)/r$. For NFW, $d/dx$ of $[\ln(1 + x) - x/(1 + x)]/x$ vanishes where $\ln(1 + x) = x/(1 + x) + x^2/(1 + x)^2$, at $x = 2.163$ (by a root finder). For Hernquist, $v_c^2 \propto x/(1 + x)^2$, whose derivative $(1 - x)/(1 + x)^3$ vanishes at $x = 1$: $r_{v,\text{max}} = r_S$ exactly.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Differentiating $M_\text{NFW}$ gives back $M_0\,x/(1 + x)^2$, which is $4\pi r^2\rho$ in these units; likewise $d M_\text{H}/dx = M_0\,x/(1 + x)^3$. At $x = 2.163$ the NFW bracket is 0.4677 and the bracket over $x$ is 0.2162, larger than at $x = 2$ (0.2160) or $x = 2.5$ (0.2154).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="B" points={10}>

(Our addition.) The book also defines the effective radius $r_e$, the radius inside which half the mass lies. Find $r_e/r_S$ for the Hernquist profile (half its total mass; tolerance 0.5%). The NFW profile has no total mass, so take a halo truncated at $r_h = c\,r_S$ with $c = 5$ (the concentration of Exercise 3.5) and find $r_e/r_S$ for half the mass inside $r_h$ (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

For Hernquist, $x^2/(1 + x)^2 = \frac12$ is a quadratic in disguise: take the square root first.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For NFW, solve $\ln(1 + x) - x/(1 + x) = \frac12\left[\ln 6 - \frac56\right]$ with a root finder (or bisection by hand), between $x = 1$ and $x = 5$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e3-re-hern" label="Hernquist, $r_e/r_S$:" value="2.414" tolerance="0.5%" calc="e3-re-hern" />

<Answer id="e3-re-nfw" label="NFW with $c = 5$, $r_e/r_S$:" value="2.217" tolerance="1%" calc="e3-re-nfw" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Set the enclosed mass equal to half the total (or half the mass inside the truncation radius) and solve for $x$.

<Step title="Hernquist">

$x/(1 + x) = 1/\sqrt2$ gives $x = 1/(\sqrt2 - 1) = 1 + \sqrt2 = 2.414$.

</Step>

<Step title="NFW truncated at c = 5">

The mass inside $r_h$ is $M_0[\ln 6 - 5/6] = 0.9584\,M_0$; half of it is $0.4792\,M_0$, reached at $x = 2.217$, so $r_e = 2.217\,r_S = 0.443\,r_h$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Hernquist: $2.414^2/3.414^2 = 5.828/11.657 = 0.500$. NFW: the NFW $r_e$ for $c = 5$ is close to its $r_{v,\text{max}} = 2.163\,r_S$; that is a coincidence of this concentration, since $r_{v,\text{max}}$ does not depend on $c$ but $r_e$ does (a more concentrated halo, truncated further out in units of $r_S$, has its half-mass radius further out too). The NFW half-mass radius depends on where the halo is cut, the Hernquist one does not: another face of the logarithmic divergence.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Exercise 3.4

<Exercise id="e4" title="How fast does a halo with spin parameter λ rotate?" points={20} mit="Exercise 3.4">

The spin parameter of a halo of angular momentum $L$, energy $E$ and mass $M$ is

<Formula label="Spin parameter" tex="\lambda = \frac{L\,|E|^{1/2}}{G\,M^{5/2}}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.6), p. 62" />

Model a halo as a uniform sphere of mass $M$ and radius $R$, rotating rigidly with angular velocity $\omega$. Find the ratio of $\omega$ to the angular velocity $\omega_0$ it would have if it were fully supported by rotation, and compare that ratio with $\lambda$ (book, Exercise 3.4, p. 62).

<Part label="A" points={6}>

Our reading of "fully supported by rotation": at the equator, the centrifugal acceleration balances gravity. Find $\omega_0$ in terms of $G$, $M$ and $R$.

<Hint>

At the surface, gravity gives the acceleration $GM/R^2$ towards the centre. A point on the equator moving in a circle of radius $R$ with angular velocity $\omega_0$ needs which centripetal acceleration?

</Hint>

<Answer id="e4-w0" label="$\omega_0 =$" expr="sqrt(G*M/R^3)"
  vars={{ G: [0.5, 2], M: [1, 10], R: [3, 5] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(G*M/R)", hint: "Check the units: that is a speed (the circular speed at the surface). Divide by R to turn it into an angular velocity." },
    { expr: "sqrt(2*G*M/R^3)", hint: "A factor 2 has crept in: that would be the escape speed divided by R. A circular orbit needs only the speed whose square is GM/R." },
    { expr: "G*M/R^3", hint: "Check the units: this is the square of an angular velocity. Take the square root." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The equator of a body rotating at its break-up rate is in circular orbit.

<Step title="The balance">

$\omega_0^2R = GM/R^2$, so

<Formula label="Angular velocity at full rotational support" tex="\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R^3}}" cite="mit" at="Exercise 3.4, p. 62 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Inside a uniform sphere gravity grows in proportion to the distance from the centre, $g = GMr/R^3$, so $\omega_0^2r = GMr/R^3$ holds at every distance in the equatorial plane, not only at the surface: rigid rotation at $\omega_0$ supports the whole equatorial plane. Away from that plane, the centrifugal force points away from the rotation axis and not away from the centre, so the sphere could never be supported by rotation alone everywhere. The units: $\sqrt{\text{m}^3\,\text{s}^{-2}/\text{m}^3}$ = 1/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="B" points={10}>

Our reading of $E$: the halo is in equilibrium, so by the virial theorem its total energy is half its potential energy. Show that $\lambda$ is then a fixed number $C$ times $\omega/\omega_0$, and give $C$ (tolerance 1%). For the typical simulated halo, $\lambda \approx 0.04$ (book, p. 62): what is $\omega/\omega_0$ (tolerance 1%)?

<Hint>

A uniform sphere has the moment of inertia $\frac25MR^2$ and the potential energy $-\frac35GM^2/R$ (stated by us).

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put $L = \frac25MR^2\omega$ and $|E| = \frac3{10}GM^2/R$ into the definition, and collect $\omega R^{3/2}/\sqrt{GM}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e4-c" label="$C =$" value="0.219" tolerance="1%" calc="e4-c" />

<Answer id="e4-ratio" label="$\omega/\omega_0$ for $\lambda = 0.04$:" value="0.183" tolerance="1%" calc="e4-ratio" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Every quantity in $\lambda$ is a power of $G$, $M$ and $R$ times $\omega$; the powers combine into $\omega/\omega_0$.

<Step title="The spin parameter of the sphere">

$L = \frac25MR^2\omega$ and $|E| = \frac12 \cdot \frac35\frac{GM^2}{R} = \frac3{10}\frac{GM^2}{R}$. Then

<Formula label="Spin parameter of a rigidly rotating uniform sphere" tex="\lambda = \frac{\frac25MR^2\omega\,\sqrt{\frac{3}{10}}\,G^{1/2}MR^{-1/2}}{GM^{5/2}} = \frac25\sqrt{\frac{3}{10}}\;\omega\sqrt{\frac{R^3}{GM}} = \frac25\sqrt{\frac{3}{10}}\;\frac{\omega}{\omega_0}" cite="mit" at="Exercise 3.4, p. 62 (our own solution)" />

so $C = \frac25\sqrt{0.3} = 0.219$.

</Step>

<Step title="A typical halo">

$\omega/\omega_0 = \lambda/C = 0.04/0.219 = 0.183$: the halo turns at less than a fifth of its break-up rate.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$\lambda$ is dimensionless, and so is $\omega/\omega_0$, so a pure number had to come out. Had we used the potential energy itself for $E$ (not the virial total), $C$ would be $\frac25\sqrt{0.6} = 0.310$ and $\omega/\omega_0 = 0.129$: the conclusion, a slowly turning halo, does not depend on that choice.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="C" points={4}>

Compare: what does $\lambda$ of a fully rotation-supported sphere come out as in this model, and how does that compare with the book's remark that rotation-supported systems have $\lambda \sim 1$? Then (our addition) find the fraction of the halo's kinetic energy that is in rotation when $\lambda = 0.04$, as a decimal (tolerance 1%). By the virial theorem the total kinetic energy is $\frac3{10}GM^2/R$.

<Hint>

The rotational kinetic energy of a rigid body is $\frac12I\omega^2$. Write $\omega^2$ as $(\omega/\omega_0)^2\,GM/R^3$.

<Answer id="e4-krot" label="Rotational fraction of the kinetic energy:" value="0.0222" tolerance="1%" calc="e4-krot" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $\lambda$ is roughly the ratio of the actual rotation to the rotation needed for support, up to a factor of order one that depends on the shape and the mass distribution.

<Step title="Full support">

With $\omega = \omega_0$, $\lambda = C = 0.22$ in this model. That is "of order one" in the loose sense the book means: it is five times the typical halo's 0.04, and the ratio $\omega/\omega_0$ is the meaningful comparison. (A rotating disc, in which all the mass is on circular orbits, needs a larger $\lambda$ than a uniform sphere for full support; our model is the book's simple one.)

</Step>

<Step title="The energy in rotation">

$K_\text{rot} = \frac12 \cdot \frac25MR^2\omega^2 = \frac15\frac{GM^2}{R}\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_0}\right)^2$. Divided by the total kinetic energy $\frac3{10}GM^2/R$:

<Formula label="Rotational share of the kinetic energy" tex="\frac{K_\text{rot}}{K} = \frac23\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_0}\right)^2 = \frac23\left(\frac{\lambda}{C}\right)^2" cite="mit" at="Exercise 3.4, p. 62 (our own addition)" />

For $\lambda = 0.04$: $(\lambda/C)^2 = 0.0016/0.048 = 0.0333$, and $K_\text{rot}/K = 0.0222$, about 2%.

<Step title="Check">

Even at $\omega = \omega_0$ the share is only $\frac23$: the rest of the kinetic energy must be random motion, because rigid rotation supports only the equatorial plane (part A). For a typical halo about 98% of the kinetic energy is random motion: halos are "hot", pressure-supported systems, as the lesson says.

## Exercise 3.5

<Exercise id="e5" title="Adiabatic contraction of an NFW halo around a Hernquist galaxy" points={25} mit="Exercise 3.5">

When baryons sink slowly to the centre of a halo, each spherical shell of dark matter keeps $r\,M(r)$ fixed (circular orbits, no shell crossing). The standard prescription compares the initial and final states:

<Formula label="Adiabatic contraction" tex="r_i\,M_i(r_i) = r_f\left[M_g(r_f) + M_\text{dm}(r_f)\right], \qquad M_\text{dm}(r_f) = (1 - f_d)\,M_i(r_i)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.7), p. 63" />

where $M_i$ is the initial enclosed mass (dark matter and baryons mixed), $M_g$ and $M_\text{dm}$ the final enclosed masses of the galaxy and the dark matter, and $f_d$ the fraction of dissipative (baryonic) mass. Apply it to an NFW halo of unit mass and concentration $c = 5$, with a galaxy that follows a Hernquist profile with the same scale radius, and $f_d = 0.1$. Solve the equation numerically and compare the rotation curves before and after contraction (book, Exercise 3.5, p. 64).

Our reading of the units and the galaxy: $G = 1$, $r_h = 1$, and the initial mass inside $r_h$ is 1, so $r_S = 0.2$ and

<Formula label="Initial enclosed mass of the halo" tex="M_i(r) = \frac{\ln(1 + r/r_S) - \dfrac{r/r_S}{1 + r/r_S}}{\ln(1 + c) - \dfrac{c}{1 + c}}" cite="mit" at="Exercise 3.5, p. 64 (our own normalisation)" />

All the baryons end up in the galaxy, whose total mass is $f_d$: $M_g(r) = f_d\,r^2/(r + a)^2$, with $a = r_S$ (our choice of normalisation). Circular velocities are then $v_c = \sqrt{M(r)/r}$, in units of $\sqrt{GM_h/r_h}$ (1 at $r_h$ before contraction).

<Part label="A" points={15}>

A dark matter shell starts at $r_i = 0.1$. Where does it end up, $r_f$ (tolerance 1%)? Then find the ratio of the final to the initial circular velocity, $v_f/v_i$, at the radius $r = 0.05$ (tolerance 0.5%) and at $r = 0.7$ (tolerance 0.5%). Describe how contraction changes the rotation curve.

<Hint>

For a given $r_i$, Eq. (3.7) is one equation for one unknown, $r_f$: $r_f[M_g(r_f) + (1 - f_d)M_i(r_i)] - r_iM_i(r_i) = 0$. It has a root between 0 and $r_i$ when the shell contracts. A fixed-point iteration also converges here: put a guess for $r_f$ into $M_g$, compute $r_iM_i(r_i)/[M_g(r_f) + (1 - f_d)M_i(r_i)]$ as the next guess, and repeat.

</Hint>

<Hint>

To find the final velocity at a given radius $r$, solve the same equation for the $r_i$ whose shell lands at $r_f = r$. Then the final mass inside $r$ is $r_iM_i(r_i)/r$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e5-rf" label="$r_f$ for $r_i = 0.1$:" value="0.0961" tolerance="1%" calc="e5-rf" />

<Answer id="e5-v005" label="$v_f/v_i$ at $r = 0.05$:" value="1.071" tolerance="0.5%" calc="e5-v005" />

<Answer id="e5-v07" label="$v_f/v_i$ at $r = 0.7$:" value="0.983" tolerance="0.5%" calc="e5-v07" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The galaxy is more concentrated than the baryons were when they were mixed with the halo, so inner shells feel more mass and move in; $rM$ stays fixed, so $v_c^2 = rM/r^2$ rises where shells move in.

<Step title="The shell from 0.1">

$M_i(0.1) = [\ln 1.5 - 1/3]/[\ln 6 - 5/6] = 0.07213/0.9584 = 0.07526$. Solving $r_f[0.1\,r_f^2/(r_f + 0.2)^2 + 0.9 \times 0.07526] = 0.1 \times 0.07526$ gives $r_f = 0.0961$: the shell shrinks by about 4%.

</Step>

<Step title="The rotation curve">

The shell that lands at $r = 0.05$ started at $r_i = 0.0526$; the final mass inside 0.05 is $r_iM_i(r_i)/0.05$, and $v_f = 0.744$ against $v_i = 0.695$: a ratio of 1.071. At $r = 0.7$ the shell started at $r_i = 0.687$, slightly inside: it has moved **out**, and $v_f/v_i = 0.983$.

</Step>

<Step title="What changes">

The inner rotation curve rises more steeply (7% faster at $0.05\,r_h$, 2% at $0.2\,r_h$), and the outer curve drops a little. The peak moves in, from $0.43\,r_h$ (the NFW $2.163\,r_S$ of Exercise 3.3) to about $0.36\,r_h$, with almost the same height. The outer shells move out because the Hernquist galaxy, with total mass $f_d$, puts part of its mass beyond $r_h$ (only $(5/6)^2$, or 69%, of it lies inside $r_h$), so the outer shells enclose less mass than before. With this normalisation the effect is mild: the galaxy, with $a = r_S$, is not much more concentrated than the baryons were. The size of the effect depends on how compact the galaxy is (part B) and on how the "unit mass" is normalised, which the book does not spell out; do not expect our numbers to match the book's figure.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Put $r_f = 0.0961$ back in: $0.0961 \times [0.1 \times 0.0961^2/0.2961^2 + 0.06773] = 0.0961 \times 0.07827 = 0.00752 = 0.1 \times 0.07526$. Far out, as $r_i$ grows, $M_g \to f_d$ and $M_i \to 1$ near $r_h$, and $r_f/r_i \to 1$ up to the few percent caused by the galaxy mass outside $r_h$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
