All the evidence for dark matter so far is gravitational, which constrains only its mass and distribution. Non-gravitational detections have all failed. Most experiments were designed around the WIMP miracle, looking for weak-strength interactions of particles near 100 GeV. Ferreras's Section 6.6 describes the three main strategies (**direct**, **indirect** and **production**) plus searches for axions.

At the heart of every method is the **cross-section** $\sigma$ of the interaction between a dark matter particle $\chi$ and some Standard Model particle $X$: the number of events is proportional to it. For known particles, detectors measure fluxes and energies; for dark matter the field is still at "square one": each experiment's first result is an **upper limit** on the cross-section, which already rules out many candidates.

## Direct detection

A dark matter particle can **scatter** off a nucleus in a detector, $\chi + N \to \chi + N$, making it recoil. (Inelastic scattering into a heavier dark state is also conceivable.) For slow, non-relativistic particles, an elastic collision gives a recoil energy

<Formula label="Recoil energy" tex="E_R = \frac{\mu^2 v^2}{m_N}\,(1 - \cos\theta)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (6.14), p. 151" />

with $\mu = m_\chi m_N/(m_\chi + m_N)$ the reduced mass, $v$ the dark matter speed and $\theta$ the scattering angle in the centre-of-mass frame. Its maximum ($\theta = 180°$) is $2\mu^2v^2/m_N$, which for typical halo speeds is

<Formula label="Typical maximum recoil energy" tex="E_R \sim 50\ \text{keV}\left(\frac{m_\chi}{100\ \text{GeV}}\right)^2\left(\frac{100\ \text{GeV}}{m_N}\right)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (6.11), p. 149" />

(a rough scaling; the exact prefactor depends on the halo speed assumed). Recoils are detected as **heat**, phonons in a crystal cooled almost to absolute zero (germanium and silicon in CDMS and SuperCDMS), or as **scintillation** light in a large mass of liquid xenon (LUX and its successor LZ, with hundreds of kilograms or more). Thresholds of about 1 keV set the lightest detectable particles.

<WorkedExample title="How hard does a WIMP hit?">
<Problem>
A 100 GeV dark matter particle moving at 230 km/s collides head-on with a xenon nucleus of mass about 122 GeV. Find the recoil energy.
</Problem>
<Step title="Reduced mass">
$\mu = 100 \times 122/(100 + 122) \approx 55$ GeV.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed in units of c">
$v/c = 230/299\,792 \approx 7.7 \times 10^{-4}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Recoil energy">
$E_R = 2\mu^2(v/c)^2/m_N = 2 \times 55^2 \times (7.7 \times 10^{-4})^2/122$ GeV $\approx 2.9 \times 10^{-5}$ GeV, about **29 keV**. Most collisions are glancing and give less, so detectors must register recoils of a few keV.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The **event rate per unit detector mass** is $R \approx \rho_\chi\sigma\langle v\rangle/(A\,m_p\,m_\chi)$, with $\rho_\chi$ the local dark matter density and $A$ the mass number of the target nucleus. Heavy, inert nuclei and large detector masses are best. The cross-section has a **spin-independent** part, which grows as $A^2$ because the particle scatters coherently off the whole nucleus, and a **spin-dependent** part that does not. A better rate calculation includes the spread of dark matter speeds, usually taken as **Maxwellian** with dispersion $\sigma_v$:

<Formula label="Maxwellian velocity distribution" tex="f(\vec v) = \frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma_v^2)^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{v^2}{2\sigma_v^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (6.12), p. 151" />

[The book writes the one-dimensional normalisation $1/(\sigma_v\sqrt{2\pi})$; for an integral over $d^3v$, as in the next equation, the three-dimensional factor shown here is the one that integrates to 1.]

<Formula label="Scattering rate" tex="R = \frac{\rho_\chi\,\sigma}{A\,m_p\,m_\chi}\int_{v_\text{min}}^{v_\text{max}} v\,f(\vec v)\,d^3v" cite="mit" at="Eq. (6.13), p. 151" />

The upper limit is the local **escape speed** from the Galaxy, about 500 km/s. The lower limit is the slowest particle able to give a detectable recoil, $v_\text{min} = \sqrt{m_N E_\text{th}/(2\mu^2)}$ for a threshold energy $E_\text{th}$. The sensitivity curves of all such experiments share a shape: a sharp loss at low masses (recoils fall below threshold) and a slow loss at high masses (fewer particles, $n_\chi = \rho_\chi/m_\chi$).

<WorkedExample title="Why detectors weigh tonnes">
<Problem>
Take a local dark matter density of 0.3 GeV/cm³ in particles of 100 GeV, moving at about $2.3 \times 10^7$ cm/s, and a cross-section with a xenon nucleus of $10^{-42}$ cm². How many events per kilogram per year? And per tonne per year?
</Problem>
<Step title="Particle density">
$n_\chi = 0.3/100 = 3 \times 10^{-3}$ per cm³.
</Step>
<Step title="Nuclei per kilogram">
A kilogram of xenon (about 131 g/mol) holds $1000/131 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} \approx 4.6 \times 10^{24}$ nuclei.
</Step>
<Step title="Rate">
$R = n_\chi\sigma v N = 3 \times 10^{-3} \times 10^{-42} \times 2.3 \times 10^7 \times 4.6 \times 10^{24} \approx 3.2 \times 10^{-13}$ per second per kg, about $10^{-5}$ per kg per year, or about **one event per hundred tonne-years**. Only huge, very quiet detectors can reach such cross-sections.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

**The neutrino floor.** Neutrinos scattering coherently off nuclei produce recoils indistinguishable from dark matter. At low masses this background comes from solar neutrinos (from ⁷Be and ⁸B), with a drop near 10 GeV; above that, from supernova neutrinos and atmospheric neutrinos made by cosmic rays. It is the ultimate limit of the technique. Detectors are placed **deep underground** to suppress the other backgrounds: electron recoils from beta and gamma radiation, and nuclear recoils from cosmic rays and local radioactivity.

**Annual modulation.** The detector moves through the halo. Seen from the laboratory the velocity distribution is shifted,

<Formula label="Laboratory-frame distribution" tex="f_\text{LAB}(\vec v) = f_\text{GLX}(\vec v + \vec v_\odot + \vec v_\oplus)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (6.15), p. 152" />

with the Sun's motion around the Galaxy, $v_\odot \approx 200$ km/s (constant on human timescales), and the Earth's orbital motion, $v_\oplus \approx 30$ km/s, which adds to or subtracts from it during the year. The rate then varies as

<Formula label="Annual modulation" tex="R = R_0 + R_1\cos\left[\omega(t - t_0)\right], \qquad \omega = \frac{2\pi}{1\ \text{yr}}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (6.16), p. 152" />

A detectable $R_1$ would set dark matter apart from backgrounds. The **DAMA/LIBRA** experiment, using thallium-doped sodium iodide crystals, reported such a modulation around the turn of the millennium, but it has not been reproduced; taken at face value it implies a light particle (1–10 GeV) with a large cross-section (about $10^{-40}$ cm²), in conflict with other experiments such as CDMS and LUX. Seasonal effects in the apparatus could mimic the signal; new experiments (SABRE) will test it. The Earth's rotation should add a smaller daily modulation too. Future multi-tonne xenon and tonne-scale germanium detectors will push down to the neutrino floor.

## Indirect detection

If dark matter particles are their own antiparticles, they can **annihilate**: $\chi + \chi \to \gamma + \gamma$, or into other particle–antiparticle pairs. If they are unstable, they can **decay**. Indirect searches look for the products where dark matter is densest (the centre of the Milky Way and dark-matter-dominated dwarf galaxies) and must rule out ordinary astrophysical sources. Photons keep their direction and are caught by space telescopes such as **Fermi-LAT**; charged cosmic rays are bent by magnetic fields; ground-based **Cherenkov telescopes** such as MAGIC and the Cherenkov Telescope Array see the showers of very energetic photons in the atmosphere.

Annihilation into two photons gives a **line** at an energy equal to the particle's rest energy (wavelength $\lambda_0 = h/m_\chi c$), broadened by the particles' motion:

<Formula label="Annihilation line" tex="F(\lambda) = \frac{F_0}{\sigma_\lambda\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left[-\frac12\left(\frac{\lambda - \lambda_0}{\sigma_\lambda}\right)^2\right]" cite="mit" at="Eq. (6.17), p. 154" />

with width $\sigma_\lambda \approx 2\lambda_0\,\sigma_v/c$: for the Milky Way's halo, a relative width of about $10^{-3}$. The total line flux grows as the integral of $n_\chi^2$ along the line of sight, since two particles must meet. Here $F$ counts photons: $F_0$ is the number of line photons arriving per unit area per unit time (gamma-ray astronomers quote photons cm⁻² s⁻¹), and $F(\lambda)$ is the same per unit wavelength. [The book gives no unit; this follows from $F_0$ counting annihilations.] Annihilation into heavier pairs ($W^+W^-$, $ZZ$, quark–antiquark) instead makes showers of pions and a broad spectrum of photons, much harder to separate from other sources. The event rates per unit volume are

<Formula label="Annihilation and decay rates" tex="\Gamma_a = n_\chi^2\,\langle\sigma v\rangle \quad\text{(annihilation)}, \qquad \Gamma_d = \frac{n_\chi}{\tau_\text{dec}} \quad\text{(decay)}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (6.18), p. 154" />

up to factors that depend on the channels. The thermal-relic benchmark $\langle\sigma v\rangle \sim 10^{-26}$ cm³/s sets the target flux. No convincing signal has been found.

## Production at colliders

A collider smashes Standard Model particles together; at high enough energy, dark matter pairs could be among the products. The Large Hadron Collider reaches 13 TeV, enough for WIMP-like masses, but the low interaction cross-section and the finite beam **luminosity** (collision rate per unit cross-section) limit how many would be made. For scale, Ferreras quotes that during the Higgs discovery the LHC reached a peak luminosity of about $2 \times 10^{34}$ cm⁻² s⁻¹, making about one Higgs boson per second, of which only about one in a thousand was detected. Dark matter would not be seen directly: a "detection" is **missing energy** in the collision products, and its interpretation depends on assumptions about what else was produced.

## Axion searches

Axions need a different trap. Because the QCD axion couples to gluons, and through mixing with mesons to photons, it can convert into a photon in a strong magnetic field. A **haloscope** is a resonant cavity inside a magnet of several tesla, tuned to the axion mass; the tiny microwave signal is read out with quantum-limited amplifiers based on SQUIDs (superconducting quantum interference devices). Several such experiments are running. **Helioscopes** look instead for axions made in the Sun, and stellar evolution already gives some of the strongest limits on axion properties.

## Summary

- Every search hinges on the dark matter–Standard Model cross-section; so far, experiments give only upper limits.
- Direct detection looks for nuclear recoils ($E_R = \mu^2v^2(1-\cos\theta)/m_N$, tens of keV) in cryogenic crystals or liquid xenon, deep underground. Rates are tiny (around one event per hundred tonne-years for $\sigma = 10^{-42}$ cm² per xenon nucleus), and the neutrino floor is the final limit. An annual modulation, $R_0 + R_1\cos[\omega(t-t_0)]$, is expected; DAMA/LIBRA's claim is unconfirmed.
- Indirect detection seeks annihilation ($\propto n_\chi^2\langle\sigma v\rangle$) or decay products (gamma-ray lines and cosmic rays) from the Galactic centre and dwarf galaxies.
- Colliders could produce dark matter, seen only as missing energy.
- Axions are sought with tuned microwave cavities in strong magnetic fields (haloscopes) and with helioscopes.
