This course follows Ignacio Ferreras's open textbook *Fundamentals of Dark Matter* (UCL Press, 2025). It adds what the MIT courses on this site leave out: the evidence that dark matter exists, what it might be made of, and the main alternative. Its first chapter sets the scene: how we learn about the universe at all, why gravity is the only tool that reveals dark matter, and why physicists are willing to postulate something they have never detected.

## How we learn about the sky

Almost everything we know about objects beyond the Earth arrives as light. Even the nearest body, the Moon, is about 60 Earth radii away, and sunlight travels some 23 500 Earth radii to reach us, through a vacuum emptier than any pump can make. Astronomy began with what the eye can see; astrophysics widened the window to radio, infrared, ultraviolet, and (from space, above the absorbing atmosphere) X-rays and gamma rays. From the light we measure energies and wavelengths (a **spectrum**, in detail by spectroscopy or in broad bands by photometry), and its polarisation tells us about magnetic fields at the source or along the way.

In recent decades other messengers have joined in: high-energy protons (cosmic rays), neutrinos from the Sun's core, and gravitational waves from colliding black holes and neutron stars. Yet one ingredient of the universe has avoided every one of these channels. It is all around us, yet no detector of any kind has registered it. That is **dark matter**.

## Four forces, and only one of them sees dark matter

Everything that happens in the universe is driven by four fundamental forces. Two of them, the strong and weak nuclear forces, act only over tiny distances (they explain nuclear fusion and the decay of the neutron). Electromagnetism works at every scale, from the energy levels of atoms to magnetic fields in galaxies. Gravity is the weakest, but it dominates over large distances: it governs planets, stars, galaxies and the universe as a whole.

Dark matter has shown itself **only through gravity**. The evidence in this course says that it is the dominant form of matter in the universe, yet so thinly spread, and interacting so weakly with anything else, that no laboratory has caught it.

## Missing things in the history of physics

To many people, dark matter sounds like a trick: when the theory fails, invent something invisible. But physics has done this before, sometimes with success and sometimes not.

- **Neptune.** In the nineteenth century the planet Uranus (found by William Herschel in 1781) did not move quite as Newton's laws predicted. Rather than doubt Newton, John Couch Adams and Urbain Le Verrier calculated where an unseen planet would have to be, and Neptune was found there in 1846. A missing body turned out to be real.
- **Vulcan.** Mercury's orbit also showed an anomaly: it slowly precesses a little more than the other planets can explain. A new inner planet, named Vulcan, was proposed, but it does not exist. The answer came in 1915 from general relativity, which changes Newtonian gravity slightly near the Sun. Here the law, not the inventory, was wrong. (Ferreras notes that supporters of modified gravity, Chapter 7, find this case encouraging.)
- **Planet X.** Supposed irregularities in Neptune's motion led to the search for a further planet. It was not there, but the search by Clyde Tombaugh found the much smaller Pluto in 1930.
- **The neutrino.** In beta decay a nucleus emits an electron, for example carbon-14 turning into nitrogen-14. If only two particles came out, the electron would always carry the same energy; instead it shows a whole range of energies up to a maximum. In 1930 Wolfgang Pauli proposed an extra neutral, nearly massless particle to carry the rest:

<Formula label="Beta decay of carbon-14" tex="{}^{14}_{\;6}\mathrm{C} \;\to\; {}^{14}_{\;7}\mathrm{N} + e^- + \bar\nu_e" cite="mit" at="Eq. (1.1), p. 6" />

  It was detected only in 1956, by Clyde Cowan and Frederick Reines, using the antineutrinos from a nuclear reactor, a quarter of a century of waiting.
- **The Sun's energy.** For decades stellar physics worked except for one gap: what powers the stars. Nuclear physics later filled it with the fusion of hydrogen into helium.

Dark matter was first proposed in the 1930s. We are still in the waiting period: neither found, like Neptune, nor explained away, like Vulcan. Both kinds of answer remain possible, which is why this course also covers the alternative (Chapter 7).

## Weighing the Sun with the planets

Gravity is the tool, so it is worth seeing how it weighs things. Take the planets on circular orbits of radius $r$ around the Sun, whose mass $M$ is so much larger that it stays put. Newton's law of gravitation gives the attraction between two masses $M$ and $m$:

<Formula label="Newton's law of gravitation" tex="\vec F = -\,G\,\frac{M m}{r^2}\,\hat e_r" cite="mit" at="Eq. (1.2), p. 7" />

The minus sign and the unit vector $\hat e_r$ say that the force points from the planet towards the Sun. On a circle the planet's speed stays constant while its direction turns, and the gravitational acceleration must supply exactly the centripetal acceleration $v^2/r$:

<Formula label="Circular orbital speed" tex="a = \frac{GM}{r^2} = \frac{v^2}{r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v^2 = \frac{GM}{r}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (1.3), p. 8" />

This innocent equation is the root of almost every mass measurement in astronomy. The speed of an orbit, together with its size, tells us the mass inside it. With the period $T = 2\pi r / v$, it becomes Kepler's third law in the form $M = 4\pi^2 r^3/(G T^2)$.

Ferreras applies it to all eight planets, using their orbital radii and periods (Table 1.1 of the book, from NASA's fact sheet). Each planet should give the same mass, the Sun's, and they do: the average is $(2.00 \pm 0.02) \times 10^{30}$ kg, close to the accurate value of $1.98847 \times 10^{30}$ kg. Only Neptune stands a little apart, which a more careful treatment (elliptical orbits, the pull of the other planets) removes. The graph shows the same thing as speeds.

Two lessons follow. First, the method works so well that **dark matter plays no noticeable role in the Solar System**: Newtonian gravity with the visible Sun explains every orbit. Second, a falling curve like this is what a central mass produces. In galaxies, as the next chapter shows, the curve does *not* fall, and that is the strongest sign of dark matter.

The same idea extends beyond circular orbits. For a galaxy whose stars move randomly with a typical speed $\sigma$ (the velocity dispersion), more complete models give $\sigma^2 \approx \alpha\, G M_g / R_g$, with $M_g$ and $R_g$ the galaxy's mass and size and $\alpha$ a number of order one, the same structure as the circular-orbit formula.

<WorkedExample title="Weighing Jupiter with its moon Io">
<Problem>
Io orbits Jupiter at an average distance of 421 800 km, once every 1.769 days (NASA's Jovian satellite fact sheet). Treating the orbit as a circle, find Jupiter's mass.
</Problem>
<Step title="Convert to SI units">
$r = 4.218 \times 10^8$ m and $T = 1.769 \times 86\,400\ \text{s} = 1.528 \times 10^5$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Apply the orbit formula">
$M = \dfrac{4\pi^2 r^3}{G T^2} = \dfrac{4\pi^2 \times (4.218 \times 10^8)^3}{6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times (1.528 \times 10^5)^2} \approx 1.90 \times 10^{27}$ kg.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
This is about one thousandth of the Sun's mass, as expected for Jupiter. Io's own mass is negligible here (about 0.005% of Jupiter's), just as the planets' masses are negligible next to the Sun's.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A density from cosmology

A second route to dark matter, followed in Chapter 5 (on this site mostly in 8.286 and 8.902), comes from cosmology. For a homogeneous, isotropic universe, general relativity singles out a **critical density**: the density that separates a closed universe from an open one.

<Formula label="Critical density today" tex="\rho_c = \frac{3 H_0^2}{8\pi G}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (1.4), p. 10" />

Here $H_0$ is today's value of the Hubble parameter $H(t)$, "constant" only in the sense that it is the same everywhere at a given time; it changes as the universe ages, and so does the critical density. Its value is still debated but close to 70 km/s per Mpc: two galaxies 10 Mpc apart separate at about 700 km/s. With this value, $\rho_c \approx 9.2 \times 10^{-27}$ kg/m³, roughly five hydrogen atoms per cubic metre.

Measurements put the average density of dark matter at about a quarter of this. Such a low density means that dark matter is gravitationally irrelevant in any laboratory, or even across the Solar System. Astrophysics is a science of extremes: a cubic centimetre of white-dwarf material holds about a tonne, and neutron stars are denser still, like atomic nuclei. Dark matter sits at the other end: averaged over the universe, the dark matter adding up to the mass of one person (70 kg) fills about $3.0 \times 10^{28}$ m³, a cube some 3.1 million km on a side, about eight times the distance to the Moon.

<WorkedExample title="Emptier than any vacuum">
<Problem>
A good laboratory vacuum reaches a pressure of $10^{-9}$ Pa at room temperature (293 K). Compare its number density of particles with interstellar gas (about one hydrogen atom per cm³) and with dark matter particles of mass 100 GeV/$c^2$ (about $1.78 \times 10^{-25}$ kg), taking the full critical density as a round upper figure (dark matter itself is about a quarter of it).
</Problem>
<Step title="The laboratory vacuum">
For an ideal gas $n = P/(kT) = 10^{-9} / (1.381 \times 10^{-23} \times 293) \approx 2.5 \times 10^{11}$ m⁻³, or about 250 000 particles per cm³.
</Step>
<Step title="Dark matter">
$n = \rho_c / m = 9.2 \times 10^{-27} / 1.78 \times 10^{-25} \approx 0.052$ m⁻³, or about $5 \times 10^{-8}$ per cm³, one particle in every 20 cubic metres or so.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
Interstellar space is some 250 000 times emptier than the best laboratory vacuum, and dark matter particles of this mass would be about 20 million times sparser again. This is why dark matter can only be weighed over astronomical volumes.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Tackling the problem

Dark matter is a problem for the whole of physics. The first evidence came from extragalactic astronomy: the rotation of galaxies and the motions of galaxies in clusters. Cosmology soon added its own evidence from the abundance of light elements made in the Big Bang, the pattern of the cosmic microwave background, and the clustering of galaxies on large scales. At the other extreme of distance, particle physicists search for the particle itself, and theorists explore extensions of the Standard Model, or of general relativity. The range of possible explanations is enormous; the task is to rule out all but one with observations.

## Summary

- All evidence for dark matter is **gravitational**. It has never been detected through light, nuclear forces or in a laboratory.
- Physics has postulated unseen things before: some were real (Neptune, the neutrino), and some pointed instead to new laws (Vulcan → general relativity). Dark matter, proposed in the 1930s, is still waiting for its answer.
- Orbits weigh masses: $v^2 = GM/r$, or $M = 4\pi^2 r^3/(GT^2)$. The planets all give the Sun's mass, $(2.00 \pm 0.02) \times 10^{30}$ kg, so dark matter is negligible in the Solar System.
- The critical density $\rho_c = 3H_0^2/(8\pi G) \approx 9.2 \times 10^{-27}$ kg/m³ (for $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc), and dark matter makes up about a quarter of it: far too thin to notice except over astronomical distances.
