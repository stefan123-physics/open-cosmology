The core–cusp problem is one challenge to cold dark matter on small scales. Ferreras's Section 3.5 turns to the other: **substructure**, the halos that live inside larger halos.

## Halos within halos

In warm and hot dark matter models, fast-moving particles **free-stream** out of small density fluctuations and erase them. Cold dark matter particles move slowly, so fluctuations survive on **all scales**, and collapsed halos should exist down to the smallest masses. The fluctuations also have more power towards small scales (on this site: [power spectrum](/courses/8-902/power-spectrum)), so many small halos form and then fall into larger ones. Being small, they collapsed early and are **highly concentrated**, so they are not fully torn apart: they survive as **subhalos**. Satellite galaxies in clusters, for instance, keep their own subhalos as they move through the cluster's halo.

Simulations confirm an apparently endless hierarchy of ever smaller subhalos, down to the resolution limit set by the simulation's particle mass and gravitational softening (Chapter 4). A halo like the Milky Way's, simulated out to 250 kpc, is filled with them. The questions are how far down in mass galaxies follow halos, whether there are halos with no stars at all, **dark galaxies**, and how such objects could ever be detected.

## The missing satellites

The best place to look is our own Galaxy, and next the Andromeda galaxy. Observationally the task is to find faint, diffuse galaxies. Galaxies below about $10^9\,M_\odot$ are traditionally called **dwarfs**; those with stellar masses below about $10^5\,M_\odot$ are now called **ultra-faint dwarfs**. Studies that pick out individual stars in colour–magnitude diagrams have found about **50 satellite galaxies around the Milky Way** and about **30 around Andromeda**, some with only a few hundred solar masses in stars. Wide surveys, such as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (York and collaborators, 2000) and the Dark Energy Survey (2016), and targeted searches such as PAndAS around Andromeda (Huxor and collaborators, 2014) made these discoveries possible.

Simulations, however, predict **as many as 1000 subhalos** above the same limit. This mismatch of about a factor of twenty is, together with cores, among the most important challenges to the cold dark matter paradigm.

Comparing numbers needs care, because "the size of a halo" has several definitions: the virial radius (where $2K \approx -V$), the splashback radius, or $R_{200}$, inside which the mean density is 200 times the background. More precisely, the virial density contrast is about 178 at the redshifts where most galaxies form, rising to about 330 today. Masses follow suit ($M_\text{vir}$, $M_{200}$, …), and simulations add their own sampling limits. These estimates are comparable but not equal.

<Problem>
Before collapse, the matter in a halo occupied a sphere at the mean density. Find the radius of that sphere (in today's comoving units) for a Milky Way-sized halo of $10^{12}\,M_\odot$ and for a subhalo of $10^9\,M_\odot$, with mean matter density $\bar\rho = 4.08 \times 10^{10}\,M_\odot$ per Mpc³ ($H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc, $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.3$). Express each as a wavenumber $k = \pi/r$.
</Problem>
<Step title="The Milky Way halo">
$r = \left(\dfrac{3M}{4\pi\bar\rho}\right)^{1/3} = \left(\dfrac{3 \times 10^{12}}{4\pi \times 4.08 \times 10^{10}}\right)^{1/3}$ Mpc $\approx 1.8$ Mpc, so $k \approx 1.7$ Mpc⁻¹.
</Step>
<Step title="A subhalo">
A thousand times less mass means a radius ten times smaller: $r \approx 0.18$ Mpc, $k \approx 17$ Mpc⁻¹.
</Step>
<Step title="Meaning">
Galaxies like ours come from fluctuations of order a megaparsec, so the substructure inside them tests the power spectrum on scales below a megaparsec, exactly where cold, warm and self-interacting dark matter differ.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Solutions from the dark matter side

**Warm dark matter** can be tuned to erase fluctuations below about galaxy scales. A warm dark matter particle of **3 keV**, for example, would suppress halos below about $10^9\,M_\odot$, which host galaxies of up to a few times $10^7\,M_\odot$ in stars. A heavier particle allows smaller halos; the main effect is a **minimum halo mass**.

**Self-interacting dark matter** (SIDM) gives the particles a small cross-section for scattering off each other: small enough to keep dark matter collisionless on large scales and at low densities, but important in forming subhalos. Ferreras notes that it might even produce an observable signal, high-energy photons from the densest galaxy centres.

Both have a cost. By suppressing small structures today, warm and self-interacting dark matter also suppress them at early times, slowing galaxy formation, in tension with the fairly massive galaxies and quasars seen at redshifts above about 3.

Ferreras sketches, in one of his "How about…?" boxes, how self-interaction would make cores. If dark matter self-interacts it has a pressure, which can be modelled by a **polytropic** equation of state with index $n$ (an isothermal gas is the limit $n \to \infty$):

<Formula label="Polytropic equation of state" tex="P = K\rho^{1 + 1/n}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.15), p. 73" />

In spherical symmetry, hydrostatic equilibrium and the mass profile give

<Formula label="Hydrostatic equilibrium and mass" tex="\frac{dP}{dr} = -\frac{GM(r)\,\rho}{r^2}, \qquad \frac{dM}{dr} = 4\pi r^2\rho" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.16), p. 73" />

Writing $\rho = \rho_0\theta^n$ and $r = a\xi$ (with $a$ a combination of $K$, $n$ and the central density $\rho_0$), these combine into the **Lane–Emden equation**

<Formula label="Lane–Emden equation" tex="\frac{1}{\xi^2}\frac{d}{d\xi}\left(\xi^2\frac{d\theta}{d\xi}\right) = -\theta^n" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.17), p. 73" />

with $\theta(0) = 1$ and $\theta'(0) = 0$ at the centre, conditions that rule out a cusp. For $n = 5$ there is a closed solution:

<Formula label="Lane–Emden solution for n = 5" tex="\theta = \left(1 + \frac{\xi^2}{3}\right)^{-1/2}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.18), p. 74" />

<WorkedExample title="Checking the n = 5 solution (the book's exercise)">
<Problem>
Show that $\theta = (1 + \xi^2/3)^{-1/2}$ satisfies the Lane–Emden equation with $n = 5$, and that the central density is finite.
</Problem>
<Step title="Differentiate">
$\xi^2\theta' = -\tfrac{\xi^3}{3}(1 + \xi^2/3)^{-3/2}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Differentiate again">
Differentiating and dividing by $\xi^2$ gives $-(1 + \xi^2/3)^{-5/2} = -\theta^5$, as required. At $\xi = 0$, $\theta = 1$: the central density is $\rho_0$, finite: a **core**, not a cusp. (This solution never reaches zero density, so it has no edge; in general $n$ can be left free to fit galaxy data, as Sánchez-Almeida and collaborators did in 2021.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Solutions from the baryon side

Alternatively, keep cold dark matter and suppress **galaxy formation** in small subhalos. Observations would then agree with the simulations, at the price of a large population of **dark subhalos**, perhaps detectable through dynamics or gravitational lensing. In the shallow potential wells of small halos it is easy to stop star formation: **supernova feedback** can blow the gas out, and the **ultraviolet background** of the universe heats and ionises it. The same mechanisms can make cores where there are enough stars to move the dark matter (as on the previous page); in the smallest galaxies subhalos may keep their cusps but have too few stars to be seen. At the time of writing, detecting and characterising the halos of the faintest galaxies is at the forefront of research.

A related puzzle is **too big to fail**. Even among the most massive subhalos around the Milky Way (around $10^{10}\,M_\odot$), simulations and observations disagree. These subhalos are too massive for feedback to have prevented star formation (they are "too big to fail" to form stars), yet they do not match the satellites we see: in the usual statement of the problem, the most massive simulated subhalos are denser in their centres than any of the Milky Way's bright satellites.

## Summary

- Cold dark matter keeps fluctuations on all scales, so halos contain many concentrated subhalos; simulations show an apparently endless hierarchy down to their resolution.
- About 50 satellite galaxies are known around the Milky Way and 30 around Andromeda, against about 1000 predicted subhalos: the missing satellites problem.
- Halo sizes and masses depend on their definition (virial, splashback, $R_{200}$; virial contrast about 178 rising to 330 today). Substructure in a Milky Way halo (from a region about 1.8 Mpc in radius) probes the power spectrum below a megaparsec.
- Dark matter solutions: warm dark matter (3 keV suppresses halos below about $10^9\,M_\odot$) or self-interacting dark matter (with polytropic cores): both risk too few early galaxies.
- Baryonic solutions: supernova feedback and the ultraviolet background keep small subhalos dark. The "too big to fail" problem remains for the most massive subhalos (about $10^{10}\,M_\odot$).
