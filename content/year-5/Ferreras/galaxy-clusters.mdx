Rotation curves weigh single galaxies. Ferreras's next step is the largest gravitationally bound objects in the universe: **clusters of galaxies**, with hundreds or thousands of galaxies moving inside a common potential well, galaxies playing the part of planets around an oversized "Solar System". This is where dark matter was first proposed.

## Zwicky and the Coma cluster

In 1933 **Fritz Zwicky** measured the velocities of galaxies in the Coma cluster. Their spread was so large that the cluster could only hold together if its total mass were far larger than the mass of all the stars in its galaxies.

A cluster is a "hot" dynamical system: its galaxies move on random orbits, not in an ordered rotation. As for elliptical galaxies, the dynamical mass follows from the **velocity dispersion** $\sigma$ (the standard deviation of the galaxies' line-of-sight velocities) and the size $r$:

<Formula label="Dynamical mass of a pressure-supported system" tex="M_\text{dyn} \approx \alpha\,\frac{\sigma^2 r}{G}" cite="mit" at="Section 2.3, p. 23" />

Here $\alpha$ is a "fudge factor" of order one that hides the details of the density profile and the orbits. Even a rough estimate gives a gravitating mass about **ten times** the luminous mass.

<WorkedExample title="Weighing a cluster">
<Problem>
Take a cluster as a sphere of radius $R$ with an isothermal density $\rho \propto 1/r^2$ inside $R$ and nothing outside. Its galaxies have a one-dimensional velocity dispersion $\sigma$ in every direction. (a) Find $\alpha$. (b) A cluster has $\sigma = 800$ km/s and $R = 1.2$ Mpc, and its galaxies emit $5 \times 10^{12}\,L_\odot$. Find its mass and mass-to-light ratio.
</Problem>
<Step title="The virial theorem">
In equilibrium, twice the kinetic energy plus the potential energy is zero: $2K + W = 0$. With random motions in three directions, $K = \tfrac32 M\sigma^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="The potential energy">
For $\rho \propto 1/r^2$ the enclosed mass grows linearly, $M(r) = M r/R$, and the shell between $r$ and $r + dr$ holds $dm = (M/R)\,dr$. Then $W = -\int_0^R G M(r)\,dm / r = -\int_0^R G (M r/R)(M/R)\,dr / r = -GM^2/R$.
</Step>
<Step title="Solve for α">
$3M\sigma^2 = GM^2/R$ gives $M = 3\sigma^2 R/G$, so $\alpha = 3$.
</Step>
<Step title="The mass">
With $R = 1200$ kpc and $G = 4.30 \times 10^{-6}$ kpc (km/s)² $M_\odot^{-1}$: $M = 3 \times 800^2 \times 1200 / 4.30 \times 10^{-6} \approx 5.4 \times 10^{14}\,M_\odot$.
</Step>
<Step title="Mass-to-light ratio">
$M/L \approx 5.4 \times 10^{14} / 5 \times 10^{12} \approx 110$ in solar units. As the next page shows, stars give at most about 10. The galaxies' starlight accounts for only a small part of the mass.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The hot gas between the galaxies

After Zwicky's time, astronomers found that clusters hold a great deal of ordinary (baryonic) matter that is not in stars: hot, diffuse gas filling the space between the galaxies, the **intracluster medium** (ICM). Settled in the cluster's potential, it reaches temperatures of about $10^7$ K and more (thermal energies $kT$ of order a keV), with central densities around $10^{-3}$ particles per cm³. At such temperatures and densities its cooling time is very long, so it cannot cool and form stars; it stays spread through the cluster.

Such hot gas shines in **X-rays** by **bremsstrahlung** (free electrons deflected by ions). X-ray telescopes measure the gas density and temperature, including how they change with radius. The result is consistent: even with the intracluster gas included, ordinary matter falls far short of the dynamical mass.

## Two ways to weigh a cluster

Ferreras points out a neat parallel between weighing a cluster with its gas and with its galaxies.

**The gas.** Assume the gas is an ideal gas in **hydrostatic equilibrium**: at each radius its pressure gradient balances gravity. With gas density $\rho_g$, temperature $T$, mean particle mass $\mu m_p$ ($m_p$ the proton mass, $\mu \approx 0.6$ for ionised gas), the mass inside $r$ is

<Formula label="Hydrostatic mass" tex="M(r) = -\,\frac{r\,kT}{G\,\mu m_p}\left(\frac{d\ln\rho_g}{d\ln r} + \frac{d\ln T}{d\ln r}\right)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.7), p. 25" />

X-ray observations give maps of the luminosity $L_X$ (by photometry) and of the temperature $T_X$ (by fitting the spectrum). Bremsstrahlung emission scales as $L_X \propto n_g^2\,\Lambda(T) \propto n_g^2\,T^{1/2}$, so the maps yield the density and temperature profiles, and hence $M(r)$. If the gas is not in equilibrium, for example just after a merger of two clusters, this mass is biased; usually it comes out too low, because turbulence and bulk motions give extra support that the formula leaves out.

**The galaxies.** Galaxies hardly ever collide, so they behave as collisionless test particles in the cluster's potential. The tool for such systems is the **Jeans equation**. With $\nu$ the number density of galaxies, $\sigma_r$ their velocity dispersion along the radius, and $\beta$ the orbital anisotropy:

<Formula label="Jeans mass estimate" tex="M(r) = -\,\frac{r\,\sigma_r^2}{G}\left(\frac{d\ln\nu}{d\ln r} + \frac{d\ln\sigma_r^2}{d\ln r} + 2\beta\right), \qquad \beta = 1 - \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2\sigma_r^2}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.8), p. 25" />

where $\sigma_t^2$ is the velocity dispersion in the two tangential directions together. The two formulas are almost identical under the analogy $kT/(\mu m_p) \leftrightarrow \sigma_r^2$: temperature is the gas's velocity dispersion. The anisotropy term appears only for galaxies: collisions keep the gas pressure the same in all directions, while galaxies may move mostly radially ($\beta > 0$) or mostly tangentially ($\beta < 0$). The role of hydrostatic equilibrium is played by **dynamical equilibrium**: a steady state in which the distribution of positions and velocities does not change with time.

<WorkedExample title="A cluster weighed by its X-ray gas">
<Problem>
X-ray data for a cluster show gas at $T = 7 \times 10^7$ K (about 6 keV) at $r = 1$ Mpc, with roughly constant temperature there and a density falling as $\rho_g \propto r^{-2}$. Take $\mu = 0.6$. Find the mass inside 1 Mpc.
</Problem>
<Step title="The slopes">
$d\ln\rho_g/d\ln r = -2$ and $d\ln T/d\ln r = 0$, so the bracket is $-2$. (This factor 2 is the local density slope, not the global α = 3 of the previous example, which also folds in the virial theorem for the whole cluster.)
</Step>
<Step title="The mass">
$M = 2\,r\,kT/(G\mu m_p) = 2 \times 3.086 \times 10^{22} \times 1.381 \times 10^{-23} \times 7 \times 10^7 / (6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 0.6 \times 1.673 \times 10^{-27})$ kg $\approx 8.9 \times 10^{44}$ kg $\approx 4.5 \times 10^{14}\,M_\odot$.
</Step>
<Step title="The galaxy equivalent">
The gas temperature corresponds to a velocity dispersion $\sigma = \sqrt{kT/(\mu m_p)} \approx 980$ km/s, just what galaxies in a rich cluster show. Gas and galaxies feel the same potential.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Fritz Zwicky's 1933 study of the Coma cluster was the first evidence for dark matter: the galaxies move too fast for the visible mass.
- For a pressure-supported system, $M_\text{dyn} \approx \alpha\sigma^2 r/G$ with $\alpha$ of order one ($\alpha = 3$ for a truncated isothermal sphere). Typical clusters have $\sigma \sim 1000$ km/s, masses $\sim 10^{14}$–$10^{15}\,M_\odot$ and mass-to-light ratios of order 100.
- Clusters contain hot X-ray gas ($T \sim 10^7$–$10^8$ K, $n \sim 10^{-3}$ cm⁻³) that is too diffuse to form stars. Including it still leaves most of the mass missing.
- Gas (hydrostatic equilibrium, Eq. 2.7) and galaxies (Jeans equation, Eq. 2.8) weigh a cluster in the same way, with $kT/\mu m_p$ playing the part of $\sigma_r^2$; only the galaxies need an anisotropy term.
