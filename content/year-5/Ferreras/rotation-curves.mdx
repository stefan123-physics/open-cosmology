The previous page weighed the Sun with the planets. This page applies the same idea to galaxies. Stars and gas in a disc galaxy circle its centre, and their speed at each radius, the **rotation curve**, tells us how much mass lies inside. Ferreras calls the result the most direct evidence for dark matter: far out, where almost no stars or gas remain, the curve should fall like the planets' speeds, but it stays flat.

## Gravity as a potential

Dark matter shows itself on the scale of galaxies and beyond, from about 10 kpc upwards (a kiloparsec, kpc, is about 3 × 10¹⁹ m; the Sun is about 8 kpc from the centre of our Galaxy). The basic tool is Newtonian gravity, written as **Poisson's equation**: the gravitational potential $\Phi(\vec r)$ is sourced by the density $\rho(\vec r)$ of all matter, visible or not.

<Formula label="Poisson's equation" tex="\nabla^2 \Phi = 4\pi G \rho" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.1), p. 15" />

The potential holds everything about the gravitational field: the force on a particle of mass $m$ is $\vec F = -m\nabla\Phi$. For one test particle in a fixed field this is easy to solve. A real galaxy contains billions of stars that also create the field, so galaxy dynamics uses a statistical description instead: a **distribution function** counting the stars in each small volume of position and velocity. From it come "macroscopic" quantities (density, mean velocity, velocity dispersion) rather like temperature and pressure in a gas, and these are what observations connect to the density that makes the field. Ferreras's companion textbook (*Fundamentals of Galaxy Dynamics, Formation and Evolution*) treats this in full; here only the parts that weigh dark matter are needed.

## A spherical galaxy

For a spherically symmetric density, only the mass $M(r)$ inside radius $r$ matters (Newton's shell theorem). Poisson's equation then gives the potential

<Formula label="Potential of a spherical mass distribution" tex="\Phi(r) = -G\int\frac{\rho(\vec r\,')}{|\vec r\,' - \vec r|}\,d^3r' = -\frac{GM(<r)}{r}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.2), p. 15" />

[The last form holds where no mass lies outside $r$, for example for a test particle beyond the galaxy. Inside a spherical system the shells outside $r$ add to the potential, and in general $\Phi(r) = -G\int_r^\infty M(r')/r'^2\,dr'$; they exert no net force, so the acceleration below is unaffected.]

and the radial acceleration

<Formula label="Radial acceleration" tex="\vec a(r) = -\,\frac{G M(r)}{r^2}\,\hat e_r" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.3), p. 15" />

So the motion of a star depends only on the mass inside its own radius. If we can measure motions in the outer parts of a galaxy, we can weigh everything inside.

Real orbits come in all inclinations and shapes. Two extreme cases bracket them. In **pressure-supported** ("hot") systems the stars move in random directions, and their **velocity dispersion** plays the role of a temperature: elliptical galaxies are mostly like this. In **rotationally supported** ("cold") systems the stars and gas share an orderly rotation: disc galaxies. For a disc we can approximate the motion by circular orbits obeying the acceleration above.

## From the rotation curve to the mass

A circular orbit with acceleration $a$ has speed $v_c = \sqrt{a r}$. Combining this with the acceleration of a spherical distribution links the measured speed directly to the mass inside:

<Formula label="Mass from the rotation curve" tex="M(r) = 4\pi \int_0^r \rho(s)\,s^2\,ds = \frac{r\,v_c^2(r)}{G}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.4), p. 16" />

Three simple cases show what to expect:

1. **A point mass** (all mass at the centre): $M$ is constant, so $v_c \propto 1/\sqrt r$, the **Keplerian** fall of the planets.
2. **A uniform sphere**: $M \propto r^3$, so $v_c \propto r$, a linear rise, as for a rigid rotating body.
3. **An isothermal sphere**, $\rho \propto 1/r^2$: $M \propto r$, so $v_c = v_0$, constant, a **flat** curve.

Real disc galaxies usually rise linearly near the centre, then stay roughly flat, and sometimes decline slowly far out (though not in the Keplerian way).

The decisive observations, pioneered by **Vera Rubin**, found the flat part of the curve at large distances from the centre, in regions with very little gas or stars. Since $v_c^2 = GM(r)/r$ is linear in the mass, the contributions of the separate components add **in quadrature**: $v_c^2 = v_\text{stars}^2 + v_\text{gas}^2 + v_\text{halo}^2$. The visible components alone would give a Keplerian decline beyond the stars. The missing decline demands an extra component, and the flatness suggests it is roughly isothermal there. Chapter 3 shows that the real profile is more complicated, but the isothermal slope remains a useful anchor: it is what a self-gravitating system in the equivalent of thermal equilibrium looks like. (Complete equilibrium is in fact impossible for gravity: such a system keeps contracting, the "gravothermal catastrophe".)

<WorkedExample title="How much of a galaxy is dark?">
<Problem>
A disc galaxy has a rotation curve that stays flat at 180 km/s from 5 kpc out to 40 kpc, where the last gas clouds are seen. Its stars and gas, from their light and radio emission, add up to about $5 \times 10^{10}\,M_\odot$. How much mass lies within 40 kpc, and what fraction is dark?
</Problem>
<Step title="A convenient value of G">
In astronomical units, $G = 4.30 \times 10^{-6}$ kpc (km/s)² $M_\odot^{-1}$: speeds in km/s, distances in kpc, masses in solar masses.
</Step>
<Step title="Mass inside 40 kpc">
$M = r v_c^2 / G = 40 \times 180^2 / 4.30 \times 10^{-6} \approx 3.0 \times 10^{11}\,M_\odot$.
</Step>
<Step title="The dark fraction">
The visible $5 \times 10^{10}\,M_\odot$ is only about a sixth of this: about 83% of the mass inside 40 kpc is dark.
</Step>
<Step title="What the visible mass alone would do">
If only the visible mass were there, by 40 kpc it would act almost like a point mass, giving $v = \sqrt{GM/r} = \sqrt{4.30 \times 10^{-6} \times 5 \times 10^{10} / 40} \approx 73$ km/s, not the 180 km/s measured.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

For a real example, Ferreras takes the spiral galaxy NGC 2841, whose rotation curve stays roughly flat at about 300 km/s out to 35 kpc. The same formula gives about $7.3 \times 10^{11}\,M_\odot$. Its total luminosity in the V band is about $\log(L_V/L_\odot) \approx 10.85$ (from an absolute magnitude of −22.3, against 4.83 for the Sun), so its **mass-to-light ratio** is $\Upsilon = M/L_V \approx 10$ in solar units, more than any population of stars can provide, as the next pages show.

## Measuring a rotation curve

Speeds come from the **Doppler effect**. Light of wavelength $\lambda_0$ from a source moving at speed $v$ along the line of sight (small compared with $c$) is observed at

<Formula label="Doppler shift" tex="\lambda_\text{obs} = \lambda_0\left(1 + \frac{v}{c}\right)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.5), p. 19" />

where $v$ is positive for a receding source. The galaxy as a whole moves relative to us (through the cosmic expansion and its motion within its group); on top of that, its rotation makes one side approach and the other recede. Optical spectra show the starlight's continuum with absorption lines from stellar atmospheres, plus strong emission lines from ionised gas.

- **Long-slit spectroscopy**: a narrow slit across the galaxy's image, dispersed by a spectrograph, gives one cut through the rotation curve. Each emission line traces an **S shape**: shifted one way on one side, the other way on the other side, levelling off where the curve is flat.
- **Integral field units (IFUs)** take a spectrum at every point of the image. Together they make a two-dimensional map of line-of-sight velocity, which a model of circular rotation fits with a characteristic "spider" pattern.

<WorkedExample title="The spider pattern">
<Problem>
A disc rotates with speed $v(R)$ on circles of radius $R$, and its axis is tilted by an angle $i$ from our line of sight ($i = 0$ face-on, $i = 90°$ edge-on). Show that the line-of-sight velocity at azimuthal angle $\phi$ around the disc (measured from the major axis) is $v(R)\cos\phi\,\sin i$, after removing the galaxy's systemic velocity.
</Problem>
<Step title="Velocity in the disc plane">
Take $x$ along the major axis and $y$ along the minor axis, in the disc. A point at angle $\phi$ moves along the circle, perpendicular to its radius: its velocity is $v(R)\,(-\sin\phi,\ \cos\phi,\ 0)$.
</Step>
<Step title="Tilt the disc">
Tilting the disc by $i$ about the major axis turns the disc's $y$ direction partly towards us: a vector $(0, 1, 0)$ in the disc has a component $\sin i$ along the line of sight, while the $x$ direction (the tilt axis) has none.
</Step>
<Step title="Project">
The line-of-sight component is therefore $v(R)\cos\phi\,\sin i$. It is largest along the major axis ($\phi = 0$ or $180°$) and zero along the minor axis. For example, a disc at $i = 60°$ rotating at 200 km/s shows ±173 km/s at the ends of its major axis. Lines of equal velocity on the sky form the spider pattern.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Other tracers reach the outermost parts of galaxies: **planetary nebulae** (dying stars with bright, narrow emission lines such as [O III] at 5007 Å) and **globular clusters**. They are harder to interpret than rotating gas, because their orbits may be biased, for example mostly radial, with little rotation.

## The Tully–Fisher relation

Disc galaxies obey a striking scaling law. With $v_\text{max}$ the maximum speed of the rotation curve and $L_i$ the luminosity in a given passband $i$:

<Formula label="Tully–Fisher relation" tex="L_i \propto v_\text{max}^{\alpha_i}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.6), p. 22" />

The exponent $\alpha_i$ depends on the passband but is always close to 4. Converting luminosity to stellar mass with a mass-to-light ratio gives a passband-independent version, $M_s \propto v_\text{max}^\alpha$. Adding the gas mass gives the **baryonic Tully–Fisher relation** (McGaugh and collaborators, 2000), which holds even more tightly over a wide range of galaxy masses.

This tightness is a puzzle. If the rotation speed in the outer parts is set by the dark matter halo, while the stars and gas went through messy processes (gas cooling, star formation, feedback from supernovae), why should the two be so closely tied? In the dark matter picture this looks like fine-tuning. Supporters of Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND, Chapter 7) see the baryonic Tully–Fisher relation as direct evidence against cold dark matter.

## Summary

- Poisson's equation, $\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho$, links motions to all the mass present. For a spherical system the acceleration at $r$ depends only on the mass inside $r$.
- The rotation curve weighs the galaxy: $M(r) = r v_c^2(r)/G$. Point mass → $v_c \propto r^{-1/2}$; uniform sphere → $v_c \propto r$; isothermal sphere ($\rho \propto r^{-2}$) → flat.
- Rotation curves stay flat far beyond the stars and gas (Vera Rubin's work). The visible mass alone would give a Keplerian fall, so an extra, roughly isothermal component is needed: a dark matter halo. In NGC 2841 the mass-to-light ratio is about 10.
- Speeds are measured with the Doppler effect, by long-slit spectra (S-shaped lines) or integral field maps (the spider pattern, $v\cos\phi\sin i$).
- The Tully–Fisher relation, $L \propto v_\text{max}^4$, and its baryonic version (stellar plus gas mass against $v_\text{max}$) tie the visible matter tightly to the rotation speed, a puzzle for dark matter, and a key argument for MOND.
