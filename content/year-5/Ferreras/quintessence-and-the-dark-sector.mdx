This course is about dark matter, but Ferreras ends with the other dark component. The cosmological constant and the supernova evidence for accelerating expansion (his Sections 8.1–8.2) are covered on this site by Guth's 8.286 lectures; so is the puzzle of the vacuum energy (see [the vacuum energy puzzle](/courses/8-286/vacuum-energy-puzzle)). This page follows the last two sections: dark energy as a **particle-physics field**, and whether dark energy and dark matter could be two faces of one thing.

## Dark energy as a field

In the spirit of Chapter 6, one might try to explain dark energy as a particle property. But it is a different kind of problem. A dark matter particle only needs enough mass, weak enough interactions and the right behaviour for structure formation; in general relativity it is simply another, standard, matter component. Dark energy needs either a change in the equations of gravity or a component with the strangest of equations of state: **negative pressure**.

In quantum field theory the **vacuum energy** is the energy density of the ground state, the minimum of the potential. Phase transitions, like those of the Standard Model, change that minimum, so they could set a vacuum energy that affects the expansion. Ferreras stresses that this path is risky: it joins general relativity and quantum behaviour without a theory that unites them, so it is speculative.

Suppose dark energy is a **scalar field** $\phi$ with potential $V(\phi)$:

<Formula label="Scalar field Lagrangian" tex="\mathcal{L} = \frac12\,\partial^\mu\phi\,\partial_\mu\phi - V(\phi)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (8.16), p. 187" />

If the field is the same everywhere (homogeneous and isotropic), its energy density and pressure are

<Formula label="Density and pressure of a scalar field" tex="\rho_\phi = \frac12\dot\phi^2 + V(\phi), \qquad p_\phi = \frac12\dot\phi^2 - V(\phi)" cite="mit" at="Eqs. (8.17)–(8.18), p. 187" />

so the equation-of-state parameter $w = p/\rho$ ranges from $-1$, when potential energy dominates (a cosmological constant), to $+1$, when kinetic energy dominates. Supernovae extend to about redshift 1, so $w \approx -1$ must hold for a long time: in standard ΛCDM ($H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc, $\Omega_m = 0.3$) the light from redshift 1 left about 7.7 billion years ago, some 57% of the universe's age. So both the field's speed and its acceleration must be small compared with the potential and its slope:

<Formula label="Slow-roll conditions" tex="\dot\phi^2 \ll V(\phi), \qquad |\ddot\phi| \ll \left|\frac{dV}{d\phi}\right|" cite="mit" at="Eq. (8.20), p. 187" />

A field rolling slowly like this, as the inflaton does during inflation (on this site: [the false vacuum and inflation](/courses/8-286/false-vacuum)), gives a **dynamical** dark energy whose $w$ changes with time. This is **quintessence**.

<WorkedExample title="How close to −1?">
<Problem>
A quintessence field has kinetic energy equal to 5% of its potential energy. Find $w$. How much faster than a cosmological constant does its density dilute?
</Problem>
<Step title="The equation of state">
With $\tfrac12\dot\phi^2 = 0.05V$: $w = (0.05V - V)/(0.05V + V) = -0.95/1.05 \approx -0.905$.
</Step>
<Step title="Dilution">
A component with constant $w$ dilutes as $\rho \propto a^{-3(1+w)} = a^{-0.29}$. Between redshift 1 and today ($a$ doubles) its density falls by $2^{-0.29} \approx 0.82$, about 18%, whereas a cosmological constant stays fixed and matter falls by a factor of 8. Precise supernova and other measurements of $w$ can tell these apart.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A tiny number, and a coincidence

Numerically, with $\Omega_\Lambda \approx 0.7$, the present dark energy density is about $3 \times 10^{-47}$ GeV⁴ (in natural units, where energy density has units of energy to the fourth power), an energy scale of only about 2 meV. (The book quotes about $10^{-48}$ GeV⁴; the value here is computed for $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc.) Particle physics expects something wildly larger. Adding the zero-point energy of all field modes up to a cut-off $k_\text{max}$,

<Formula label="Vacuum energy with a cut-off" tex="\rho_v = \frac12\int_0^{k_\text{max}}\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\sqrt{k^2 + m^2} \sim k_\text{max}^4" cite="mit" at="Eq. (8.21), p. 188" />

so a phase transition at energy scale $\epsilon$ gives $\rho_v \sim \epsilon^4$. At the grand unification scale, $\epsilon \sim 10^{15}$ GeV, that is $10^{60}$ GeV⁴, more than a hundred orders of magnitude too large. Explaining the difference would need an unprecedented degree of fine-tuning. A dark energy that decays from huge early values to today's tiny one is conceivable, but then why does it start to dominate **now**, with $\Omega_\Lambda \approx 0.7$ and heading towards 1? A successful theory must decide whether this is pure coincidence.

<WorkedExample title="Why now?">
<Problem>
Today dark energy and matter make up 0.7 and 0.3 of the critical density. Matter dilutes as $a^{-3}$ while a cosmological constant stays fixed. Find the ratio of their densities at redshifts 1 and 3.
</Problem>
<Step title="Scaling">
$\rho_\Lambda/\rho_m = (0.7/0.3)(1+z)^{-3} = 2.33\,(1+z)^{-3}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The ratios">
At $z = 1$: $2.33/8 \approx 0.29$. At $z = 3$: $2.33/64 \approx 0.036$, so dark energy was under 4% of the matter. Far in the future it will dominate completely. The two are comparable only during a brief cosmic moment, and that moment is now.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Are dark matter and dark energy connected?

Today about **70%** of the universe's energy budget is dark energy and about **25%** dark matter (about 80% of all the matter). It would be economical if both were manifestations of one thing. Yet they behave very differently and dominated at different times:

- **Dark matter** has never been detected, but gravitationally it behaves exactly like ordinary matter. One would ultimately hope to catch it in a particle detector.
- **Dark energy** needs an exotic equation of state and looks more like vacuum fluctuations, a far more arcane scenario.

Both could be signs that general relativity needs changing, but in very different ways. To replace dark matter, gravity must be **stronger** at low accelerations (Chapter 7) [the book says "weaken" here, but MOND's effective gravity at low accelerations is stronger than Newton's], already hard to achieve in a fully covariant theory. To replace dark energy, gravity must change its very nature and allow **repulsion**, more like electromagnetism.

Some researchers have published Lagrangians that try to explain both at once. One example is the covariant **emergent gravity** proposed by Verlinde and by Hossenfelder, in which a vector field coupled to baryonic matter produces the dynamical effects of dark matter and also acts as a cosmological constant (Hossenfelder, 2017). [The book spells the first name "Verlinder"; the physicist is Erik Verlinde.] As Ferreras ends his book: the road lies ahead.

## Summary

- Dark energy could be a homogeneous scalar field: $\rho = \tfrac12\dot\phi^2 + V$, $p = \tfrac12\dot\phi^2 - V$, so $-1 \leq w \leq 1$. Supernovae need $w \approx -1$ over the past 7.7 billion years, hence a slowly rolling field, quintessence, with a time-dependent $w$.
- The measured density, about $3 \times 10^{-47}$ GeV⁴ (about 2 meV to the fourth power), is more than a hundred orders of magnitude below a grand-unification vacuum energy: a fine-tuning problem.
- Dark energy dominates only now (it was under 4% of the matter at redshift 3): the coincidence problem.
- About 70% dark energy and 25% dark matter make up the budget. Dark matter gravitates like matter; dark energy repels. Unified theories, such as emergent gravity, remain tentative.
