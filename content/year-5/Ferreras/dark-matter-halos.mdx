Chapter 2 showed that galaxies and clusters hold a component that is invisible, dominates at large radii, and cannot be made of compact clumps, so it must be diffuse. (Chapter 5 of the book shows that it cannot be made of baryons either; on this site that is covered by the 8.286 and 8.902 pages.) This changes what a galaxy *is*. Ferreras's Chapter 3 describes the resulting picture: every galaxy sits in a **dark matter halo**, and halos can be described by a handful of properties.

## Galaxies inside halos

Observationally, a galaxy is a system of stars, gas and dust, bound by its own gravity. Stars within a galaxy are packed far more closely than galaxies are spaced, so galaxies look like "island universes". Dark matter changes that picture. Each galaxy is surrounded by a halo that extends far beyond its stars and dominates its mass (except in the inner regions). The halos reach out towards neighbouring galaxies, linking them into a **cosmic web** of filaments, with the largest concentrations, galaxy clusters, at the nodes.

Dark matter is the simplest part of structure formation to follow: it is diffuse and collisionless and feels only gravity in the expanding universe. Gravitational instability makes overdense regions collapse until the random motions of the particles halt the collapse, the counterpart of pressure in a gas. A structure that has reached this stable state is a **halo**. In the spherical collapse model (on this site: [spherical collapse](/courses/8-902/spherical-collapse)), a halo's mean density is about 180 times the background density at the time it collapsed, so halos that formed earlier are denser.

Baryons start out mixed with the dark matter as diffuse gas, mostly hydrogen and helium. The difference is that baryons can **lose energy**: collisions excite atoms, which then emit photons that carry energy away. The cooled gas sinks to the bottom of the potential well, forms clouds of molecular hydrogen and eventually stars. Stars, once formed, behave like dark matter, as collisionless particles, but they formed only after the gas had sunk inwards. That is why the luminous part of a galaxy is much smaller than its halo: the Milky Way's stellar disc reaches about 10–20 kpc, its dark halo about 100–200 kpc.

<WorkedExample title="How big is a halo?">
<Problem>
Take a halo of $3 \times 10^{11}\,M_\odot$ that has just formed today, with mean density 200 times the present mean matter density. Use $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc and $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.3$. Find its radius.
</Problem>
<Step title="The background density">
The critical density is $\rho_c = 3H_0^2/(8\pi G) \approx 1.36 \times 10^{11}\,M_\odot$ per Mpc³, so the mean matter density is $\bar\rho = 0.3\rho_c \approx 4.08 \times 10^{10}\,M_\odot$ per Mpc³.
</Step>
<Step title="The radius">
$M = \tfrac43\pi R^3 \times 200\bar\rho$ gives $R = \left(\dfrac{3M}{800\pi\bar\rho}\right)^{1/3} = \left(\dfrac{3 \times 3 \times 10^{11}}{800\pi \times 4.08 \times 10^{10}}\right)^{1/3}$ Mpc $\approx 0.21$ Mpc, or about 210 kpc.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
A galaxy in such a halo has a stellar disc perhaps 10 kpc across, or 5 kpc in radius: the halo's radius is some forty times the disc's. A halo that collapsed earlier, when the background was denser, would be smaller for the same mass.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Mass

Dark matter moves on orbits in the potential of everything else; two-body encounters matter only in very dense places such as the cores of globular clusters, and only gas feels non-gravitational forces. Relaxation takes longer than the age of a galaxy, so each halo remembers how it was assembled, and no two halos are identical: their shapes and the filaments that join them to the cosmic web depend on their history and environment. Still, a few properties allow halos to be compared.

The most important is **mass**. Cold dark matter grows **hierarchically**, bottom-up: small halos form first and merge into larger ones, so halo mass is statistically tied to cosmic time. Halos in overdense regions assemble earlier. The number of halos of each mass, the **halo mass function**, follows from spherical collapse applied to a Gaussian random field of initial fluctuations (the Press–Schechter approach, on this site in [Press–Schechter](/courses/8-902/press-schechter)); ellipsoidal collapse gives mass functions that closely match simulations. Cluster-sized halos ($\gtrsim 10^{14}\,M_\odot$) become common only at redshifts below about 2.

The link between mass and formation history has a large **scatter**, which depends on halo properties other than mass: this is **assembly bias**. Simulations also suggest that about 40% of a halo's mass is gained by **smooth accretion** of dark matter that was never part of a smaller halo, rather than by mergers (Genel and collaborators, 2010).

## The density profile

The simplest argument, borrowed from thermodynamics, gives the **isothermal sphere**: equal velocity dispersion ("temperature") everywhere, and a density $\rho \propto 1/r^2$. It has two problems: the density diverges at the centre (though the enclosed mass does not), and the total mass diverges as $M \propto r$. A **core** fixes the first, an outer cut-off (the truncated isothermal sphere) the second. A useful measure of a profile is its **logarithmic slope**

<Formula label="Logarithmic slope of the density" tex="\gamma(r) = \frac{d\ln\rho}{d\ln r}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.1), p. 55" />

which is $-2$ everywhere for the isothermal sphere.

But halos are not thermalised gases: their long relaxation times keep memory of their initial conditions. Even so, simulations of gravitational collapse found that halos share, on average, a **universal shape**: the slope reaches the isothermal value $-2$ at a **scale radius** $r_S$, is shallower inside and steeper outside. This motivates the double power law

<Formula label="Double power-law density profile" tex="\rho(x; \alpha, \beta) = \frac{\rho_0}{x^\alpha (1+x)^\beta}, \qquad x = \frac{r}{r_S}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.2), p. 55" />

with slope $-\alpha$ near the centre and $-(\alpha + \beta)$ far out. The pair $(\alpha, \beta) = (2, 0)$ is the isothermal sphere. $(1, 2)$ is the **Navarro–Frenk–White (NFW) profile** (1996), which fits simulated halos well; others have argued for steeper centres ($\alpha \approx 1.5$) or cores ($\alpha \approx 0$). The **Hernquist profile** (1990), $(1, 3)$, fits the stars of elliptical galaxies. Another common choice is the **Einasto profile**:

<Formula label="Einasto profile" tex="\rho(x; \alpha) = \rho_S\,\exp\left[-\frac{2}{\alpha}\left(x^\alpha - 1\right)\right]" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.3), p. 56" />

whose slope $\gamma = -2x^\alpha$ passes through $-2$ at $r = r_S$ for any $\alpha$; with $\alpha \approx 0.2$ its slope is comparable to that of the NFW profile.

Other characteristic radii are used too: the radius $r_{v,\text{max}}$ where the rotation curve peaks, and the **effective radius** $r_e$ containing half the mass (or its projected version $R_e$, used to compare with observations).

<WorkedExample title="Mass and rotation curve of an NFW halo (the book's Exercise 3.3)">
<Problem>
For the NFW profile, find the mass inside radius $r$, show that it keeps growing at large radius, and find where the rotation curve peaks.
</Problem>
<Step title="Enclosed mass">
$M(r) = 4\pi\int_0^r \rho\,r'^2\,dr' = 4\pi\rho_0 r_S^3\int_0^x \frac{x'}{(1+x')^2}\,dx' = 4\pi\rho_0 r_S^3\left[\ln(1+x) - \frac{x}{1+x}\right]$.
</Step>
<Step title="No finite total">
At large $x$ the bracket grows like $\ln x$: the mass diverges, slowly. (The Hernquist profile, with its steeper $r^{-4}$ fall-off, has a finite total mass.) For NFW, a halo must be cut off at some radius to have a mass at all.
</Step>
<Step title="Peak of the rotation curve">
$v_c^2 = GM(r)/r$ is largest where $\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{\ln(1+x) - x/(1+x)}{x}\right] = 0$, which gives $\ln(1+x) = x^2/(1+x)^2 + x/(1+x)$, solved numerically at $x \approx 2.163$. The rotation curve of an NFW halo peaks at about $2.16\,r_S$. (For the Hernquist profile, $M \propto x^2/(1+x)^2$ and $v_c^2 \propto x/(1+x)^2$, which peaks exactly at $x = 1$, the scale radius.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Size and concentration

Like a galaxy's, a halo's edge is fuzzy. Simulations place it where the structure stops being virialised, and in practice spherical collapse is used: the halo radius $r_h$ is where the mean enclosed density is about **200 times the background density at virialisation**. (Some authors use 200 times the present mean density, or 200 times the critical density; these radii differ, especially for low-mass halos that collapsed early.) The **concentration** is

<Formula label="Concentration" tex="c = \frac{r_h}{r_S}" cite="mit" at="Section 3.2, p. 57" />

High values mean more mass packed into a small volume. Because the NFW mass diverges, the same inner profile can have any concentration, depending on where it is truncated: if mass is the main parameter of a halo, concentration is "the second parameter".

Simulations and observations, from galaxies to clusters, show that **more massive halos are less concentrated**. In hierarchical growth small halos collapse early, when the universe was denser, and massive ones later. In the Uchuu simulation the median concentration at fixed mass is different at redshift 1 and today, and the relation steepens with time.

A more physical edge is the **splashback radius**. As a halo settles, some particles become bound, some escape, and some fly out to large distances before falling back. These "backsplash" particles make a sharp steepening of the density profile at the outermost point of their orbits, the splashback radius, which is less sensitive to arbitrary definitions than the virial radius. Galaxies falling into clusters behave the same way: some become satellites, and some, **backsplash galaxies**, pass through the cluster and are found far outside it, carrying signs of their passage.

## Shape and spin

Real halos are not spherical. The simplest next step describes the surfaces of equal density as ellipsoids with axes $a_1 \geq a_2 \geq a_3$, assumed the same at all densities, with axis ratios

<Formula label="Halo axis ratios" tex="s = \frac{a_3}{a_1}, \qquad q = \frac{a_2}{a_1}, \qquad p = \frac{a_3}{a_2}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.4), p. 61" />

and the **triaxiality**

<Formula label="Triaxiality" tex="T = \frac{1 - q^2}{1 - s^2}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.5), p. 61" />

which runs from 0 (an oblate, flattened sphere) to 1 (a prolate, cigar-like shape). Low-mass halos tend to be more spherical, having undergone fewer mergers; massive ones, built by many mergers from all directions, are more irregular, and their orientation should follow the filaments feeding them. Baryons change halo shapes too: in simulations, hydrodynamics alone does little, but feedback and radiation make halos rounder. Observational constraints (from galaxies in clusters or stellar streams) are not yet conclusive.

Asymmetric collapse also gives halos some **angular momentum**, measured by the dimensionless **spin parameter**

<Formula label="Spin parameter" tex="\lambda = \frac{L\,|E|^{1/2}}{G\,M^{5/2}}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.6), p. 62" />

with $L$, $E$ and $M$ the halo's total angular momentum, energy and mass. A system supported by rotation has $\lambda \sim 1$. Simulated halos follow a log-normal distribution peaking at $\lambda \approx 0.04$: halos are **pressure-supported**, their dark matter moving more like the stars of an elliptical galaxy than the stars of a disc.

## Summary

- Galaxies sit inside dark matter halos that are ten or more times larger: baryons cool and sink to the centre, dark matter cannot. Halos link up into a cosmic web.
- Mass is the main property of a halo; halos grow hierarchically, with scatter (assembly bias) and about 40% of their mass from smooth accretion.
- Simulated halos follow a near-universal profile: the NFW profile $\rho_0/[x(1+x)^2]$ or the Einasto profile, with slope −2 at the scale radius. The NFW rotation curve peaks at $2.16\,r_S$, and its mass diverges logarithmically.
- Concentration $c = r_h/r_S$ is the second parameter; low-mass halos are more concentrated. The splashback radius is a physical alternative to the virial radius.
- Halos are triaxial ellipsoids (axis ratios $s$, $q$; triaxiality $T$) and spin slowly ($\lambda \approx 0.04$): they are hot, pressure-supported systems.
