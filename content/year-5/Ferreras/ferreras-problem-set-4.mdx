Where the book leaves a number or a convention open, we choose one and say so ("our choice", "our reading"); parts beyond the book's are marked "(our addition)".

The freeze-out of a heavy, cold particle (a WIMP) is left to the next set. Exercise 5.5 here is about a particle that was still relativistic when it decoupled: a hot relic.

## How to answer

Work each exercise on paper first (Exercise 5.6 also needs a root finder: a calculator, a spreadsheet or a few lines of Python will do). Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it; type decimals, for example `0.25` or `1.2e5`, in the unit the box names. A formula box takes an expression in the letters it names, for example `a*b/c` or `sqrt(a)/b^2`; type `^` for powers.

The book gives no points: the points are ours, 100 in all (15, 10, 15, 15, 20, 15 and 10 for the seven exercises), and so is every split between parts. Parts that ask you to show or discuss something have no box: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is the book's. $T$ is a temperature, $k_B$ Boltzmann's constant, $\hbar$ the reduced Planck constant and $c$ the speed of light; the book often sets $\hbar = c = k_B = 1$, and we write the constants out wherever a number in SI units is wanted. $g$ is the number of internal (spin) states of a species, counted without the fermion factors $\tfrac78$ and $\tfrac34$, which the book writes separately. (The 8.286 lessons fold those factors into their $g$; the results are the same.) $z$ is redshift, $a$ the scale factor (1 today), $H$ the Hubble parameter and $\zeta(3) \approx 1.202$ the Riemann zeta function at 3.

Constants (CODATA 2018): $k_B = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K, $\hbar = 1.054572 \times 10^{-34}$ J s, $c = 2.997925 \times 10^8$ m/s, $G = 6.67430 \times 10^{-11}$ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻², the proton mass $m_p = 1.672622 \times 10^{-27}$ kg, the Thomson cross-section $\sigma_T = 6.652459 \times 10^{-29}$ m² (the book's "0.67 barn", p. 112), 1 eV $= 1.602177 \times 10^{-19}$ J, and 1 Mpc $= 3.0857 \times 10^{22}$ m. The critical density is $\rho_c = 3H_0^2/8\pi G = 1.8783 \times 10^{-26}\,h^2$ kg/m³, where $H_0 = 100h$ km/s/Mpc; as an energy density, $\rho_c c^2 = 10{,}537\,h^2$ eV/cm³.

## Exercise 5.1

<Exercise id="e1" title="Photons and neutrinos in the cosmic background" points={15} mit="Exercise 5.1">

Treat the cosmic microwave background as black-body radiation at $T = 2.73$ K, with the spectral radiance (the power per unit area, per unit solid angle and per unit frequency) given by Planck's law:

<Formula label="Planck's law for the spectral radiance" tex="B(\nu; T) = \frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2}\,\frac{1}{\exp(h\nu/k_BT) - 1}" cite="mit" at="eq. (5.4), p. 106" />

where $h = 2\pi\hbar$ and $\nu$ is the frequency. Find the number density and the energy density of the photons, and compare them with the cosmic neutrino background: $N_\nu = 3$ families of neutrinos at $T_{\nu,0} = 1.95$ K (book, Exercise 5.1, p. 106).

<Part label="A" points={5}>

What is the number density $n_\gamma$ of the background photons today, in photons per cm³ (tolerance 1%)?

<Hint>

Radiation that is the same in every direction carries an energy density $\frac{4\pi}{c}B\,d\nu$ in the frequency range $d\nu$. Divide by the energy $h\nu$ of one photon to count photons.

</Hint>

<Hint>

With $x = h\nu/k_BT$ the integral becomes $\int_0^\infty x^2\,dx/(e^x - 1) = 2\zeta(3)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e1-ng" label="$n_\gamma$ in cm⁻³:" value="413" tolerance="1%" calc="e1-ng" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Turn the radiance into an energy density per frequency, divide by the photon energy and integrate over all frequencies.

<Step title="From radiance to number density">

Each frequency band holds the energy density $(4\pi/c)B\,d\nu$, so the number of photons per volume is

<Formula label="Photon number density from Planck's law" tex="n_\gamma = \frac{4\pi}{c}\int_0^\infty \frac{B(\nu;T)}{h\nu}\,d\nu = 8\pi\left(\frac{k_BT}{hc}\right)^3\int_0^\infty\frac{x^2\,dx}{e^x - 1} = \frac{2\zeta(3)}{\pi^2}\left(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar c}\right)^3" cite="mit" at="Exercise 5.1, p. 106 (our own solution)" />

using $8\pi/(2\pi)^3 \times 2\zeta(3) = 2\zeta(3)/\pi^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

$k_BT/\hbar c = 1192.2$ m⁻¹ at 2.73 K, so $(k_BT/\hbar c)^3 = 1.6945 \times 10^9$ m⁻³ and $n_\gamma = 0.24359 \times 1.6945 \times 10^9$ m⁻³ $= 4.128 \times 10^8$ m⁻³ $= 413$ photons per cm³.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

This is the formula of the [black-body radiation](/courses/8-286/black-body-radiation) lesson with $g = 2$ photon polarisations: $n = 2\,\zeta(3)/\pi^2\,(k_BT/\hbar c)^3$. Every cubic centimetre of the universe holds about four hundred CMB photons.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="B" points={4}>

What is the photon energy density $\rho_\gamma$ today, in eV per cm³ (tolerance 2%)?

<Hint>

Now integrate the energy itself: $\int_0^\infty x^3\,dx/(e^x - 1) = \pi^4/15$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e1-rg" label="$\rho_\gamma$ in eV/cm³:" value="0.262" tolerance="2%" calc="e1-rg" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same integral with one more power of $x$.

<Step title="The energy density">

<Formula label="Photon energy density" tex="\rho_\gamma = \frac{4\pi}{c}\int_0^\infty B(\nu;T)\,d\nu = \frac{\pi^2}{15}\,k_BT\left(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar c}\right)^3" cite="mit" at="Exercise 5.1, p. 106 (our own solution)" />

$\rho_\gamma = 0.65797 \times (3.7692 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J}) \times 1.6945 \times 10^9$ m⁻³ $= 4.202 \times 10^{-14}$ J/m³ $= 0.262$ eV/cm³.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The mean energy per photon is $\rho_\gamma/n_\gamma = 6.35 \times 10^{-4}$ eV $= 2.70\,k_BT$ (since $k_BT = 2.35 \times 10^{-4}$ eV), the familiar black-body value $\pi^4/(30\zeta(3)) = 2.70$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="C" points={6}>

Each neutrino family has a neutrino and an antineutrino, each with one spin state ($g = 1$, as the book says on p. 114); this is our reading of "3 families". Neutrinos are fermions. What is the total number density $n_\nu$ of the three families today, in cm⁻³ (tolerance 2%)? And what is the ratio $\rho_\nu/\rho_\gamma$ of their energy density to that of the photons, taking the neutrinos to be massless (tolerance 1%)?

<Hint>

For fermions the occupation is $1/(e^x + 1)$. The number integral is then $\tfrac34$ of the boson one, and the energy integral $\tfrac78$ of it (Exercise 5.4 asks you to show this).

</Hint>

<Hint>

Count the states: three families, two particles each, one spin state each. Compare with the photon's two polarisations.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e1-nn" label="$n_\nu$ in cm⁻³:" value="338" tolerance="2%" calc="e1-nn" />

<Answer id="e1-ratio" label="$\rho_\nu/\rho_\gamma$:" value="0.683" tolerance="1%" calc="e1-ratio" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The neutrinos are a thermal gas of massless fermions with six states in all, at a lower temperature than the photons.

<Step title="Number density">

<Formula label="Neutrino number density, three families" tex="n_\nu = 3 \times 2 \times \frac34\,\frac{\zeta(3)}{\pi^2}\left(\frac{k_BT_{\nu,0}}{\hbar c}\right)^3 = \frac94\left(\frac{T_{\nu,0}}{T}\right)^3 n_\gamma" cite="mit" at="Exercise 5.1, p. 106 (our own solution)" />

$(1.95/2.73)^3 = 0.3644$, so $n_\nu = 2.25 \times 0.3644 \times 412.8$ cm⁻³ $= 338$ cm⁻³, about 113 per family.

</Step>

<Step title="Energy density">

<Formula label="Neutrino to photon energy density" tex="\frac{\rho_\nu}{\rho_\gamma} = \frac{3 \times 2 \times \frac78}{2}\left(\frac{T_{\nu,0}}{T}\right)^4" cite="mit" at="Exercise 5.1, p. 106 (our own solution)" />

$(1.95/2.73)^4 = 0.2603$, so $\rho_\nu/\rho_\gamma = 2.625 \times 0.2603 = 0.683$.

</Step>

<Step title="Contrast and check">

The neutrino background has almost as many particles as the photon background (338 against 413 per cm³) but only two thirds of its energy, because each neutrino state counts $\tfrac34$ (number) or $\tfrac78$ (energy) of a boson state and the neutrinos are colder. The temperature 1.95 K is $(4/11)^{1/3} \times 2.73$ K, the ratio derived in the [neutrino lesson](/courses/8-286/neutrinos-and-particle-counting); with that exact ratio $\rho_\nu/\rho_\gamma = \tfrac{21}{8}(4/11)^{4/3} = 0.681$, within the box's tolerance. (If neutrinos have masses of a fraction of an eV, as oscillations show, they are no longer relativistic today and their energy density is larger; the number density is unchanged.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Exercise 5.2

<Exercise id="e2" title="How ordinary matter cools" points={10} mit="Exercise 5.2">

The book finds how the photon temperature falls in the expanding universe from an adiabatic expansion: radiation has $p \propto T^4$, the adiabatic law $pV^\gamma = $ constant holds with $\gamma = 4/3$, and the volume grows as $V \propto a^3$, which gives the following.

<Formula label="Temperature of the photons" tex="T \propto a^{-1} \propto 1 + z" cite="mit" at="eq. (5.10), p. 109" />

Apply the same argument to ordinary matter, an ideal monatomic gas with $p \propto \rho T$, that no longer exchanges heat with the photons (book, Exercise 5.2, p. 110).

<Part label="A" points={5}>

A gas has temperature $T_d$ at redshift $z_d$, when it stops exchanging heat with the photons. What is its temperature $T$ at a later redshift $z$? Type it with `T` for $T_d$, `w` for $z_d$ and `z`.

<Hint>

For an ideal gas the density is $\rho \propto 1/V$, so $p \propto T/V$. Put that into $pV^\gamma = $ constant.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A monatomic gas has $\gamma = 5/3$, and $V \propto a^3 \propto (1 + z)^{-3}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e2-temp" label="$T =$" expr="T*((1+z)/(1+w))^2"
  vars={{ T: [100, 1000], z: [0, 20], w: [100, 200] }}
  names={{ T: "T_d", w: "z_d" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "T*(1+z)/(1+w)", hint: "That is how radiation cools (adiabatic index 4/3). A monatomic gas has γ = 5/3." },
    { expr: "T*((1+z)/(1+w))^5", hint: "That keeps T V^γ fixed. With p ∝ T/V the adiabatic law p V^γ = constant keeps T V^(γ − 1) fixed instead." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The adiabatic law with the gas's own exponent, written in terms of the temperature.

<Step title="The adiabatic invariant">

With $p \propto \rho T \propto T/V$, $pV^\gamma \propto TV^{\gamma - 1} = $ constant. For $\gamma = 5/3$ this is $TV^{2/3} = $ constant, and with $V \propto a^3$:

<Formula label="Temperature of a decoupled monatomic gas" tex="T \propto a^{-3(\gamma - 1)} = a^{-2} \propto (1 + z)^2, \qquad T = T_d\left(\frac{1 + z}{1 + z_d}\right)^2" cite="mit" at="Exercise 5.2, p. 110 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The same route for radiation gives $T \propto a^{-3(4/3 - 1)} = a^{-1}$, the book's result. A second way to see the gas law: the gas particles' momenta redshift as $1/a$, and for non-relativistic particles the kinetic energy, and so the temperature, goes as momentum squared, $1/a^2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="B" points={5}>

(Our addition.) Suppose the gas follows the photons, $T = 2.73\ \text{K} \times (1 + z)$, until $z_d = 150$ (our choice), and cools adiabatically afterwards. What is the gas temperature at $z = 20$, in K (tolerance 2%)? Compare it with the photon temperature then.

<Hint>

At $z_d$ the gas has the photon temperature of that moment. Then use your result of part A.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e2-temp20" label="$T$ at $z = 20$ in K:" value="7.97" tolerance="2%" calc="e2-temp20" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Start from the photon temperature at $z_d$ and cool by the square of the redshift factor.

<Step title="The temperature">

$T_d = 2.73 \times 151 = 412$ K. Then $T(z = 20) = 412\ \text{K} \times (21/151)^2 = 412 \times 0.01934$ K $= 7.97$ K.

</Step>

<Step title="Check and comparison">

The photons at $z = 20$ are at $2.73 \times 21 = 57.3$ K, about seven times warmer. Equivalently $T_\text{gas}/T_\gamma = (1 + z)/(1 + z_d) = 21/151 = 0.139$, and $57.3 \times 0.139 = 7.97$ K. In the real universe a few free electrons left over after recombination keep the gas close to the photon temperature until a redshift of roughly a hundred or more; after that, the gas does cool faster than the radiation, as here, until the first stars heat it again.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Exercise 5.3

<Exercise id="e3" title="A first estimate of decoupling" points={15} mit="Exercise 5.3">

Photons stay coupled to matter while the rate at which a photon scatters off free electrons is larger than the expansion rate $H$; they decouple when that rate falls below $H$ (book, eq. 5.13, p. 113). Assume that decoupling happens in the matter-dominated era, and estimate its redshift. Treat the ordinary matter as fully ionised hydrogen and helium, with mass fractions $X = 0.75$ and $Y = 0.25$, a baryon density today of $\Omega_{b,0}h_0^2 = 0.022$ and $h_0 = 0.67$. Under what conditions would decoupling instead happen in the radiation-dominated era (book, Exercise 5.3, p. 114)? Take the scattering to be Thomson scattering, with cross-section $\sigma_T$, the matter density $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.3$ and, for the matter era, $H = H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}}\,(1 + z)^{3/2}$ (our choices: the exercise does not give them).

<Part label="A" points={4}>

What is the number density $n_{e,0}$ that the free electrons would have today, in m⁻³, if the gas were still fully ionised (tolerance 2%)? Take the mass of a helium nucleus as $4m_p$ (our choice).

<Hint>

Each hydrogen nucleus brings one electron per mass $m_p$; each helium nucleus brings two electrons per mass $4m_p$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e3-ne" label="$n_{e,0}$ in m⁻³:" value="0.216" tolerance="2%" calc="e3-ne" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Count electrons per unit mass of gas, then multiply by the baryon mass density.

<Step title="Electrons per unit mass">

Hydrogen gives $X/m_p$ electrons per unit mass and helium $2 \times Y/(4m_p) = Y/(2m_p)$, so $n_e = (X + Y/2)\,\rho_b/m_p = 0.875\,\rho_b/m_p$.

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

$\rho_{b,0} = 0.022 \times 1.8783 \times 10^{-26}$ kg/m³ $= 4.132 \times 10^{-28}$ kg/m³, so $n_{e,0} = 0.875 \times 4.132 \times 10^{-28}/1.6726 \times 10^{-27}$ m⁻³ $= 0.216$ m⁻³.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The baryon number density is $\rho_{b,0}/m_p = 0.247$ m⁻³, and $0.875 \times 0.247 = 0.216$: slightly less than one electron per baryon, because a helium nucleus has two electrons for four baryons.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="B" points={6}>

The scattering rate is $\Gamma = n_e\sigma_Tc$, with $n_e = n_{e,0}(1 + z)^3$ while the gas stays ionised. Write $\Gamma_0 = n_{e,0}\sigma_Tc$. Find $1 + z_\text{dec}$, where $\Gamma = H$ in the matter era, as a formula: type `q` for $\Gamma_0$, `H` for $H_0$ and `W` for $\Omega_{m,0}$. Then give the number $z_\text{dec}$ (tolerance 2%).

<Hint>

$\Gamma$ grows as $(1 + z)^3$ into the past, $H$ only as $(1 + z)^{3/2}$. So $\Gamma/H$ is a power of $1 + z$ times a constant.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$H_0 = 67$ km/s/Mpc is $2.171 \times 10^{-18}$ s⁻¹.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e3-zf" label="$1 + z_\text{dec} =$" expr="(H*sqrt(W)/q)^(2/3)"
  vars={{ H: [2e-18, 2.4e-18], W: [0.2, 0.4], q: [3e-21, 6e-21] }}
  names={{ q: "\\Gamma_0", H: "H_0", W: "\\Omega_{m,0}" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(H*sqrt(W)/q)^(3/2)", hint: "The ratio H₀√Ω/Γ₀ equals (1 + z) to the power 3/2. To find 1 + z, raise the ratio to the power 2/3, not 3/2." },
    { expr: "(H/q)^(2/3)", hint: "That is the result for a universe with Ω_m,0 = 1. With less matter the expansion rate is lower by √Ω_m,0." },
    { expr: "(H*sqrt(W)/q)^(1/3)", hint: "Check the powers: Γ grows as (1 + z)³ and H as (1 + z)^(3/2), so Γ/H grows as (1 + z)^(3/2)." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="e3-z" label="$z_\text{dec}$:" value="41.4" tolerance="2%" calc="e3-z" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Set the scattering rate equal to the expansion rate and solve for the redshift.

<Step title="The condition">

<Formula label="Decoupling of a fully ionised gas in the matter era" tex="\Gamma_0(1 + z)^3 = H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}}\,(1 + z)^{3/2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1 + z_\text{dec} = \left(\frac{H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}}}{\Gamma_0}\right)^{2/3}" cite="mit" at="Exercise 5.3, p. 114 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

$\Gamma_0 = 0.2162 \times 6.6525 \times 10^{-29} \times 2.9979 \times 10^8$ s⁻¹ $= 4.311 \times 10^{-21}$ s⁻¹, and $H_0\sqrt{0.3} = 1.1893 \times 10^{-18}$ s⁻¹. Their ratio is 275.9, and $275.9^{2/3} = 42.4$, so $z_\text{dec} = 41.4$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check, and another reading">

At $z = 41.4$: $\Gamma = 4.311 \times 10^{-21} \times 42.38^3$ s⁻¹ $= 3.28 \times 10^{-16}$ s⁻¹ and $H = 1.1893 \times 10^{-18} \times 42.38^{1.5}$ s⁻¹ $= 3.28 \times 10^{-16}$ s⁻¹, equal. Matter does dominate then (radiation is only about 1.3% of it: $42.4/3214$, with the equality redshift of part C). If you took a matter-only universe, $\Omega_{m,0} = 1$, you get $1 + z_\text{dec} = 63.3$, $z_\text{dec} = 62$: the same order.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="C" points={5}>

Decoupling happens in the radiation era if $\Gamma$ has already fallen below $H$ by the time of matter-radiation equality. Take the radiation density $\Omega_{r,0}h_0^2 = 4.19 \times 10^{-5}$ (photons plus three neutrino families, from Exercise 5.1), so that $1 + z_\text{eq} = \Omega_{m,0}/\Omega_{r,0}$, and at equality $H = H_0\sqrt{2\Omega_{m,0}}\,(1 + z_\text{eq})^{3/2}$. Keeping $h_0$, $\Omega_{m,0}$ and the composition fixed, below what value of $\Omega_{b,0}h_0^2$ would the gas decouple before equality (tolerance 3%)? Then compare the whole estimate with the decoupling of the real universe.

<Hint>

$\Gamma$ is proportional to the baryon density. Compute $\Gamma$ and $H$ at equality for the baryon density of part A, and scale.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e3-obmax" label="largest $\Omega_{b,0}h_0^2$:" value="4.71e-5" tolerance="3%" calc="e3-obmax" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Since $\Gamma \propto \Omega_{b,0}h_0^2$, the condition $\Gamma(z_\text{eq}) < H(z_\text{eq})$ is an upper limit on the baryon density.

<Step title="Equality">

$\Omega_{m,0}h_0^2 = 0.3 \times 0.4489 = 0.1347$, so $1 + z_\text{eq} = 0.1347/4.19 \times 10^{-5} = 3214$.

</Step>

<Step title="Rates at equality">

For $\Omega_{b,0}h_0^2 = 0.022$: $\Gamma(z_\text{eq}) = 4.311 \times 10^{-21} \times 3214^3$ s⁻¹ $= 1.431 \times 10^{-10}$ s⁻¹, and $H(z_\text{eq}) = 2.171 \times 10^{-18}\sqrt{0.6} \times 3214^{3/2}$ s⁻¹ $= 3.064 \times 10^{-13}$ s⁻¹. $\Gamma$ is 467 times too large, so

<Formula label="Baryon density for decoupling before equality" tex="\Omega_{b,0}h_0^2 < 0.022\,\frac{H(z_\text{eq})}{\Gamma(z_\text{eq})} = \frac{0.022}{467} = 4.71 \times 10^{-5}" cite="mit" at="Exercise 5.3, p. 114 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The conditions, and the real universe">

In words: with about 470 times fewer free electrons (fewer baryons, or the same baryons largely neutral), photons would stream freely already in the radiation era. Equivalently, decoupling moves earlier when there is less to scatter off or when the expansion is faster. In the real universe the baryon density is the measured one, and our estimate gives $z \approx 41$, but that is not when the CMB was released. The assumption that fails is full ionisation: once the temperature drops to a few thousand kelvin the electrons combine with nuclei, $n_e$ falls far faster than $(1 + z)^3$, and the photons decouple at $T \approx 3000$ K (the value in the [temperature and decoupling](/courses/8-286/temperature-and-decoupling) lesson), that is at $1 + z \approx 3000/2.73$, $z \approx 1100$. The estimate of part B is when the photons would decouple from a gas that never recombined. It also shows why the reionisation of the gas by the first stars, at redshifts below about 10, did not couple the photons to it again: by then even a fully ionised gas scatters a photon less than once per expansion time.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Exercise 5.4

<Exercise id="e4" title="Number and energy densities of relativistic particles" points={15} mit="Exercise 5.4">

In thermal equilibrium a species with $g$ internal states and chemical potential $\mu$ has the number density (with $\hbar = c = k_B = 1$; the upper sign is for fermions, the lower for bosons)

<Formula label="Number density in equilibrium" tex="n(T) = \frac{g}{2\pi^2}\int_m^\infty \frac{(E^2 - m^2)^{1/2}}{\exp[(E - \mu)/T] \pm 1}\,E\,dE" cite="mit" at="eq. (5.15), p. 115" />

Take $\mu = 0$. Show that in the ultra-relativistic limit $m \ll T$ the number density and the energy density are as follows, with the factor $\tfrac34$ or $\tfrac78$ for fermions and 1 for bosons:

<Formula label="Ultra-relativistic number and energy densities" tex="n(T) = \left\{\tfrac34,\ 1\right\}\frac{g}{\pi^2}\,\zeta(3)\,T^3, \qquad \rho(T) = \left\{\tfrac78,\ 1\right\}\frac{\pi^2}{30}\,g\,T^4" cite="mit" at="eqs. (5.16) and (5.17), p. 115" />

Summing over species gives the effective number of degrees of freedom $g_*(T)$, with $\rho_\text{total} \propto g_* T^4$ (book, Exercise 5.4, p. 115). The book writes "assuming zero gravitational potential"; from the text just before it, which sets the chemical potential to zero, we read this as $\mu = 0$ (our reading).

<Part label="A" points={6}>

Show the two results. Along the way, give the value of the fermion integral $I = \int_0^\infty x^2\,dx/(e^x + 1)$ to three decimals (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

For $m \ll T$ set $m = 0$, so $(E^2 - m^2)^{1/2}E \to E^2$, and substitute $x = E/T$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\frac{1}{e^x + 1} = \frac{1}{e^x - 1} - \frac{2}{e^{2x} - 1}$. In the second term put $u = 2x$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e4-integral" label="$I =$" value="1.803" tolerance="1%" calc="e4-integral" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the massless limit both densities are $T^3$ or $T^4$ times a pure number; the fermion integrals follow from the boson ones by a simple identity.

<Step title="Bosons">

With $m = 0$ and $x = E/T$: $n = \frac{g}{2\pi^2}T^3\int_0^\infty \frac{x^2\,dx}{e^x - 1} = \frac{g}{2\pi^2}T^3 \times 2\zeta(3) = \frac{g}{\pi^2}\zeta(3)T^3$. The energy density puts one more $E$ in the integrand: $\rho = \frac{g}{2\pi^2}T^4\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3\,dx}{e^x - 1} = \frac{g}{2\pi^2}T^4\frac{\pi^4}{15} = \frac{\pi^2}{30}gT^4$.

</Step>

<Step title="Fermions">

Using the identity of the hint, $\int_0^\infty \frac{x^n\,dx}{e^x + 1} = \left(1 - 2^{-n}\right)\int_0^\infty\frac{x^n\,dx}{e^x - 1}$, because the second term, with $u = 2x$, is $2 \times 2^{-(n+1)}$ times the boson integral. For $n = 2$ the factor is $\tfrac34$ and for $n = 3$ it is $\tfrac78$: these are the factors in the formulas above. So $I = \tfrac34 \times 2\zeta(3) = \tfrac32\zeta(3) = 1.803$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

A numerical integration of $x^2/(e^x + 1)$ from 0 to 50 gives 1.8031. Summing $\rho$ over the relativistic species, each at its own temperature $T_i$ and weighted by $\tfrac78$ for fermions, gives the book's $g_*$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Exercise id="e5" title="The relic density of a hot relic" points={20} mit="Exercise 5.5">

<Exercise id="e7" title="Dark matter perturbations in the radiation era" points={10} mit="Exercise 5.7">

The linear growth equation for the density contrast $\delta$ (book, eq. 5.42, p. 128) is, for collisionless dark matter with no pressure term,

<Formula label="Linear growth equation without pressure" tex="\ddot\delta + 2H\dot\delta - 4\pi G\langle\rho\rangle\delta = 0" cite="mit" at="eq. (5.42), p. 128" />

<Formula label="Growth equation in terms of y" tex="\frac{d^2\delta}{dy^2} + \frac{2 + 3y}{2y(1 + y)}\frac{d\delta}{dy} - \frac{3\delta}{2y(1 + y)} = 0" cite="mit" at="eq. (5.43), p. 130" />

and that it has the growing solution $\delta_+ \propto 1 + \tfrac32 y$ (book, Exercise 5.7, p. 130; eq. 5.44).

<Part label="A" points={4}>

Derive the equation in $y$ and check the growing mode. Mark yourself against the solution.

<Hint>

Use $\dot\delta = \dot y\,\delta' = yH\,\delta'$ (a prime is $d/dy$), and write $H^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho_m\left(1 + \frac1y\right)$ with $\rho_m \propto y^{-3}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You will need $dH/dy$. Since $H^2 \propto y^{-4}(1 + y)$, $\frac{y}{H}\frac{dH}{dy} = -\frac{4 + 3y}{2(1 + y)}$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Change the time variable to $y$; the matter term and the expansion terms both become functions of $y$ alone.

<Step title="Derivatives">

$\dot\delta = yH\delta'$ and $\ddot\delta = yH\frac{d}{dy}(yH\delta') = y^2H^2\delta'' + yH^2\delta' + y^2H\frac{dH}{dy}\delta'$. So $\ddot\delta + 2H\dot\delta = y^2H^2\delta'' + yH^2\left(3 + \frac{y}{H}\frac{dH}{dy}\right)\delta'$, and $3 - \frac{4 + 3y}{2(1 + y)} = \frac{2 + 3y}{2(1 + y)}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The gravity term">

From $H^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho_m\frac{1 + y}{y}$: $4\pi G\rho_m = \frac32H^2\frac{y}{1 + y}$. Dividing the whole equation by $y^2H^2$ gives the equation in $y$ above.

</Step>

<Step title="The growing mode">

For $\delta = 1 + \tfrac32y$: $\delta'' = 0$ and $\delta' = \tfrac32$, so the left side is $\frac{\frac32(2 + 3y) - 3\left(1 + \frac32y\right)}{2y(1 + y)} = \frac{3 + \frac92y - 3 - \frac92y}{2y(1 + y)} = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The equation needs no assumption $y < 1$ (the book states it for the radiation era): it holds as long as the radiation stays smooth. For $y \gg 1$ the growing mode becomes $\delta \propto y \propto a$, the matter-era growth of the [linear perturbations](/courses/8-902/linear-perturbations) lesson. For $y \ll 1$ it is nearly constant: the stalling (Mészáros effect) of the [cold dark matter](/courses/8-902/why-cold-dark-matter) lesson.

</Step>

</Solution>
