Dynamics weighs a galaxy through the motions of its stars and gas, and that requires assumptions: circular orbits, equilibrium, a known anisotropy. Gravitational lensing weighs it in a completely different way. Mass bends the light from objects behind it, and the distorted images reveal the mass along the line of sight, **whatever its dynamical state**. The catch is that lensing needs a chance alignment with a bright, distant source, so only selected systems can be studied, and it measures only the mass **projected** onto the sky.

## The lens equation

The simplest lens has a distant source (usually a galaxy or quasar), a lensing mass in front of it, and us. Angles are measured on the sky from the line of sight through the lens centre: $\vec\beta$ is the true position of the source, $\vec\theta$ the observed position of an image (there may be several), and $\hat{\vec\alpha}$ the deflection angle at the lens. Simple geometry gives the **lens equation**:

<Formula label="The lens equation" tex="\vec\theta = \vec\beta + \frac{D_{LS}}{D_S}\,\hat{\vec\alpha}(\vec\theta)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.9), p. 32" />

The distances are **angular-diameter distances**: $D_L$ to the lens, $D_S$ to the source, and $D_{LS}$ from lens to source. For a flat universe with matter and a cosmological constant,

<Formula label="Angular-diameter distances" tex="D_L = \frac{c}{H_0 (1+z_L)}\int_0^{z_L}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}(1+z)^3 + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}}}, \qquad D_{LS} = \frac{c}{H_0 (1+z_S)}\int_{z_L}^{z_S}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}(1+z)^3 + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}}}" cite="mit" at="Eqs. (2.10)–(2.11), pp. 32–33" />

and $D_S$ like $D_L$ with $z_S$. Note that $D_{LS} \neq D_S - D_L$ in an expanding universe. (On this site, distances in cosmology are introduced in 8.286; these formulas assume a flat universe.)

## Why light bends

A gravitational potential $\Phi$ acts on light like a medium with an **effective index of refraction**

<Formula label="Effective index of refraction" tex="n(\vec r) = 1 + \frac{2}{c^2}\left|\Phi(\vec r)\right| = 1 + \frac{2G}{c^2}\int\frac{\rho(\vec r\,')}{|\vec r\,' - \vec r|}\,d^3r'" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.12), p. 33" />

and, as in optics, a ray bends towards the gradient of $n$ perpendicular to its path:

<Formula label="Deflection as a path integral" tex="\hat{\vec\alpha} = -\int \nabla_\perp n\,dl = \frac{2}{c^2}\int\nabla_\perp\Phi\,dl" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.13), p. 33" />

For small deflections the integral can be taken along the undeflected straight path. A Newtonian estimate, treating light as a particle moving at speed $c$, gives a deflection $2GM/(c^2 b)$ for a point mass passed at distance $b$. General relativity, solving for the light path in the Schwarzschild metric, gives **twice** that, $4GM/(c^2 b)$.

<WorkedExample title="Light bending at the edge of the Sun">
<Problem>
A light ray grazes the Sun's surface ($b = 6.96 \times 10^8$ m). Find the deflection in the Newtonian picture and in general relativity.
</Problem>
<Step title="Newtonian estimate">
The sideways pull integrated along the path changes the photon's direction by $2GM/(c^2 b) = 2 \times 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 1.989 \times 10^{30} / ((3.00 \times 10^8)^2 \times 6.96 \times 10^8) \approx 4.24 \times 10^{-6}$ rad, or about 0.87 arcseconds.
</Step>
<Step title="General relativity">
Twice as much: about 1.75 arcseconds. Eddington's 1919 eclipse expedition measured stars near the Sun shifted by about this amount, one of the first confirmations of general relativity.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Thin lenses

Most lenses are thin compared with the distances to source and observer, so the lens can be described by its **surface mass density** $\Sigma(\vec\xi) = \int\rho(\vec\xi, z)\,dz$ on the lens plane, with $\vec\xi = D_L\vec\theta$. The deflection then sums the contributions of every mass element:

<Formula label="Deflection by a thin lens" tex="\hat{\vec\alpha}(\vec\xi) = \frac{4G}{c^2}\int\Sigma(\vec\xi\,')\,\frac{\vec\xi - \vec\xi\,'}{|\vec\xi - \vec\xi\,'|^2}\,d^2\xi'" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.14), p. 34" />

Equivalently, define the **lensing potential**, a scaled projection of the gravitational potential,

<Formula label="Lensing potential" tex="\psi(\vec\theta) = \frac{2 D_{LS}}{c^2 D_L D_S}\int\Phi(D_L\vec\theta, z)\,dz" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.15), p. 34" />

whose gradient is the (scaled) deflection, $\vec\alpha = \nabla_\theta\psi$ (Eq. 2.16). For a circularly symmetric lens the deflection points along $\vec\theta$, so source, lens and images lie on one line. Like Poisson's equation in two dimensions, the Laplacian of $\psi$ gives the surface density:

<Formula label="Convergence and critical density" tex="\nabla_\theta^2\psi = 2\kappa, \qquad \kappa = \frac{\Sigma}{\Sigma_\text{cr}}, \qquad \Sigma_\text{cr} = \frac{c^2}{4\pi G}\,\frac{D_S}{D_L D_{LS}}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.17), p. 35" />

[The book's text after its Eq. (2.17) prints the critical density upside down, as $(4\pi G/c^2)(D_L D_{LS}/D_S)$; the form above is the one that follows from the equation itself.]

The **convergence** $\kappa$ is the surface density in units of the **critical density** $\Sigma_\text{cr}$, which depends only on the redshifts of lens and source (and the adopted cosmology). Lenses with $\Sigma \ll \Sigma_\text{cr}$ hardly lens; very high $\Sigma_\text{cr}$ makes lensing unlikely. That is why most lenses are found at intermediate redshift, lensing sources at high redshift. For a lens at $z_L = 0.5$ and a source at $z_S = 2$, $\Sigma_\text{cr} \approx 2100\,M_\odot$ per pc² (with $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc, $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.3$).

## Magnification, convergence and shear

Lensing neither emits nor absorbs light, so **surface brightness is conserved** (Liouville's theorem). An image is brighter than the source exactly as much as it is larger: the **magnification** is the ratio of image area to source area,

<Formula label="Magnification" tex="\mu = \frac{1}{\det A}, \qquad A_{ij} = \frac{\partial\beta_i}{\partial\theta_j} = \delta_{ij} - \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial\theta_i\,\partial\theta_j}" cite="mit" at="Eqs. (2.18)–(2.19), p. 35" />

Writing $\psi_{ij}$ for the second derivatives, the trace gives $\psi_{11} + \psi_{22} = 2\kappa$. Define the **shear** components $\gamma_1 = (\psi_{11} - \psi_{22})/2 = \gamma\cos 2\varphi$ and $\gamma_2 = \psi_{12} = \gamma\sin 2\varphi$. Then

<Formula label="The lensing matrix" tex="A = (1-\kappa)\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} - \gamma\begin{pmatrix}\cos 2\varphi & \sin 2\varphi\\ \sin 2\varphi & -\cos 2\varphi\end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.20), p. 36" />

The convergence scales an image uniformly; the shear stretches it along one direction, turning a circular source into an ellipse. The magnification is

<Formula label="Magnification from convergence and shear" tex="\mu = \frac{1}{(1-\kappa)^2 - \gamma^2}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.21), p. 36" />

[The book's Eq. (2.21) prints $(1 - \kappa^2)$ in the denominator; it should be $(1 - \kappa)^2$, as follows from Eq. (2.20).]

It diverges where $(1-\kappa)^2 = \gamma^2$, which without shear means $\kappa = 1$, a surface density near the critical one. There a small shift of the source changes the images enormously. The curves on the source plane where the number of images changes are called **caustics**.

For a **circularly symmetric** lens everything becomes one-dimensional. The deflection at $\theta$ depends only on the mass inside $\theta$:

<Formula label="Deflection of a symmetric lens" tex="\alpha(\theta) = \frac{2}{\theta}\int_0^\theta \theta'\,\kappa(\theta')\,d\theta'" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.22), p. 36" />

and the convergence and shear follow from it:

<Formula label="Convergence and shear of a symmetric lens" tex="\kappa = \frac12\left(\frac{\alpha}{\theta} + \frac{d\alpha}{d\theta}\right), \qquad \gamma = \frac12\left(\frac{\alpha}{\theta} - \frac{d\alpha}{d\theta}\right)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.23), p. 36" />

## The point mass and the Einstein ring

For a point mass $M$ the lens equation becomes a quadratic,

<Formula label="Lens equation for a point mass" tex="\theta^2 - \beta\theta - \theta_E^2 = 0, \qquad \theta_E^2 = \frac{4GM\,D_{LS}}{c^2 D_L D_S}" cite="mit" at="Eqs. (2.24)–(2.25), p. 37" />

with two images, $\theta_\pm = \tfrac12\left(\beta \pm \sqrt{\beta^2 + 4\theta_E^2}\right)$, one on each side of the lens. If the source lies exactly behind the lens ($\beta = 0$), symmetry turns the images into an **Einstein ring** of angular radius $\theta_E$, the **Einstein radius**. In units of $\theta_E$, with $x = \theta/\theta_E$ and $y = \beta/\theta_E$:

<Formula label="Point-mass images in Einstein units" tex="x_\pm = \frac12\left(y \pm \sqrt{y^2 + 4}\right)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.26), p. 37" />

This separates **strong** from **weak** lensing. Near $y = 0$ the effect is strongest. As $y$ grows, one image moves into the lens centre and becomes too faint to see, while the other approaches the true position $x \to y$. The transition happens around $y \approx 1$.

A point mass always makes two images (Eq. 2.26); the inner one fades as it approaches the centre. A singular isothermal sphere makes images at β ± θ_E, the second only while the source lies inside the Einstein radius." />

## Strong lensing

When the source lies within about an Einstein radius of the lens centre, we see **strong lensing**: arcs, rings and multiple images. Strong lensing requires a surface density close to critical, so lenses are almost always massive galaxies or galaxy clusters at intermediate redshift, lensing sources at high redshift.

A more realistic lens than a point mass is the **singular isothermal sphere**, the profile that gives flat rotation curves. With stars (or dark matter particles) of velocity dispersion $\sigma$, its projected density is $\Sigma(\xi) = \sigma^2/(2G\xi)$, its Einstein radius is

<Formula label="Einstein radius of an isothermal sphere" tex="\theta_E = 4\pi\,\frac{\sigma^2}{c^2}\,\frac{D_{LS}}{D_S}" cite="mit" at="Section 2.6, p. 38" />

and its images lie at $\theta = \beta \pm \theta_E$. To avoid the infinite central density, a **core** of radius $r_c$ can be added:

<Formula label="Cored isothermal sphere" tex="\rho(r) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\pi G}\,\frac{1}{r^2 + r_c^2}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.28), p. 38" />

Its lens equation, with $\theta_c = r_c/D_L$ the angular size of the core and $\theta_E$ as for the singular sphere, is

<Formula label="Lens equation of a cored isothermal sphere" tex="\beta = \theta - \theta_E\,\frac{\sqrt{\theta^2 + \theta_c^2} - \theta_c}{\theta}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.29), p. 38" />

Depending on where the source lies, such a lens produces one image or three, the change happening at the caustic. A third image near the centre is usually too faint to observe. (More generally, for a power-law density $\rho \propto r^{-n}$ the deflection grows as $\theta^{2-n}$: constant for the isothermal $n = 2$; Exercise 2.7.) Real lenses are not circular: the next step is an elliptical lensing potential, whose caustics have more complex shapes. Observed systems are mostly **doubles** and **quads** (two or four visible images), as in the CASTLES survey of lenses imaged with the Hubble Space Telescope.

<WorkedExample title="The Einstein radius of a lens galaxy">
<Problem>
An elliptical galaxy at $z_L = 0.5$ has a velocity dispersion of 250 km/s. A quasar lies behind it at $z_S = 2.0$. For $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc, $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.3$, $\Omega_{\Lambda,0} = 0.7$, the distance formulas give $D_L \approx 1259$ Mpc, $D_S \approx 1727$ Mpc and $D_{LS} \approx 1097$ Mpc. Find the Einstein radius, as an angle and in kpc.
</Problem>
<Step title="The distance ratio">
$D_{LS}/D_S \approx 0.635$. (Not $1 - D_L/D_S$: angular-diameter distances do not simply add.)
</Step>
<Step title="The angle">
$\theta_E = 4\pi \times (2.5 \times 10^5 / 3.00 \times 10^8)^2 \times 0.635 \approx 5.54 \times 10^{-6}$ rad $\approx 1.14$ arcseconds.
</Step>
<Step title="The physical size">
$\xi_E = D_L\theta_E \approx 1259\ \text{Mpc} \times 5.54 \times 10^{-6} \approx 7.0$ kpc, comparable to the visible size of the galaxy, which is why such lenses are well suited to weighing galaxies.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Weighing a lens and its dark matter

The positions of the images fix the mass inside the Einstein radius, but the answer depends on the model, as the next example shows. Ferreras works through a real case, the double quasar HE 2149−2745 (lens at $z = 0.60$, source at $z = 2.03$), comparing a point mass and an isothermal sphere in the same way. [Its point-mass numbers are not used here: the Einstein radius it quotes, 2.73″, is four times what its own image positions give, $\sqrt{1.354 \times 0.344} \approx 0.68″$, and the derived size and mass carry the same factor. Its isothermal values agree with the image positions.] The **magnification**, the flux ratio of the images, helps to decide between models.

<WorkedExample title="Point mass or isothermal sphere?">
<Problem>
A quasar at $z_S = 2.0$ appears as two images on opposite sides of a lens galaxy at $z_L = 0.5$, at 1.20″ and 0.40″ from the galaxy's centre. Using the distances of the previous example, find the Einstein radius, the true source position and the mass inside the Einstein radius for (a) a point mass and (b) a singular isothermal sphere. Then use the flux ratio to choose.
</Problem>
<Step title="Point mass">
The images satisfy $\theta_+\theta_- = \theta_E^2$ and $\theta_+ - \theta_- = \beta$ (with $\theta_-$ as a distance from the centre). So $\theta_E = \sqrt{1.20 \times 0.40} \approx 0.69″$ and $\beta = 0.80″$.
</Step>
<Step title="Isothermal sphere">
The images lie at $\beta \pm \theta_E$, so $\theta_E = (1.20 + 0.40)/2 = 0.80″$ and $\beta = (1.20 - 0.40)/2 = 0.40″$. The implied velocity dispersion is about 210 km/s.
</Step>
<Step title="The masses">
For any circular lens the mass inside the Einstein radius is $M = \pi\Sigma_\text{cr}(D_L\theta_E)^2 = \theta_E^2 c^2 D_L D_S/(4G D_{LS})$. This gives about $1.2 \times 10^{11}\,M_\odot$ for the point mass and $1.6 \times 10^{11}\,M_\odot$ for the isothermal sphere, a difference of a third, from the model alone.
</Step>
<Step title="The flux ratio decides">
A point mass magnifies its images by $|1 - (\theta_E/\theta)^4|^{-1}$, giving a flux ratio of 9 between the two images; an isothermal sphere magnifies by $|1 - \theta_E/\theta|^{-1}$, a ratio of 3. Measuring the flux ratio (in magnitudes, about 2.4 versus 1.2) tells which model fits.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The lensing mass can then be compared with the stellar mass from the lens galaxy's light and population models. Lenses are mostly early-type galaxies, whose ordinary matter is almost all in stars, so the difference is dark matter. Ferreras shows a sample of strong lenses (Leier and collaborators, 2011): within small apertures, the lensing mass is close to the stellar mass; as the aperture grows to several effective radii, the lensing mass pulls ever further ahead. Like the rotation curves, lensing shows **dark matter dominating the outer regions** of galaxies.

## Summary

- Lensing weighs projected mass independently of dynamics, but only where a background source happens to be aligned.
- The lens equation $\vec\beta = \vec\theta - (D_{LS}/D_S)\hat{\vec\alpha}$ uses angular-diameter distances. Mass acts like a refractive index $n = 1 - 2\Phi/c^2$; general relativity doubles the Newtonian deflection (1.75″ at the Sun's edge).
- In the thin-lens description, $\nabla^2\psi = 2\kappa$ with $\kappa = \Sigma/\Sigma_\text{cr}$; images are magnified by $\mu = 1/[(1-\kappa)^2 - \gamma^2]$, with κ scaling and γ stretching them.
- A point mass gives two images and, for perfect alignment, an Einstein ring with $\theta_E^2 = 4GM D_{LS}/(c^2 D_L D_S)$; an isothermal sphere has $\theta_E = 4\pi\sigma^2 D_{LS}/(c^2 D_S)$.
- Image positions fix the mass inside the Einstein radius only up to the model; flux ratios help. Compared with stellar masses, lenses show dark matter growing in importance with radius.
