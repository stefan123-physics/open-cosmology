So far dark matter has been defined by what it does: it gravitates. At the smallest scales it should be a **particle**, and its properties must fit everything the astronomy of galaxies, large-scale structure and the cosmic microwave background tells us. Part of the mass budget could be in known particles (neutrinos, or ordinary gas in the warm-hot intergalactic medium), but most of it seems to be a non-baryonic particle not yet found in any laboratory. Ferreras's Chapter 6 reviews the candidates; this page covers the background and the neutrino.

## A particle physics primer

Three of the four forces, electromagnetism and the weak and strong nuclear forces, are described by quantum field theories; gravity has so far resisted joining them. The **Standard Model** combines the three in one symmetry group:

<Formula label="Standard Model gauge group" tex="SU(3) \times SU(2) \times U(1)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (6.1), p. 133" />

$SU(3)$ is the strong force (quantum chromodynamics, QCD); $SU(2) \times U(1)$ is the unified electroweak force (on this site: [the Standard Model and grand unification](/courses/8-286/grand-unified-theories)). Eight **gluons** bind six kinds of **quarks** (up, down, charm, strange, top, bottom) into hadrons: a proton is $uud$, a neutron $udd$. The $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ bosons carry the weak force, felt by the **leptons** (electron, muon, tau and their neutrinos) and by quarks too: a free neutron decays after some minutes into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino. Photons carry electromagnetism. Every particle has an **antiparticle** with the same mass and spin and opposite charges; a photon is its own antiparticle, and two energetic photons (or one photon passing a nucleus) can create an electron–positron pair.

Each particle is characterised by a few properties: its charges under the three forces, its mass and its spin (which makes it a boson or a fermion). A dark matter particle would be one more entry: **massive**, **stable** over cosmic times, and with **negligible** (but not necessarily zero) interactions through the three quantum forces. Most candidates were in thermal equilibrium in the early universe (on this site: [freeze-out](/courses/8-902/dark-matter-freeze-out)); some, such as axions, were not.

**Matter and antimatter.** When the temperature drops below a particle's mass, particle–antiparticle pairs annihilate. Equal numbers would leave nothing; yet electrons and protons remain, so a small **asymmetry** must have existed. The annihilations also explain the huge number of photons per baryon, about a billion. For ultra-relativistic fermions with chemical potential $\mu$ (and $-\mu$ for the antiparticles), the excess of particles over antiparticles is

<Formula label="Particle–antiparticle excess" tex="n - \bar n = \frac{g\,T^3}{6\pi^2}\left[\pi^2\left(\frac{\mu}{T}\right) + \left(\frac{\mu}{T}\right)^3\right]" cite="mit" at="Eq. (6.2), p. 136" />

(in units with $\hbar = c = k_B = 1$; $g$ counts the spin states). A non-zero excess needs a non-zero chemical potential, so at some point baryon or lepton number cannot have been conserved. Explaining this, **baryogenesis**, is one of the great open problems of particle physics.

**A thermal timeline.** At the highest temperatures all species were in a single hot soup (Gamow's "ylem"). Then:

- about $3 \times 10^{12}$ K, $10^{-5}$ s: quarks are confined into hadrons (the QCD transition);
- about 1 MeV, 1 s: neutrinos decouple; a few seconds later electrons and positrons annihilate, heating the photons but not the neutrinos;
- about $10^9$ K, a few minutes: nucleosynthesis makes deuterium, helium-4 and traces of other nuclei;
- a few thousand kelvin, a few hundred thousand years: electrons and nuclei recombine and the universe becomes transparent.

The photon–baryon coupling until late times means baryons could not have seeded today's structure (see [why cold dark matter](/courses/8-902/why-cold-dark-matter)), and nucleosynthesis fixes the baryon density at $\Omega_b h^2 \approx 0.02$, well below the dark matter density $\Omega_\text{dm} h^2 \approx 0.12$. Dark matter must be something else.

## Hot, warm and cold

Candidates span an enormous range of masses. The decisive property is whether a particle was **relativistic** when it decoupled, because that leaves a mark on how structure forms:

- **Hot dark matter**: highly relativistic at decoupling. Its particles stream out of small fluctuations and erase them, suppressing the power spectrum on small scales. Large structures collapse first, and galaxies form later by fragmentation, **top-down**.
- **Cold dark matter**: non-relativistic at decoupling. Fluctuations survive on small scales, small halos form first and grow by accretion and mergers, **bottom-up**.
- **Warm dark matter**: in between; fluctuations are erased below some scale, but growth is still bottom-up.

These differences show up in the **matter power spectrum** $P(k)$, with $k \sim 1/\lambda$ for a length scale $\lambda$, which the clustering of galaxies measures. The scale-invariant Harrison–Zeldovich spectrum, $P(k) \propto k$, is modified by a **transfer function** that differs between the three flavours, removing much more small-scale power for warm and especially hot dark matter. Combined measurements (Planck, DES, SDSS, BOSS) agree with the ΛCDM prediction over many orders of magnitude in scale, one of the strongest supports for cold dark matter. Hot dark matter would strongly suppress power above about $k \approx 0.1\,h$ Mpc⁻¹, and is firmly rejected.

## Neutrinos

The neutrino is a natural first suspect: it exists, it interacts only weakly, and there are many of them. Pauli proposed it in 1930 to rescue energy conservation in beta decay. The Standard Model has three flavours, matching the electron, muon and tau. Neutrinos were long thought massless, but **oscillations** (a neutrino born as one flavour detected as another, which first explained the deficit of solar neutrinos) show that they have mass and that the mass states are not the flavour states. Experiments measure only differences of squared masses, allowing a **normal** ordering (the two lightest states close together) or an **inverted** one (the two heaviest close together).

Neutrinos stayed in equilibrium as long as their reaction rate beat the expansion. For weak interactions $\langle\sigma v\rangle \propto G_F^2T^2$, so the rate $\Gamma = n\langle\sigma v\rangle \propto G_F^2T^5$, while in the radiation era $H^2 \propto GT^4$. Setting $\Gamma \approx H$:

<Formula label="Neutrino decoupling" tex="T_{\nu,\text{dec}}^3 \sim \frac{G^{1/2}}{G_F^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T_{\nu,\text{dec}} \approx 1\ \text{MeV}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (6.3), p. 141" />

reached about one second after the Big Bang. Since then the neutrinos have streamed freely as the **cosmic neutrino background**, cooling as $1/a$. Electron–positron annihilation then heated only the photons. Entropy conservation gives the ratio of the two temperatures,

<Formula label="Neutrino and photon temperatures" tex="\frac{T_{\nu,2}}{T_{\gamma,2}} = \left(\frac{g_{*s,2}}{g_{*s,1}}\right)^{1/3} = \left(\frac{4}{11}\right)^{1/3} \approx 0.714" cite="mit" at="Eqs. (6.4)–(6.5), p. 141" />

so with the CMB at 2.725 K, the neutrino background is at about 1.95 K today (on this site derived in [neutrinos and particle counting](/courses/8-286/neutrinos-and-particle-counting)). Detecting it directly is beyond any foreseeable technology; it would show the universe one second after the Big Bang.

With $N_\nu$ species the present number density is

<Formula label="Cosmic neutrino number density" tex="n_{\nu,0} = 2N_\nu\,\frac34\,\frac{\zeta(3)}{\pi^2}\,T_{\nu,0}^3" cite="mit" at="Eq. (6.6), p. 142" />

(neutrinos plus antineutrinos, one helicity each), about 112 per cm³ per flavour. [The book's Eq. (6.6) gives about 108; its own temperature of 1.95 K gives 112. The difference changes no conclusion.] For an average mass $m_\nu$ the density parameter is

<Formula label="Neutrino density parameter" tex="\Omega_{\nu,0}\,h^2 \approx 0.01\,N_\nu\left(\frac{m_\nu}{\text{eV}}\right)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (6.7), p. 142" />

The width of the $Z^0$ decay shows that $N_\nu = 3$, so neutrinos could make up all the dark matter ($\Omega_\text{dm}h^2 \approx 0.12$) only if each had about 4 eV. And even then they would be **hot** dark matter, ruled out by the clustering of galaxies.

<WorkedExample title="How much do neutrinos weigh in the universe?">

</Problem>
<Step title="Mass density">
The total number is $3 \times 112 = 336$ per cm³; with an average mass of $0.1/3$ eV each, the mass-energy density is $112 \times 0.1 = 11.2$ eV per cm³.
</Step>
<Step title="Density parameter">
The critical density is $\rho_c \approx 1.05 \times 10^4\,h^2$ eV per cm³, so $\Omega_\nu h^2 \approx 11.2/1.05 \times 10^4 \approx 0.0011$.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
That is about 1% of the dark matter's $0.12$: real, but minor. It equals $\sum m_\nu/(94\ \text{eV})$, the usual form of the formula.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

**Sterile neutrinos.** In the electroweak theory, left-handed leptons come in pairs (doublets) with weak isospin $\pm\tfrac12$: $(\nu_e, e^-)_L$, $(\nu_\mu, \mu^-)_L$, $(\nu_\tau, \tau^-)_L$. They feel the weak force through $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$. Right-handed leptons have zero weak isospin and come singly, $e_R$, $\mu_R$, $\tau_R$ (Eqs. 6.8–6.9). The Standard Model has **no right-handed neutrinos**. An extension can add them: with zero isospin they would not feel the weak force, hence **sterile**, but they could have mass, and could be dark matter.

## Summary

- Dark matter should be a new particle: massive, stable, with negligible strong, weak and electromagnetic interactions. The Standard Model is $SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)$; the matter–antimatter asymmetry (baryogenesis) is unexplained.
- Nucleosynthesis gives $\Omega_b h^2 \approx 0.02$ against $\Omega_\text{dm} h^2 \approx 0.12$: most dark matter is not baryonic.
- Hot dark matter (relativistic at decoupling) forms structure top-down and erases small-scale power; cold dark matter forms it bottom-up. The measured power spectrum rules out hot dark matter as the main component.
- Neutrinos decoupled at about 1 MeV and now form a 1.95 K background of about 112 per cm³ per flavour. With $\Omega_\nu h^2 \approx 0.01N_\nu(m_\nu/\text{eV})$ they would need about 4 eV each to be the dark matter, and would be hot. Sterile (right-handed) neutrinos remain possible candidates.
