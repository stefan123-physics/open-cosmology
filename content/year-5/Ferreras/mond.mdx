Most of this course follows the majority view: rotation curves and cluster dynamics need extra, invisible mass. Ferreras's Chapter 7 presents the main alternative. If dark matter has never been detected outside gravity, perhaps what is wrong is **gravity itself**.

## The problem restated

Newtonian gravity works on small scales: the Solar System, even the inner kiloparsec of our Galaxy. But beyond some radius $R_0$, typically about 10 kpc, the measured rotation speeds are too high for the observed mass in stars and gas:

<Formula label="Rotation with the observed mass" tex="v^2(R) = \frac{G M_\text{OBS}(R)}{R} \quad\text{fails for}\ R \gtrsim R_0" cite="mit" at="Eq. (7.1), p. 158" />

The standard fix adds mass, $M_\text{TOT} = M_\text{OBS} + M_\text{DM}$:

<Formula label="Rotation with dark matter" tex="v^2(R) = \frac{G M_\text{TOT}(R)}{R}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (7.2), p. 158" />

Newton's laws are really about **acceleration**, so the same statement reads

<Formula label="The same in terms of acceleration" tex="a(R) = \frac{v^2(R)}{R} = \frac{G M_\text{TOT}(R)}{R^2}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (7.3), p. 158" />

The alternative is to keep $M_\text{OBS}$ and suppose that the true acceleration in the outskirts of galaxies is somehow **larger** than Newton predicts.

## Modified Newtonian Dynamics

That is the proposal of **Mordecai Milgrom** and others: **Modified Newtonian Dynamics** (MOND). It is an ad-hoc hypothesis, but worth pursuing because of its observational successes, and because gravity, Newtonian or relativistic, has been tested almost exclusively at accelerations far higher than those in galaxy outskirts, exactly where dark matter is invoked.

MOND changes the relation between acceleration and the Newtonian potential $\Phi_N$ of the visible mass:

<Formula label="The MOND law" tex="f\!\left(\frac{|\vec a|}{a_0}\right)\vec a = -\nabla\Phi_N" cite="mit" at="Eq. (7.4), p. 159" />

where $f$ is an **interpolating function**. For two masses $M$ and $m$, the force becomes

<Formula label="MOND force between two masses" tex="F(r) = \begin{cases}\dfrac{GMm}{r^2} & GM/r^2 \gg a_0\\[2ex] \dfrac{m\sqrt{GMa_0}}{r} & GM/r^2 \ll a_0\end{cases}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (7.5), p. 160" />

(MOND can also be read as a change to inertia, to $F = ma$ itself, but that is more radical and raises problems with conservation laws, so it is usually treated as modified gravity.) Note what is **not** changed: the dependence on distance. The modification switches on at low **acceleration**, wherever that happens.

The scale $a_0$ is set where Newtonian gravity with visible matter stops working, by fitting rotation curves: about $10^{-10}$ m/s². Observations support the choice of acceleration over distance. In near-infrared light, where stellar populations change the mass-to-light ratio little, the **dynamical** mass-to-light ratio of disc galaxies correlates poorly with radius but tightly with the local acceleration (Sanders and McGaugh). And **low surface brightness galaxies**, whose accelerations are low everywhere, need "dark matter" throughout, as MOND predicts.

The function $f$ must approach 1 at high accelerations (Newton) and $a/a_0$ at low ones. Then, for the same mass, a small $f$ means a **larger** acceleration and faster rotation. The exact form matters little; common choices are $f(x) = x/(1+x)$ and $f(x) = x/\sqrt{1+x^2}$. The law can be written either way round: as the Newtonian acceleration in terms of the true one, $a_N = a\,f(a/a_0)$ (Eq. 7.6), or inverted, $a = \tilde f^{-1/2}(a_N/a_0)\,a_N$ with a function $\tilde f$ of the same limits (Eq. 7.7).

**Why flat rotation curves follow.** Far out, a galaxy acts like a point mass $M$ and $a \ll a_0$, so $a = \sqrt{GMa_0}/r$. Setting $a = v^2/r$:

<Formula label="Deep-MOND rotation speed" tex="v^4 = G M a_0" cite="mit" at="follows from Eq. (7.5), p. 160" />

Now the speed no longer depends on $r$. Flat rotation curves come out of the visible mass alone, and so does a **Tully–Fisher relation** with exponent 4 between baryonic mass and rotation speed.

Ferreras shows the disc galaxy **NGC 2403** (data from the SPARC database): the stars and gas, added in quadrature, match the measured rotation only in the centre and fall short beyond about 5 kpc. With MOND, the gravitating mass recovered from the curve follows the observed stars and gas: no dark matter needed. This works for practically all disc galaxies.

<WorkedExample title="A galaxy weighed with MOND">
<Problem>
At $R = 15$ kpc, a disc galaxy rotates at 150 km/s, while its stars and gas alone (Newtonian) would give 90 km/s. (a) How much dark matter would Newtonian gravity need there, as a fraction of the visible mass? (b) With $f(x) = x/(1+x)$, what value of $a_0$ explains the data without dark matter?
</Problem>
<Step title="Dark matter needed by Newton">
Mass scales as $v^2$ at fixed radius: $M_\text{TOT}/M_\text{OBS} = (150/90)^2 \approx 2.78$, so dark matter would be about 1.78 times the visible mass inside 15 kpc.
</Step>
<Step title="The two accelerations">
$R = 15 \times 3.086 \times 10^{19}$ m $= 4.63 \times 10^{20}$ m. True: $a = v^2/R = (1.5 \times 10^5)^2/4.63 \times 10^{20} \approx 4.86 \times 10^{-11}$ m/s². Newtonian: $a_N = (9 \times 10^4)^2/4.63 \times 10^{20} \approx 1.75 \times 10^{-11}$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Solve for a₀">
$a_N = a\,x/(1+x)$ with $x = a/a_0$ gives $x = a_N/(a - a_N)$, so $a_0 = a(a - a_N)/a_N = 4.86 \times 10^{-11} \times 3.11/1.75 \approx 8.6 \times 10^{-11}$ m/s², the expected order of magnitude.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="MOND and the Solar System">
<Problem>
Compare the Sun's pull on Neptune (orbital radius 30.07 AU) with $a_0 = 10^{-10}$ m/s². And what flat rotation speed does MOND predict for a galaxy of $5 \times 10^{10}\,M_\odot$ of stars and gas?
</Problem>
<Step title="Neptune">
$a = GM_\odot/r^2 = 1.327 \times 10^{20}/(4.50 \times 10^{12})^2 \approx 6.6 \times 10^{-6}$ m/s², about 66 000 times $a_0$. Deep in the Newtonian regime: MOND would change nothing measurable in the Solar System.
</Step>
<Step title="A galaxy">
$v = (GMa_0)^{1/4} = (6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 9.94 \times 10^{40} \times 10^{-10})^{1/4} \approx 1.6 \times 10^5$ m/s, about 160 km/s (or 168 km/s with $a_0 = 1.2 \times 10^{-10}$ m/s²), the kind of flat speed the dark-matter example on the rotation-curves page needed a large halo to explain.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A puzzling constant

MOND answers the rotation-curve problem by raising a deeper one: why should there be an **absolute acceleration** $a_0$? Speeds and accelerations are measured relative to a frame, and relativity rests on the principle that physical laws do not depend on the frame. MOND seems to break this.

There is an intriguing coincidence, though. The Hubble parameter $H = \dot a/a$ has units of inverse time, so $cH$ is an acceleration. Today $cH_0 \approx 9.7h \times 10^{-10}$ m/s², with $h \approx 0.7$, about six to seven times $a_0$. Coincidence, or a sign that MOND is connected to cosmology?

A modification that depends on **distance** instead, for example a force that switches from $1/r^2$ to $1/(r\,r_0)$ beyond a fixed length $r_0 \sim 10$ kpc (Eq. 7.8), does not work: it fails to give the Tully–Fisher relation, because $v^2 = GM/r_0$ would make $L \propto v^2$ rather than $v^4$. Acceleration, not distance, is what the data single out.

## Summary

- Newtonian gravity with visible matter fails beyond about 10 kpc in galaxies. Instead of adding dark matter, MOND changes gravity below an acceleration $a_0 \approx 10^{-10}$ m/s²: $f(a/a_0)\,\vec a = -\nabla\Phi_N$.
- The force becomes $m\sqrt{GMa_0}/r$ at low accelerations, giving flat rotation curves with $v^4 = GMa_0$ and a baryonic Tully–Fisher relation with exponent 4.
- The modification depends on acceleration, not distance: supported by the mass-to-light ratios of galaxies and by low surface brightness galaxies. A distance-based modification fails the Tully–Fisher test.
- MOND fits disc galaxies such as NGC 2403 with stars and gas alone, and leaves the Solar System (accelerations tens of thousands of times $a_0$) untouched.
- It needs an absolute acceleration scale; $cH_0 \approx 9.7h \times 10^{-10}$ m/s² is suspiciously close.
