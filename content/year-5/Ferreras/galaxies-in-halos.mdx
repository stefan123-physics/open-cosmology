The previous page described how simulations work. This one, following Ferreras's Sections 4.5–4.7, asks how they are tested against real galaxies, and how simpler statistical models tie galaxies to the halos they live in.

## Constraining the simulations

The dark matter part of a simulation is trustworthy: its initial conditions are simple and its evolution is pure gravity (apart from the baryons' back-reaction, such as adiabatic contraction, and assuming dark matter really is collisionless). The weak point is the **subgrid physics**: how efficiently gas turns into stars and how strongly stars and black holes push back. And every test must use visible matter, whose modelling is exactly what is uncertain. So simulations are **calibrated** on observations.

The most basic constraint is the **galaxy stellar mass function** (GSMF): the number of galaxies per unit comoving volume in each range of stellar mass, measured from large surveys such as SDSS. It is well described by a **Schechter function**:

<Formula label="Schechter function" tex="\Phi(M) = \Phi_0\left(\frac{M}{M_*}\right)^{\alpha} e^{-M/M_*}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (4.13), p. 94" />

with a characteristic mass $M_*$ above which galaxies become exponentially rare, a power-law slope $\alpha$ at low masses, and a normalisation $\Phi_0$. Galaxies live in halos, so the halo mass function is the starting point, but stellar mass does **not** simply follow halo mass, because star formation is very inefficient in both low- and high-mass halos. Different subgrid recipes can give the same GSMF (the problem is degenerate), so further tests are needed, usually at low redshift where data are best:

- the **size–mass** and **morphology–mass** relations, which test how angular momentum flows (early simulations without feedback made discs that were too small and compact);
- the **Tully–Fisher** relation and its baryonic version, which MOND proponents also invoke (Chapter 7);
- the **age–mass** and **metallicity–mass** relations, and the **colour–magnitude** diagram with its two populations: the **blue cloud** of star-forming, mostly late-type galaxies and the **red sequence** of quiescent, mostly early-type ones.

## Halo occupation

Simulations are expensive, so simpler **phenomenological** models are useful. **Halo occupation distribution** (HOD) models describe statistically how galaxies populate halos. They aim at galaxy samples like those of real surveys, which are limited in brightness and miss faint dwarfs, so a threshold in stellar mass or luminosity is imposed. Galaxies are split into **centrals** (the main galaxy of a halo) and **satellites** (galaxies that fell in earlier with their own halos and now orbit the central).

The key difficulty is **bias**: galaxies are not distributed exactly like the mass. Clustering is measured by the **two-point correlation function** $\xi(r)$, the excess probability of finding two galaxies a distance $r$ apart compared with a random distribution. Surveys find a power law:

<Formula label="Galaxy correlation function" tex="\xi(r) = \left(\frac{r}{r_0}\right)^{-\gamma}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (4.14), p. 96" />

with a **correlation length** $r_0 \approx 3$–5 Mpc and $\gamma \approx 1.8$, depending on the galaxy sample. On small scales $\xi$ counts pairs of galaxies inside one halo (the **1-halo term**, governed by baryon physics); on large scales pairs in different halos (the **2-halo term**, governed by the dark matter); the switch happens at the size of large halos, about 1–2 Mpc. The **bias** compares galaxies with dark matter:

<Formula label="Bias" tex="b(r) = \left[\frac{\xi_g(r)}{\xi_\text{DM}(r)}\right]^{1/2}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (4.15), p. 97" />

(the square root because the bias is defined on the density contrast, $\delta_g = b\,\delta_\text{DM}$, and $\xi$ is quadratic in δ). Red, old, massive galaxies are more biased, more clustered, than blue discs: they formed in rarer peaks of the density field. Halos themselves are biased tracers of the dark matter, since massive halos sit preferentially in overdense regions.

An HOD model gives the probability $P(N\,|\,M_h)$ that a halo of mass $M_h$ hosts $N$ galaxies, fitted to semi-analytic or hydrodynamical simulations. Its mean,

<Formula label="Mean halo occupation" tex="\langle N\rangle(M_h) = \sum_{N=0}^{\infty} N\,P(N\,|\,M_h)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (4.16), p. 98" />

is about 1 near the threshold mass and grows with halo mass; the second moment gives the scatter. A standard five-parameter form splits $\langle N\rangle = N_\text{cen} + N_\text{sat}$, with

<Formula label="Central galaxies" tex="N_\text{cen} = \frac12\left[1 + \operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{\log M - \log M_\text{min}}{\sigma_{\log M}}\right)\right]" cite="mit" at="Eq. (4.17), p. 98" />

<Formula label="Satellite galaxies" tex="N_\text{sat} = \begin{cases}\left(\dfrac{M}{M_1}\right)^{\alpha} & M > M_0\\[1ex] 0 & M \leq M_0\end{cases}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (4.18), p. 98" />

To build a mock catalogue, a central galaxy is placed at each halo's centre and satellites are scattered following the halo's density profile. Centrals are usually drawn from a Bernoulli distribution and satellites from a Poisson distribution.

<WorkedExample title="How many galaxies in a cluster-mass halo?">
<Problem>
With $\log M_\text{min} = \log M_0 = 12$, $\sigma_{\log M} = 0.2$, $\log M_1 = 13.3$ and $\alpha = 1$, find $\langle N\rangle$ for halos of $10^{12}$ and $10^{14}\,M_\odot$.
</Problem>
<Step title="A halo at the threshold">
For $M = 10^{12}\,M_\odot$: $N_\text{cen} = \tfrac12[1 + \text{erf}(0)] = 0.5$ and $N_\text{sat} = 0$, so $\langle N\rangle = 0.5$.
</Step>
<Step title="A cluster-mass halo">
For $M = 10^{14}\,M_\odot$: $N_\text{cen} = \tfrac12[1 + \text{erf}(10)] = 1$, and $N_\text{sat} = 10^{14}/10^{13.3} \approx 5.0$. So $\langle N\rangle \approx 6$ galaxies above the survey threshold.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Mass is assumed to be the only "environment variable" here. Halos of equal mass can have different histories (a **fossil group**, for instance, assembled differently from a normal group), so extensions add concentration, formation time or spin: **assembly bias** again. A further step, the **conditional luminosity function** $\Phi(L\,|\,M_h)$, predicts galaxy luminosities as well as numbers:

<Formula label="Conditional luminosity function" tex="\Phi(L) = \int_0^\infty \Phi(L\,|\,M_h)\,n(M_h)\,dM_h, \qquad \langle N\rangle(M_h) = \int_{L_\text{min}}^\infty \Phi(L\,|\,M_h)\,dL" cite="mit" at="Eqs. (4.19)–(4.20), p. 99" />

with $n(M_h)$ the halo mass function and $L_\text{min}$ the survey's threshold. It too splits into centrals (log-normal) and satellites (Schechter-like).

## Abundance matching

**Abundance matching** assumes that every halo hosts a galaxy at its centre, and that halos and galaxies grow together. Matching the **observed** stellar mass function to the **theoretical** halo mass function (from Press–Schechter or, better, simulations), the most massive galaxies to the most massive halos and so on down, gives a one-to-one relation between halo mass and stellar mass. Both distributions have a power law at low mass and an exponential cut-off at high mass, and their different shapes make the ratio $M_\star/M_\text{DM}$ peak at one mass and fall on both sides.

The results (Ferreras uses the relation of Moster and collaborators, 2010) are striking:

- Even at its peak, the stellar mass is far below the **cosmological limit**: what you would get if every baryon became a star, given the cosmic ratio of baryons to dark matter. Today the peak is about **3%** of the dark matter mass, against a cosmic baryon-to-dark-matter ratio of **18.6%**: only about **a sixth** of the baryons have turned into stars.
- At redshift 1.5, when the universe was only about 4 billion years old, the peak was lower still.
- The peak moves to lower halo masses with time: star formation has shifted towards smaller galaxies, a trend called **downsizing**.

The falling branches reflect feedback: **supernovae** suppress star formation in small halos, **AGNs** in large ones. Most baryons are not in galaxies at all but in warm-hot intergalactic gas, and in clusters in the hot intracluster medium seen in X-rays. Dark matter halos grow unimpeded by gravity alone; galaxies grow only as fast as gas inflow, cooling and feedback allow.

<WorkedExample title="Where are a halo's baryons?">
<Problem>
A halo of $10^{12}\,M_\odot$ is near the peak of the stellar-to-halo relation, with stars amounting to 3% of its dark matter mass. How much mass is in stars, how much baryonic mass did the halo start with (cosmic ratio of baryons to dark matter, 18.6%), and what fraction became stars?
</Problem>
<Step title="Stars">
$0.03 \times 10^{12} = 3 \times 10^{10}\,M_\odot$, about half the Milky Way's stellar mass.
</Step>
<Step title="Baryons available">
$0.186 \times 10^{12} \approx 1.9 \times 10^{11}\,M_\odot$.
</Step>
<Step title="Efficiency">
$3 \times 10^{10} / 1.86 \times 10^{11} \approx 0.16$: about one sixth. The rest is hot gas in the halo, gas expelled by feedback, or gas that never fell in.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Simulations are calibrated on the galaxy stellar mass function (a Schechter function), size–mass and morphology–mass relations, the (baryonic) Tully–Fisher relation, and age, metallicity and colour relations. Different subgrid recipes can match the same data.
- Galaxies cluster as $\xi(r) = (r/r_0)^{-\gamma}$ with $r_0 \approx 3$–5 Mpc; the bias $b = (\xi_g/\xi_\text{DM})^{1/2}$ links them to the dark matter; 1-halo and 2-halo terms meet at about 1–2 Mpc.
- Halo occupation models give the mean number of centrals and satellites per halo mass; the conditional luminosity function adds luminosities.
- Abundance matching gives a stellar-to-halo ratio peaking at about 3% today, against a cosmic baryon-to-dark-matter ratio of 18.6%: star formation is inefficient at all masses, because of feedback, and the peak shifts to lower masses with time (downsizing).
