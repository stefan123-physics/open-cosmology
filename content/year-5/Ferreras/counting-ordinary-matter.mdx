The rotation curves and cluster dynamics of the previous pages measure **all** the mass. Dark matter is then what is left after subtracting the ordinary, baryonic matter, essentially stars and gas. Dust, planets and the like do not matter for the budget: even Jupiter has only about a thousandth of the Sun's mass. So the case for dark matter rests on measuring stellar and gas masses well, and on knowing their uncertainties.

## Stellar mass from light

The mass in stars is estimated from the light in a passband that traces the stars that hold most of the mass. That means **red and near-infrared** light (about 0.6–2 μm in the galaxy's rest frame), where the numerous low-mass stars (below about one solar mass) shine. Massive stars are extremely luminous, mostly in the ultraviolet and blue (below about 0.4 μm), but there are few of them, so they add little mass.

How many stars of each mass a population is born with is the **initial mass function** (IMF). Traditionally it is a power law with a tapered low-mass end. The simplest version is Salpeter's single power law, $dN/dM \propto M^{-2.35}$. [The book's Exercises 2.3 and 2.4 write this slope per logarithmic mass interval, $dN/d\log M$; Salpeter's 2.35 is the slope per unit mass, which is the form used here.] The IMF is usually assumed to be the same everywhere, although some studies find exceptions: a **bottom-heavy** IMF (extra low-mass stars) raises the stellar mass-to-light ratio, as found in the cores of the most massive galaxies, and a **top-heavy** one (extra massive stars) lowers it, as in strongly star-forming galaxies. This is an important uncertainty, but Ferreras stresses that the dark matter evidence comes from the outskirts of galaxies, where stars contribute little whatever the IMF.

<WorkedExample title="Where the stellar mass is">
<Problem>
For a Salpeter IMF, $dN/dM \propto M^{-2.35}$, between 0.1 and 100 $M_\odot$, find the fraction of stars, by number and by mass, (a) below 1 $M_\odot$ and (b) between 8 and 50 $M_\odot$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Number and mass integrals">
The number between $M_1$ and $M_2$ is proportional to $\int M^{-2.35}\,dM = \left[M^{-1.35}/(-1.35)\right]$, and the mass to $\int M \cdot M^{-2.35}\,dM = \left[M^{-0.35}/(-0.35)\right]$.
</Step>
<Step title="Low-mass stars">
Stars of 0.1–1 $M_\odot$ are about 96% of all stars by number and about 61% of the mass.
</Step>
<Step title="Massive stars">
Stars of 8–50 $M_\odot$ are only about 0.25% of the stars but hold about 11% of the mass at birth, and they die within tens of millions of years, leaving black holes or neutron stars far lighter than the original stars.
</Step>
<Step title="Conclusion">
In an old population nearly all the stellar mass is in low-mass stars. Since these are cool, and a star's luminosity rises steeply with its surface temperature ($L \propto R^2 T^4$), their light is red. Hence red or infrared light is the right measure of stellar mass.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Light alone is not enough: the conversion needs the **stellar mass-to-light ratio** $\Upsilon_s = M_s/L$ of the population. It is found from **colours** (flux ratios between passbands) and, better, **spectra**, which constrain the distribution of stellar ages and metallicities. Models of **simple stellar populations** (all stars with one age and one chemical composition, born with a chosen IMF) predict $\Upsilon_s$ as a function of age. Ferreras compares two independent sets of such models (Bruzual & Charlot 2003, and MILES by Vazdekis and collaborators 2010): at solar metallicity and a Chabrier IMF (a good description of populations like the Milky Way's), they agree closely.

The bigger changes come from the population itself:

- **Lower metallicity** lowers $\Upsilon_s$: metal-poor stars have smaller opacities and shine brighter.
- **A different IMF** changes it even more, especially at old ages. A Salpeter IMF has many more low-mass stars than a Chabrier IMF, adding mass without adding much light.

As a population ages its mass-to-light ratio **rises**: the most massive stars, which give most of the light but little of the mass, die first, while the faint low-mass stars live on (the book's Figure 2.4 and Exercise 2.4 follow this with a simple power-law model). The crucial point: however these populations are varied, the V-band mass-to-light ratio **does not rise above about 10**. The outskirts of galaxies and galaxy clusters have total mass-to-light ratios of **tens to a few hundred**. Faint stars cannot explain that.

Population models have their own systematic problems. Different combinations of parameters can produce nearly the same observations (most notably the **age–metallicity degeneracy**, in which an older age looks like a higher metallicity), and IMF changes can mimic each other too. Careful combinations of colours and spectral features break these degeneracies partly; the best-case uncertainty of a stellar mass is about **0.2 dex** (a factor of about 1.6). In rotation-curve studies a standard test is the **maximal disc**: give the stars the largest mass-to-light ratio the inner rotation curve allows. In many galaxies even this fails, confirming the need for a dark component.

## Gas

Gas masses come from emission lines: the **21 cm line** of atomic hydrogen at radio wavelengths, and molecular lines such as those of **CO** for molecular hydrogen. The hydrogen mass is then corrected for helium using its mass fraction $Y$, which starts at about 0.24 from Big Bang nucleosynthesis and grows slowly as stars process their gas. The gas mass is therefore about $1/(1-Y) \approx 1.3$ times the hydrogen mass (ignoring the small fraction of heavier elements). Even so, the gas is never enough to remove the need for dark matter.

## Stellar systems without dark matter

If dark matter matters only when large volumes add up its low density, small systems should show little of it. Against this, star formation in small systems is easily disrupted, which can lower their stellar mass and leave them dark-matter dominated (Chapter 4 returns to this with abundance matching).

**Globular clusters** are small, dense systems of up to about a million old stars, often orbiting far from their galaxy's centre. Their stars move on random ("pressure-supported") orbits, and their velocity dispersions give dynamical masses close to their stellar masses: **no dark matter is needed**. Because they live inside galaxies, though, it is hard to separate what they were born with from what the galaxy's tides have stripped from them since; tides can remove material beyond a cluster's tidal radius.

<WorkedExample title="A globular cluster's mass">
<Problem>
A globular cluster has a velocity dispersion of 5 km/s within a radius of 3 pc and a luminosity of $3 \times 10^4\,L_\odot$. Estimate its mass with $M \approx 3\sigma^2 r/G$ and its mass-to-light ratio.
</Problem>
<Step title="The mass">
With $r = 0.003$ kpc: $M \approx 3 \times 5^2 \times 0.003 / 4.30 \times 10^{-6} \approx 5.2 \times 10^4\,M_\odot$.
</Step>
<Step title="Mass-to-light ratio">
$M/L \approx 5.2 \times 10^4 / 3 \times 10^4 \approx 1.7$, just what an old population of stars gives. No room, and no need, for dark matter.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

**Dwarf galaxies**, in general more massive than globular clusters (and comparable at the low-mass end), are the opposite: they are strongly dominated by dark matter. This contrast suggests that globular clusters formed and evolved under the strong tidal field of their host galaxy. Recent claims of dwarf galaxies without dark matter concern objects inside more massive structures, where dynamical effects can produce such discrepancies; the question is still open.

Closer still, planetary systems such as our own are described by Newtonian dynamics, and the only corrections needed are relativistic ones, near the Sun where gravity is strongest: the opposite of dark matter, which shows up where gravity is weakest, in the outskirts of galaxies.

<WorkedExample title="How much dark matter is in the Solar System?">
<Problem>
Near the Sun, the Milky Way's rotation curve is roughly flat at 220 km/s at a radius of 8 kpc. Treat the dark matter there as an isothermal sphere and estimate its local density, then the dark matter mass inside a sphere the size of Neptune's orbit (30.07 AU, or $1.46 \times 10^{-7}$ kpc).
</Problem>
<Step title="Density of an isothermal sphere">
$M(r) = r v^2/G$ gives $\rho = \dfrac{1}{4\pi r^2}\dfrac{dM}{dr} = \dfrac{v^2}{4\pi G r^2} = \dfrac{220^2}{4\pi \times 4.30 \times 10^{-6} \times 8^2} \approx 1.4 \times 10^7\,M_\odot$ per kpc³, or about $0.014\,M_\odot$ per pc³ (about 0.5 GeV/$c^2$ per cm³). This is an upper estimate: it pretends all of the 220 km/s comes from dark matter, while the Galaxy's stars supply much of it near the Sun.
</Step>
<Step title="Mass inside Neptune's orbit">
$M = \rho \times \tfrac43\pi r^3 \approx 1.4 \times 10^7 \times \tfrac43\pi \times (1.46 \times 10^{-7})^3 \approx 1.8 \times 10^{-13}\,M_\odot$, about $3.6 \times 10^{17}$ kg.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
That is the mass of a single rocky asteroid about 70 km across, about $10^{-13}$ of the Sun's mass. No planetary orbit could feel it, which is why the planets give the Sun's mass so precisely.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Dark matter = dynamical mass − (stars + gas). Dust and planets are negligible.
- Stellar masses come from red/near-infrared light times a stellar mass-to-light ratio $\Upsilon_s$ from population models; most stellar mass is in low-mass stars (for a Salpeter IMF, about 61% below 1 $M_\odot$ at birth).
- $\Upsilon_s$ depends on age, metallicity and IMF, with degeneracies and an uncertainty of about 0.2 dex, but never exceeds about 10 in the V band. Galaxy outskirts and clusters need tens to hundreds.
- Gas masses come from the 21 cm and CO lines, corrected for helium ($Y \approx 0.24$). Gas does not close the gap either.
- Globular clusters need no dark matter (their $M/L$ is that of old stars); dwarf galaxies are dominated by it. In the Solar System, dark matter at the local density adds only about $10^{-13}$ of the Sun's mass within Neptune's orbit.
