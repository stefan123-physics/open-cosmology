The previous page dealt with strong lensing: arcs and multiple images near the centre of a massive lens. Farther out, at separations well beyond the Einstein radius, background galaxies are not multiplied, only slightly **distorted**. This **weak lensing** is harder to measure but reaches the outer parts of galaxies and clusters, where dark matter dominates. The same physics, applied to stars in our own Galaxy, has also tested whether dark matter could be made of dark compact bodies.

## Weak lensing

Gravitational lensing depends on the angle between lens and source, measured in units of the Einstein radius $\theta_E$. Separations comparable to $\theta_E$ give strong lensing; larger ones only change the shape of the background galaxy. The difficulty is that we do not know the galaxy's shape before lensing. A single galaxy tells us almost nothing; only the **coherent** pattern of small distortions across many resolved background galaxies reveals the lens. The advantage is reach: weak lensing probes the distant regions of a structure, where dark matter dominates. The price is that systematic errors need much more care.

Recall the lensing matrix of the previous page: the convergence $\kappa$ changes only the **size** of an image, while the shear $\gamma$ changes its **shape**. A circular source becomes an ellipse with ellipticity $\epsilon = \gamma/(1-\kappa)$. In a random patch of sky, galaxy ellipticities average to zero, $\langle\epsilon\rangle = 0$. Behind a mass concentration, in the weak limit (dropping terms of order $\kappa^2$ and $\gamma^2$), the average becomes the shear: $\langle\epsilon\rangle \approx \gamma$.

<WorkedExample title="How many galaxies to see a shear?">
<Problem>
Background galaxies have intrinsic ellipticities scattered with a standard deviation of about 0.3. Around a galaxy cluster we expect a shear of 0.02. How many background galaxies must we average to detect it at three standard deviations?
</Problem>
<Step title="The noise of an average">
Averaging $N$ independent galaxies reduces the scatter of the mean ellipticity to $0.3/\sqrt N$.
</Step>
<Step title="Require a 3σ signal">
$0.02 \geq 3 \times 0.3/\sqrt N$ gives $\sqrt N \geq 45$, so $N \approx 2000$ galaxies.
</Step>
<Step title="What it means">
A deep image holds tens of galaxies per square arcminute, so a cluster's shear can be mapped in bins of a few square arcminutes. This is why weak lensing needs deep, wide images, and an exquisite correction for any distortion made by the telescope itself.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The standard way to turn a map of shear into a map of mass is the method of **Kaiser and Squires** (1993). Both $\gamma$ and $\kappa$ are second derivatives of the same lensing potential $\psi$, so in Fourier space ($\hat\kappa(\vec k)$ the transform of $\kappa(\vec\theta)$, and so on) they are related algebraically:

<Formula label="Kaiser–Squires inversion" tex="\hat\gamma_1 = \frac{k_1^2 - k_2^2}{k^2}\,\hat\kappa, \quad \hat\gamma_2 = \frac{2k_1k_2}{k^2}\,\hat\kappa \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat\kappa = \frac{(k_1^2 - k_2^2)\,\hat\gamma_1 + 2k_1k_2\,\hat\gamma_2}{k^2}" cite="mit" at="derived from Eq. (2.35), p. 45" />

So the measured shear field gives the convergence, the surface mass density. In practice this is demanding: background galaxies must be separated from foreground ones, the shape averages must be calibrated, and the telescope's own blurring, which varies across the image, must be corrected before any lensing signal can be trusted.

## The Bullet Cluster

The galaxy cluster 1E 0657−558 at redshift 0.296, the **Bullet Cluster**, is two clusters seen just after colliding. Its X-ray image shows the hot gas, which holds most of the ordinary matter; its weak-lensing map shows the total mass. The two are strikingly different. The gas clouds of the two clusters ran into each other and were slowed: one shows a **bow shock**, the sign of supersonic motion. The lensing mass, by contrast, passed through and sits with the galaxies, ahead of the gas on both sides.

If the extra gravity came from ordinary matter, the lensing map should peak on the gas. Instead it follows a component that, like the galaxies, does not feel the hydrodynamic forces of the collision: **collisionless dark matter**.

## Microlensing and MACHOs

In the 1990s lensing tested a specific idea for dark matter: that the halo of our Galaxy is made of dark compact bodies (dim stars, brown dwarfs, planets, stellar remnants) called **MACHOs** (Massive Astrophysical Compact Halo Objects). Such a body would act as a lens far too small to resolve its images. But as it moves across the line of sight to a background star (in the Magellanic Clouds, say), the star **brightens and fades** over days to months: **microlensing**.

Microlensing has clear signatures. It is **achromatic** (gravity bends all wavelengths equally, while intrinsic variability of a star usually changes its colour) and it is **one-off**, not periodic. For a point lens, adding the light of both images (Eq. 2.26) gives the total magnification

<Formula label="Microlensing magnification" tex="\mu(u) = \frac{u^2 + 2}{u\sqrt{u^2 + 4}}, \qquad u(t) = \sqrt{p^2 + (vt)^2}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.36), p. 47" />

where $u$ is the separation of lens and source in units of the Einstein radius, $p$ its value at closest approach (the impact parameter) and $v$ the relative speed, both also in Einstein-radius units. At $u = 1$ the magnification is $3/\sqrt5 \approx 1.34$, a brightening of 0.32 magnitudes; closer passes give much more.

The duration of an event is the time to cross the Einstein radius in the lens plane, $r_E = D_L\theta_E$, at a typical halo speed of about 200 km/s. Since $r_E \propto \sqrt M$, heavier lenses give longer events: for masses from $10^{-6}$ to a few solar masses, from hours to several months.

<WorkedExample title="How long does a microlensing event last?">
<Problem>
A compact object of 0.5 $M_\odot$ in the Galactic halo, 10 kpc away, passes in front of a star in the Large Magellanic Cloud (50 kpc away) at 200 km/s. Find its Einstein radius and the event's timescale. Repeat for a planet-sized body of $10^{-6}\,M_\odot$ and a 30 $M_\odot$ black hole.
</Problem>
<Step title="Einstein radius">
In length units, $r_E = \sqrt{\dfrac{4GM}{c^2}\,\dfrac{D_L D_{LS}}{D_S}}$, with $D_{LS} = 40$ kpc here (inside the Galaxy, distances simply subtract). For 0.5 $M_\odot$: $r_E \approx 8.5 \times 10^{11}$ m $\approx 5.7$ AU.
</Step>
<Step title="Timescale">
$t_E = r_E/v \approx 8.5 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m} / 2 \times 10^5\ \text{m/s} \approx 4.3 \times 10^6$ s, about 49 days.
</Step>
<Step title="Other masses">
$t_E \propto \sqrt M$: a $10^{-6}\,M_\odot$ body gives about 1.7 hours, and a 30 $M_\odot$ black hole about 380 days. A survey sampling nightly over a few years is blind to the heaviest lenses, which need longer campaigns.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The chance that a given star is being microlensed at any moment is the **optical depth**. For lenses spread with a constant mass density $\rho$ out to the source,

<Formula label="Microlensing optical depth" tex="\tau = \int_0^{D_S} n\,\pi r_E^2\,dD_L = \frac{4\pi G\rho}{c^2}\int_0^{D_S}\frac{D_L D_{LS}}{D_S}\,dD_L = \frac{2\pi G\rho}{3c^2}\,D_S^2" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.37), p. 48" />

which depends only on the total density, not on the mass of each lens. For a halo density of about $0.01\,M_\odot$ per pc³ out to the Large Magellanic Cloud, $\tau \approx 2.5 \times 10^{-6}$: only a few stars in a million are being lensed at any time. (A constant density all the way out overestimates it; realistic halo models give several times less.) Hence the need to monitor millions of stars, and to reject false alarms from variable stars and lensing by known stars and brown dwarfs.

The **MACHO collaboration** monitored about 12 million stars for six years and found 13 to 17 microlensing events. Their analysis (Alcock and collaborators, 2000) suggests that compact objects of 0.1–1 $M_\odot$ make up only a minor fraction of the halo, of order 10–30%, and **rules out a halo made entirely of them**. Microlensing points to a more diffuse form of dark matter.

A more exotic candidate is **primordial black holes**, formed in the very early universe (with no link to stars). Their only firm lower mass limit comes from Hawking radiation, which would have evaporated the smallest ones; Ferreras gives about $10^{11}$ kg. Heavier ones could be detected through microlensing, given long enough monitoring.

## Summary

- Weak lensing measures the small, coherent stretching of many background galaxies: $\langle\epsilon\rangle \approx \gamma$. The Kaiser–Squires inversion turns the shear map into a mass map. It reaches the outer, dark-matter dominated regions but needs careful control of systematics.
- In the Bullet Cluster the lensing mass is offset from the X-ray gas that holds most ordinary matter, as expected for collisionless dark matter.
- Microlensing by compact objects brightens a background star achromatically and once, by $\mu(u) = (u^2+2)/(u\sqrt{u^2+4})$; event durations scale as $\sqrt M$.
- The MACHO survey found too few events: compact objects of 0.1–1 $M_\odot$ cannot make up the Galactic halo. Primordial black holes remain a more exotic option.
