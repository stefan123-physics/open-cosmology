The halo profiles of the previous page come from simulations that contain **only dark matter**. Real galaxies also have baryons, and although they are a small part of the total mass (the flat outer rotation curves show that), they **dominate the centre**: gas sinks to the bottom of the potential and forms stars there. This page follows Ferreras through how baryons reshape a halo, and through one of the main open problems of cold dark matter: whether halo centres are **cusps** or **cores**.

## Adiabatic contraction

As baryons collect in the centre, their gravity pulls the dark matter in too, steepening its profile. A simple model by Blumenthal and collaborators (1986) assumes that this happens slowly, **adiabatically**, so that each particle's angular momentum is conserved. For circular orbits in a spherical system the conserved quantity is $r\,v(r) \propto \sqrt{r M(r)}$, so $r M(r)$ stays fixed for each shell. Shells are assumed to contract without crossing each other or rebounding. Comparing a shell's initial radius $r_i$ and final radius $r_f$:

<Formula label="Adiabatic contraction" tex="r_i\,M_i(r_i) = r_f\,M_f(r_f) = r_f\left[M_g(r_f) + M_\text{dm}(r_f)\right], \qquad M_\text{dm}(r_f) = (1 - f_d)\,M_i(r_i)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.7), p. 63" />

Here $M_g$ and $M_\text{dm}$ are the enclosed masses of the galaxy (baryons) and the dark matter, and $f_d$ is the small initial fraction of **dissipative** (baryonic) mass.

<WorkedExample title="How far does a shell contract?">
<Problem>
A halo starts as a singular isothermal sphere with mass $M_v$ inside $R = 200$ kpc, so $M_i(r) = M_v r/R$, with a baryon fraction $f_d = 0.05$ spread through it. The baryons then all settle into a compact galaxy at the centre. Where does a dark matter shell that started at $r_i = 10$ kpc end up? And one at 50 kpc?
</Problem>
<Step title="Set up the condition">
After contraction, the shell at $r_f$ encloses all the baryons, $f_d M_v$, plus its own dark matter, $(1 - f_d)M_v r_i/R$. Conservation of $rM$: $r_i \cdot M_v r_i/R = r_f\left[f_d M_v + (1 - f_d)M_v r_i/R\right]$.
</Step>
<Step title="Solve">
$r_f = \dfrac{r_i^2}{f_d R + (1 - f_d) r_i}$. For $r_i = 10$ kpc: $r_f = 100/(10 + 9.5) \approx 5.1$ kpc, so the shell shrinks to half its radius.
</Step>
<Step title="Farther out">
For $r_i = 50$ kpc: $r_f = 2500/(10 + 47.5) \approx 43$ kpc. The contraction is strong in the centre, where the baryons dominate, and mild far out.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The main effect is a **steeper rise of the rotation curve** in the centre, as seen in many galaxies; in this simple model the outer curve also declines slightly. Realistic models let the dissipative fraction vary with radius (restoring the flat outer curve), then simulate dark matter and gas together with full hydrodynamics, ideally in a cosmological setting (Chapter 4). The variety of rotation-curve shapes reflects the variety of formation histories. The opposite can happen too: if **feedback** injects energy into the centre and blows gas out early on, the halo **expands**, flattening its central slope: one way to make cores. (All this assumes the rotation curve traces the potential, which holds for disc galaxies; for elliptical galaxies, dominated by random motions, rotation and velocity dispersion must be modelled together.)

## The core–cusp problem

Cold, collisionless dark matter makes halos with **steep centres**: a logarithmic slope of $-2$ for an isothermal sphere, $-1$ for NFW. But observations of **dwarf galaxies** and **low surface brightness galaxies**, both dominated by dark matter, are better fit by profiles with a flat **core**, such as the **pseudo-isothermal sphere**,

<Formula label="Pseudo-isothermal sphere" tex="\rho(r) = \frac{\rho_0}{1 + (r/r_c)^2}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.8), p. 65" />

whose slope vanishes inside the core radius $r_c$ and approaches the isothermal $-2$ far out; or the **Burkert profile**,

<Formula label="Burkert profile" tex="\rho(r) = \frac{\rho_0\,r_c^3}{(r + r_c)(r^2 + r_c^2)}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.9), p. 65" />

which falls as $r^{-3}$ at large radii like NFW; or a **cored Einasto** profile (Eq. 3.3 with $x = (r + r_c)/r_S$).

The literature on this problem is vast. Proposed solutions fall into two families:

1. **Baryons**: feedback from star formation or an active galactic nucleus can move dark matter outwards and flatten the centre.
2. **Dark matter properties**: **self-interacting** dark matter, **warm** dark matter, or wave effects on kiloparsec scales if the particle is extremely light (**fuzzy dark matter**).

Systematic errors in observations or simulations may also play a part. Ferreras shows a compilation of simulations (Lazar and collaborators, 2020) in which the inner slope is measured within the central 1–2% of the virial radius, against the ratio of stellar mass to halo mass. Dark-matter-only runs are cuspy everywhere. With baryons, the slope flattens, even to a core, over a limited range, roughly $10^{-3} \lesssim M_\star/M_\text{halo} \lesssim 2 \times 10^{-2}$. With fewer stars, feedback is too weak to move the dark matter; with more, the effect becomes small again. At the low-mass end the simulated star formation and feedback are least trustworthy, so cored ultra-faint dwarfs could still be accommodated by improved recipes.

## Measuring the central slope

Halo profiles are inferred from the **kinematics of the stars**: their mean velocity and velocity dispersion, plus the surface-brightness profile that traces their distribution. From these one finds the total potential and subtracts the baryons. In the simplest case, a spherical system in equilibrium obeys the **Jeans equation**

<Formula label="Spherical Jeans equation" tex="\frac{1}{\rho_\star}\frac{d(\rho_\star\sigma_r^2)}{dr} + \frac{2\beta\sigma_r^2}{r} = -\frac{d\Phi}{dr}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.10), p. 67" />

with $\rho_\star$ the stellar density, $\sigma_r$ the radial velocity dispersion, $\Phi$ the total potential and $\beta = 1 - (\sigma_\perp/\sigma_r)^2$ the anisotropy, with $\sigma_\perp$ the dispersion in one tangential direction ($\beta = 0$ for isotropic orbits, $\beta = 1$ for purely radial ones). Galaxy centres tend to be isotropic and outskirts more radial, though real galaxies are more complicated. The Jeans equation gives the enclosed mass:

<Formula label="Mass from the Jeans equation" tex="M(r) = \frac{r\,\sigma_r^2}{G}\left(\gamma_\rho + \gamma_\sigma - 2\beta\right), \qquad \gamma_\rho = -\frac{d\ln\rho_\star}{d\ln r},\ \ \gamma_\sigma = -\frac{d\ln\sigma_r^2}{d\ln r}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.11), pp. 67–68" />

The first term comes from the light profile (with assumptions about the mass-to-light ratio and sphericity), the second from spatially resolved spectroscopy. The third cannot be observed directly: this is the **mass–anisotropy degeneracy**. Any assumed anisotropy model (such as Osipkov–Merritt) biases the slope, and with it the verdict on cores and cusps.

One way out is to look where anisotropy does not matter. Writing the total dispersion as

<Formula label="Total velocity dispersion" tex="\sigma_\text{tot}^2 = \sigma_r^2 + \sigma_\theta^2 + \sigma_\phi^2 = \sigma_r^2 + 2\sigma_\perp^2 = \sigma_r^2\,(3 - 2\beta)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.12), p. 68" />

and substituting gives

<Formula label="Mass in terms of the total dispersion" tex="M(r) = \frac{r}{G}\left[\sigma_\text{tot}^2 + \sigma_r^2\left(\gamma_\rho + \gamma_\sigma - 3\right)\right]" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.13), p. 68" />

[The book prints a minus sign before $\sigma_r^2$ here; with its definitions of $\gamma_\rho$ and $\gamma_\sigma$, Eqs. (3.11)–(3.12) give the plus sign. Both versions agree at $r_3$, where the bracket term vanishes.]

If the dispersion profile is nearly flat ($\gamma_\sigma \approx 0$), the anisotropy term vanishes at the radius $r_3$ where the stellar density slope is $-3$ ($\gamma_\rho = 3$):

<Formula label="Anisotropy-free mass estimate" tex="M(r_3) = \frac{r_3\,\sigma_\text{tot}^2}{G} \approx \frac{3\,r_3\,\langle\sigma_\text{LOS}^2\rangle}{G}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3.14), p. 68" />

replacing the total dispersion by three times the luminosity-weighted line-of-sight dispersion, which turns out to be insensitive to anisotropy (Wolf and collaborators, 2010). For most profiles $r_3$ is close to the three-dimensional half-mass radius. A second anisotropy-free point would give the slope itself: with tangential velocities (for example from the Gaia satellite) the same trick works at $r_2$, where the slope is $-2$ (Lazar and Bullock, 2020).

<WorkedExample title="Weighing a dwarf galaxy">
<Problem>
A dwarf spheroidal galaxy has a projected half-light radius of 0.25 kpc, a luminosity of $2 \times 10^5\,L_\odot$ and a line-of-sight velocity dispersion of 9 km/s. Take $r_3 \approx \tfrac43 \times 0.25 \approx 0.33$ kpc (Wolf and collaborators' relation between the two radii). Estimate the mass inside $r_3$ and the mass-to-light ratio there.
</Problem>
<Step title="The mass">
$M(r_3) = 3 r_3\langle\sigma_\text{LOS}^2\rangle/G = 3 \times 0.333 \times 9^2 / 4.30 \times 10^{-6} \approx 1.9 \times 10^7\,M_\odot$.
</Step>
<Step title="Mass-to-light ratio">
About half the light, $10^5\,L_\odot$, lies inside this radius, so $M/L \approx 1.9 \times 10^7 / 10^5 \approx 190$, far above the 1–3 of old stars. Dwarf spheroidals are among the most dark-matter dominated objects known, and so the best places to test for cores.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Baryons dominate galaxy centres. Slow infall pulls dark matter inwards (adiabatic contraction, conserving $rM(r)$: Eq. 3.7) and steepens the inner rotation curve; feedback that expels gas can do the opposite and make a core.
- Cold dark matter predicts cusps (slope −1 for NFW), but dwarf and low-surface-brightness galaxies often fit cored profiles (pseudo-isothermal, Burkert, cored Einasto): the core–cusp problem.
- Proposed solutions: baryonic feedback, which in simulations works for $10^{-3} \lesssim M_\star/M_\text{halo} \lesssim 2 \times 10^{-2}$; or self-interacting, warm or fuzzy dark matter.
- Measuring slopes uses the Jeans equation, which suffers from the mass–anisotropy degeneracy. At $r_3$ (stellar slope −3) the mass is anisotropy-free: $M(r_3) \approx 3r_3\langle\sigma_\text{LOS}^2\rangle/G$.
