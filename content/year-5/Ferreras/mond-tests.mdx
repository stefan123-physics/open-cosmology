Fitting rotation curves is not enough to prove MOND: other phenomenological recipes can fit them too. Ferreras's Sections 7.3–7.5 look at what makes MOND compelling, where it struggles, and how it might be turned into a full theory.

## The radial acceleration relation

The key is to frame the evidence in terms of **local accelerations**. Lelli and collaborators (2017) compared, at 2693 points in 240 galaxies (from the SPARC database), the acceleration observed from the kinematics, $g_\text{OBS}$, with the acceleration expected from the stars and gas alone, $g_\text{BAR}$. With no dark matter and Newtonian gravity, all points would lie on the line $g_\text{OBS} = g_\text{BAR}$. They do at high accelerations; below about $a_0$ they rise above it, all along **one tight curve**.

This is the rotation-curve result again, but it makes the link to acceleration explicit. In the dark matter picture it requires the dark matter at each place to be tuned to the baryons there, a contrived correlation. For MOND it is the basic prediction. Ferreras calls it arguably the best argument for this kind of solution.

The **Tully–Fisher relation** makes the same point statistically. Disc galaxies follow

<Formula label="Tully–Fisher relation" tex="L_X \propto v_\text{max}^{\alpha_X}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (7.9), p. 166" />

with a slope $\alpha_X$ between about 3 and 4 depending on the passband $X$, tightest in the near-infrared. Adding gas (from the 21 cm line) to stars gives the even tighter **baryonic Tully–Fisher relation**, extending to low-mass galaxies. It is natural in MOND ($v^4 = GM_\text{bar}a_0$), another face of the acceleration relation, and a demand for a close link between dark and baryonic mass in the standard picture.

## Lensing in MOND

Galaxy-scale evidence for dark matter comes from two sources: rotation curves and **strong lensing**. To compute light bending, MOND needs a full theory playing the role of general relativity, and there is no agreed one. A simple approach (Mortlock and Turner, 2001) mimics what works for standard gravity (a Newtonian-style deflection integral, doubled to match relativity), but with MOND accelerations inside the integral (Eq. 7.10, the counterpart of Eq. 2.14).

The result for a point mass: at high accelerations (near the lens) the deflection is the standard one; at low accelerations (far away) it does not fall to zero but tends to a **constant**. For $10^{11}\,M_\odot$ at a distance of about $10^3$ Mpc (redshift about 0.3), Ferreras finds a limit of about 0.3 arcseconds, easily measurable with the Hubble or James Webb telescopes. Either this is wrong, or a complete theory must make the deflection vanish far from the lens; it remains an open problem. (A fully MONDian cosmology would also need its own way of measuring distances.)

There is a second difficulty. The acceleration at angular distance $\theta$ from such a lens is

<Formula label="Acceleration near a lens" tex="a = \frac{GM}{\xi^2} = \frac{GM}{\theta^2 D_L^2} \approx 6\,a_0\left(\frac{M}{10^{11}\,M_\odot}\right)\left(\frac{\theta}{1\ \text{arcsec}}\right)^{-2}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (7.11), p. 169" />

(for $D_L = 10^3$ Mpc and $a_0 = 10^{-10}$ m/s²). Strong lenses have image separations of order an arcsecond, so they probe accelerations of several $a_0$, close to the **Newtonian** regime. MOND and standard lensing should then give similar masses, and those masses still **exceed the stellar masses**. Ferreras's own analysis of the lens HE 1104−1805 (lens galaxy at redshift 0.73 with stellar mass $\log(M_\star/M_\odot) \lesssim 11.5$; Ferreras, Sakellariadou and Yusaf, 2008) finds a lensing mass well above the stellar mass in both treatments. These galaxies have little gas, so even MOND would need dark matter here.

<WorkedExample title="How far from Newton is a strong lens?">
<Problem>
A lens galaxy of $10^{11}\,M_\odot$ is at $D_L = 1000$ Mpc. Find the acceleration at an angular distance of 1 arcsecond from its centre, in units of $a_0 = 10^{-10}$ m/s².
</Problem>
<Step title="Physical distance">
$\xi = \theta D_L = (1/206\,265) \times 1000$ Mpc $\approx 4.85$ kpc $\approx 1.50 \times 10^{20}$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Acceleration">
$a = GM/\xi^2 = 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 1.99 \times 10^{41}/(1.50 \times 10^{20})^2 \approx 5.9 \times 10^{-10}$ m/s², about $6\,a_0$.
</Step>
<Step title="Meaning">
This is the Newtonian acceleration $a_N$. With $f(x) = x/(1+x)$ of the true acceleration, $a^2/(a + a_0) = a_N = 6a_0$ gives $a = \tfrac12\left(6 + \sqrt{60}\right)a_0 \approx 6.87\,a_0$: gravity is only about 15% stronger than Newton's. Not nearly enough to close a gap of a factor of several between lensing and stellar masses.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

**Weak lensing** of clusters is harder still. In the **Bullet Cluster**, the problem is not only the amount of gravitating mass but its **shape**: the hot gas seen in X-rays holds most of the baryons, yet the lensing mass sits elsewhere, with the galaxies. Gravity sourced by baryons should follow the gas.

## Towards a full theory

MOND can look like sleight of hand. But science advances by proposing theories and testing them, and our laws of gravity have been confirmed robustly only at accelerations well above $a_0$. To give MOND a sounder footing, Bekenstein and Milgrom (1984) proposed **AQUAL** (for "a-quadratic Lagrangian": a Lagrangian that is not simply quadratic in the field gradient, as Newtonian gravity's is):

<Formula label="AQUAL Lagrangian" tex="\mathcal{L} = -\int\left[\rho\,\Phi + \frac{a_0^2}{8\pi G}\,F\!\left(\frac{|\nabla\Phi|^2}{a_0^2}\right)\right]d^3x" cite="mit" at="Eq. (7.12), p. 171" />

[In the printed Eq. (7.12) the source term after the "+" is blank; the standard Bekenstein–Milgrom form, with the matter term $\rho\Phi$, is shown here.]

where $F$ is a positive function with $F(X^2) = X^2$ in the Newtonian limit, whose derivative is the MOND function $f$. It is non-relativistic but respects Galilean invariance and conserves energy, momentum and angular momentum.

A theory that works in cosmology and for lensing must be **covariant** (relativistic), reducing to MOND on galaxy scales as general relativity reduces to Newton. Bekenstein's **TeVeS** (2004) does this with a tensor, a vector and a scalar field. The metric that matter feels is built from the Einstein metric $g_{\mu\nu}$, the scalar $\phi$ and the vector $U_\mu$:

<Formula label="TeVeS physical metric" tex="\tilde g_{\mu\nu} = e^{-2\phi}g_{\mu\nu} - U_\mu U_\nu\left(e^{2\phi} - e^{-2\phi}\right)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (7.13), p. 171" />

For lensing, the weak-field line element has the same form as in general relativity,

<Formula label="Weak-field metric" tex="ds^2 = -(1 + 2\Phi)\,dt^2 + (1 - 2\Phi)\left(dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2\right)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (7.14), p. 171" />

but with $\Phi = \Phi_N + \phi$: the scalar field adds to the Newtonian potential of the baryons and can supply the extra light bending usually blamed on dark matter. Since $\phi$ does not obey a Poisson equation of its own, TeVeS is genuinely different from dark matter. Still, it lacks a complete description of strong and weak lensing, and of **dark energy**, the next chapter's "elephant in the room".

## Summary

- The radial acceleration relation (2693 points, 240 galaxies) ties the observed acceleration tightly to the baryons' acceleration, departing from Newton below $a_0$: MOND's strongest argument. The baryonic Tully–Fisher relation (slope about 4) is the same fact seen statistically.
- Lensing needs a full relativistic theory. A simple MOND estimate gives a deflection that tends to a constant (about 0.3″ in Ferreras's estimate for $10^{11}\,M_\odot$); typical strong lenses sit at about $6a_0$, near-Newtonian, and their masses still exceed the stellar masses.
- The Bullet Cluster's lensing mass is offset from its gas: hard for any theory sourced by baryons alone.
- AQUAL (1984) gives MOND a Lagrangian; TeVeS (2004) is a relativistic tensor–vector–scalar version, still incomplete for lensing and dark energy.
