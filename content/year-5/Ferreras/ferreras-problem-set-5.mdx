## Exercise 6.1

<Exercise id="e1" title="How old was the universe at 1 MeV?" points={12} mit="Exercise 6.1">

Take $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc and $\Omega_{\gamma,0}h_0^2 = 2.5 \times 10^{-5}$ for the photons of the cosmic microwave background, whose temperature today is $T_{\gamma,0} = 2.73$ K (book, p. 142). Find the age of the universe, in the radiation-dominated era, when the temperature was 1 MeV (book, Exercise 6.1, p. 142).

<Part label="A" points={5}>

Our reading of the book's exercise: the radiation is the photons alone, with the density parameter given, and the temperature falls as $1/a$ all the way from 1 MeV to today. What is the age at $kT = 1$ MeV, in seconds (tolerance 2%)?

<Hint>

In a flat universe dominated by radiation, $H^2 = H_0^2\,\Omega_{\gamma,0}\,a^{-4}$ and $a \propto t^{1/2}$. What is $t$ in terms of $H$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

With $T \propto 1/a$, the scale factor at 1 MeV is $kT_{\gamma,0}/(1\ \text{MeV})$. Notice that $H_0^2\,\Omega_{\gamma,0}$ is $(100\ \text{km/s/Mpc})^2$ times the number given.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e1-t" label="Age at 1 MeV, in s:" value="1.71" tolerance="2%" calc="e1-t" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the radiation era $a \propto t^{1/2}$, so $H = 1/2t$; the Friedmann equation gives $H$ at a given scale factor, and the temperature gives the scale factor.

<Step title="Age and expansion rate">

With $H = H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{\gamma,0}}\,a^{-2}$ and $t = 1/2H$:

<Formula label="Age in a photon-dominated universe" tex="t = \frac{a^2}{2H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{\gamma,0}}}, \qquad a = \frac{T_{\gamma,0}}{T}" cite="mit" at="Exercise 6.1, p. 142 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

$kT_{\gamma,0} = k_B \times 2.73$ K $= 2.353 \times 10^{-4}$ eV, so at 1 MeV the scale factor was $a = 2.353 \times 10^{-10}$. The given $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc drops out: $H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{\gamma,0}} = (100\ \text{km/s/Mpc}) \times \sqrt{2.5 \times 10^{-5}} = 3.2408 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{s}^{-1} \times 0.005 = 1.620 \times 10^{-20}$ s⁻¹. Then $t = (2.353 \times 10^{-10})^2/(2 \times 1.620 \times 10^{-20}\ \text{s}^{-1}) = 1.71$ s.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Using $h_0 = 0.7$ separately instead: $\Omega_{\gamma,0} = 2.5 \times 10^{-5}/0.49 = 5.10 \times 10^{-5}$ and $H_0 = 2.2685 \times 10^{-18}$ s⁻¹ give the same $H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{\gamma,0}} = 1.620 \times 10^{-20}$ s⁻¹. About a second, at the temperature at which, as the lesson says, the neutrinos decouple.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="B" points={3}>

(Our addition.) The three neutrino species are radiation too. Today their temperature is $(4/11)^{1/3}$ times the photons' (lesson [What could dark matter be made of?](/courses/fundamentals-of-dark-matter/dark-matter-particles)), and each species, a neutrino and an antineutrino with one spin state each, has $\frac78(4/11)^{4/3}$ of the photons' energy density. Keeping $a = T_{\gamma,0}/T$ as in part A, what is the age at 1 MeV now, in seconds (tolerance 2%)?

<Hint>

The radiation density is $\Omega_{\gamma,0}$ times $1 + 3 \times \frac78 \times (4/11)^{4/3}$. How does $t$ scale with $\Omega$ at fixed $a$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="e1-tnu" label="Age at 1 MeV with neutrinos, in s:" value="1.32" tolerance="2%" calc="e1-tnu" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** More radiation, faster expansion, a younger universe at the same scale factor: $t \propto \Omega^{-1/2}$.

<Step title="The factor">

$1 + 3 \times \frac78 \times (4/11)^{4/3} = 1.681$, so $t = 1.71\ \text{s}/\sqrt{1.681} = 1.32$ s.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$\sqrt{1.681} = 1.297$, and $1.708/1.297 = 1.317$: about 23% younger.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="C" points={4}>

(Our addition.) Parts A and B both scale today's radiation back with $T \propto 1/a$. But at 1 MeV the electrons and positrons were still relativistic and abundant, and the neutrinos had the photons' temperature. Use instead the book's result for the radiation era, $H = 1/2t \approx 1.66\,g_*^{1/2}\,T^2/m_\text{Pl}$ (Eq. 5.23, p. 117), with $g_*$ counting photons, electrons and positrons, and three species of neutrinos and antineutrinos, all at the same temperature (Eq. 5.18, p. 115). What is the age at $kT = 1$ MeV, in seconds (tolerance 2%)? Why does it differ from part B?

<Hint>

Bosons count with their internal states $g$, fermions with $\frac78 g$. Photons have 2 states; electrons and positrons together 4; the neutrinos and antineutrinos of three species together 6.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In natural units $t = m_\text{Pl}/(2 \times 1.66\,g_*^{1/2}\,T^2)$ is in GeV⁻¹ when $m_\text{Pl}$ and $T$ are in GeV; multiply by $\hbar$ in GeV s.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e1-tg" label="Age at 1 MeV with $g_*$, in s:" value="0.738" tolerance="2%" calc="e1-tg" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At a given temperature the age depends only on how many relativistic species share the energy density.

<Step title="Counting">

$g_* = 2 + \frac78(4 + 6) = 10.75$, so $\sqrt{g_*} = 3.279$.

</Step>

<Step title="The age">

<Formula label="Age in the radiation era" tex="t = \frac{m_\text{Pl}}{3.32\,g_*^{1/2}\,T^2}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (5.23), p. 117 (solved for t)" />

With $T = 10^{-3}$ GeV: $t = 1.22 \times 10^{19}/(3.32 \times 3.279 \times 10^{-6})$ GeV⁻¹ $= 1.121 \times 10^{24}$ GeV⁻¹, and times $\hbar = 6.582 \times 10^{-25}$ GeV s, $t = 0.738$ s.

</Step>

<Step title="Why it differs from part B">

Part B is the same formula with $g_* = 2 + \frac78 \times 6 \times (4/11)^{4/3} = 3.363$, the value today, frozen. At 1 MeV the electron–positron pairs had not yet annihilated: they add $\frac78 \times 4$ to $g_*$, and the neutrinos, not yet colder than the photons, count fully. The extra energy density makes the expansion faster and the universe younger at that temperature. Check: $\sqrt{10.75/3.363} = 1.788$, and $1.317/0.738 = 1.785$; they agree to 0.2%, the rounding of $\Omega_{\gamma,0}h_0^2$ and $m_\text{Pl}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Exercise 6.2

<Exercise id="e2" title="When does a 100 GeV WIMP freeze out?" points={14} mit="Exercise 6.2">

A dark matter particle of mass $m_\chi = 100$ GeV annihilates with the averaged cross-section $\langle\sigma v\rangle \sim \alpha^2/m_\chi^2$. It decouples in the radiation-dominated era, while non-relativistic. Find its freeze-out temperature (a numerical solution is needed) and the cosmic time of freeze-out (book, Exercise 6.2, p. 145).

Our choices where the book leaves numbers open: the coupling $\alpha = 0.01$ (the value in the book's Eq. 6.10, p. 144), $\langle\sigma v\rangle = \alpha^2/m_\chi^2$ exactly, $g = 2$ internal states, and $g_* = 100$ relativistic degrees of freedom at freeze-out ("of order 100" at early times, book p. 116). The freeze-out condition is that the annihilation rate equals the expansion rate (Eq. 5.35, p. 122), with the non-relativistic equilibrium density and the radiation-era expansion rate:

<Formula label="Freeze-out condition" tex="n_\text{eq}(T_f)\,\langle\sigma v\rangle = H(T_f)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (5.35), p. 122" />

<Formula label="Non-relativistic number density" tex="n_\text{eq} = g\left(\frac{mT}{2\pi}\right)^{3/2}e^{-m/T}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (5.24), p. 117" />

<Formula label="Expansion rate in the radiation era" tex="H = \frac{1}{2t} \approx 1.66\,g_*^{1/2}\,\frac{T^2}{m_\text{Pl}}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (5.23), p. 117" />

<Part label="A" points={6}>

Write the condition in terms of $x_f = m_\chi/T_f$ and solve it. What is $x_f$ (tolerance 1%)?

<Hint>

Put $T = m_\chi/x$ everywhere. Both sides then carry powers of $m_\chi$ and of $x$; collect all the numbers on one side, so that only $\sqrt x\,e^{-x}$ is left on the other.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In natural units $\langle\sigma v\rangle = \alpha^2/m_\chi^2$ is in GeV⁻². The equation $\sqrt x\,e^{-x} = K$ with a tiny $K$ has its root where $x \approx \ln(1/K)$ plus a small correction; take logarithms and iterate $x = \ln(\sqrt x/K)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e2-xf" label="$x_f$:" value="26.9" tolerance="1%" calc="e2-xf" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The equilibrium density falls exponentially once $T < m_\chi$, the expansion rate only as $T^2$: they cross at a temperature well below the mass, and the crossing is fixed by a logarithm.

<Step title="The equation for x">

With $T = m_\chi/x$, the left side is $g\,(m_\chi^2/2\pi x)^{3/2}e^{-x}\,\alpha^2/m_\chi^2 = g\,\alpha^2 m_\chi\,(2\pi x)^{-3/2}e^{-x}$, and the right side is $1.66\,g_*^{1/2}m_\chi^2/(x^2 m_\text{Pl})$. So

<Formula label="Freeze-out equation for x_f" tex="\sqrt{x_f}\,e^{-x_f} = K, \qquad K = \frac{1.66\,g_*^{1/2}\,(2\pi)^{3/2}\,m_\chi}{g\,\alpha^2\,m_\text{Pl}}" cite="mit" at="Exercise 6.2, p. 145 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

$K = 1.66 \times 10 \times 15.75 \times 100/(2 \times 10^{-4} \times 1.22 \times 10^{19}) = 1.071 \times 10^{-11}$, so $\ln(1/K) = 25.26$. Iterating $x = 25.26 + \frac12\ln x$: $x = 26.87$, then $26.91$, and the root is $x_f = 26.9$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$\sqrt{26.906}\,e^{-26.906} = 5.187 \times 2.065 \times 10^{-12} = 1.071 \times 10^{-11}$. The answer is not sensitive to our choices: with $g_* = 80$ instead of 100, $x_f = 27.0$; with $g = 1$ or 4, $x_f = 26.2$ or 27.6.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="B" points={4}>

What is the freeze-out temperature $T_f$, in GeV (tolerance 2%)?

<Answer id="e2-tf" label="$T_f$ in GeV:" value="3.72" tolerance="2%" calc="e2-tf" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $T_f = m_\chi/x_f$.

<Step title="The temperature">

$T_f = 100/26.906$ GeV $= 3.72$ GeV, about $4 \times 10^{13}$ K: the WIMP is cold at freeze-out, with thermal energy a few per cent of its rest energy.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

With these values $\langle\sigma v\rangle = 10^{-8}$ GeV⁻², which is $1.17 \times 10^{-25}$ cm³ s⁻¹ (multiply by $(\hbar c)^2c$). The book's Eq. 6.10 then gives $\Omega h^2 \sim 10^{-26}/1.17 \times 10^{-25} = 0.085$: close to the observed 0.12 (book, p. 142), the "WIMP miracle" of the lesson.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="C" points={4}>

What was the cosmic time at freeze-out, in units of $10^{-8}$ s (tolerance 2%)?

<Hint>

Use the radiation-era relation $t = 1/2H$ at $T_f$, and convert from GeV⁻¹ to seconds with $\hbar$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e2-t" label="$t_f$ in units of $10^{-8}$ s:" value="1.75" tolerance="2%" calc="e2-t" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The radiation era ties time to temperature, $t \propto T^{-2}$.

<Step title="The time">

$H(T_f) = 1.66 \times 10 \times 3.717^2/1.22 \times 10^{19}$ GeV $= 1.880 \times 10^{-17}$ GeV, so $t_f = \hbar/2H = 6.582 \times 10^{-25}/(3.760 \times 10^{-17})$ s $= 1.75 \times 10^{-8}$ s.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Scale from Exercise 6.1(C): there $t = 0.738$ s at $10^{-3}$ GeV with $g_* = 10.75$. Here $t = 0.738\ \text{s} \times (10^{-3}/3.717)^2 \times (10.75/100)^{1/2} = 1.75 \times 10^{-8}$ s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Exercise 6.3

<Exercise id="e3" title="The local dark matter density, and how often the particles annihilate" points={16} mit="Exercise 6.3">

Use spherical collapse to estimate the mean density of dark matter in the Milky Way's halo, and compare it with the figure usually adopted near the Sun, $\rho_0 = 0.3$ GeV cm⁻³. Justify the result with a reasonable collapse redshift and scale radius for the halo. Then, if the particles annihilate with the cross-section $\sigma = 10^{-44}$ cm² and have mass $m_\chi = 100$ GeV, find the rate of events (book, Exercise 6.3, p. 148).

Our choices: the background of $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc and $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.3$ (as in the book's earlier exercises), and the spherical collapse result that a virialised halo has a mean density $18\pi^2$ times the background matter density at the redshift $z_c$ when it collapsed ([spherical collapse](/courses/8-902/spherical-collapse)).

<Part label="A" points={5}>

What is the mean density inside a halo that collapsed at $z_c = 1$ (our choice), in GeV cm⁻³ (tolerance 2%)?

<Hint>

The critical density today is $3H_0^2/8\pi G$; convert kg m⁻³ to GeV cm⁻³ with 1 GeV/c² $= 1.7827 \times 10^{-27}$ kg.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e3-rho1" label="Mean density in GeV cm⁻³:" value="0.00220" tolerance="2%" calc="e3-rho1" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A collapsed halo is a fixed factor denser than the universe was when it collapsed; the background then was $(1 + z_c)^3$ times denser than today.

<Step title="The background">

$\rho_{c,0} = 3H_0^2/8\pi G = 9.204 \times 10^{-27}$ kg m⁻³ $= 5.163 \times 10^{-6}$ GeV cm⁻³, and the matter density today is $0.3$ times that, $1.549 \times 10^{-6}$ GeV cm⁻³.

</Step>

<Step title="The halo">

<Formula label="Mean density of a virialised halo" tex="\bar\rho = 18\pi^2\,\Omega_{m,0}\,\rho_{c,0}\,(1 + z_c)^3" cite="mit" at="Exercise 6.3, p. 148 (our own solution)" />

$18\pi^2 = 177.7$, so for $z_c = 1$: $\bar\rho = 177.7 \times 1.549 \times 10^{-6} \times 8 = 2.20 \times 10^{-3}$ GeV cm⁻³.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

For $z_c = 0$ the same formula gives $2.75 \times 10^{-4}$ GeV cm⁻³, eight times less. Either way the mean density of the whole halo falls far short of 0.3 GeV cm⁻³.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="B" points={3}>

At what collapse redshift would the mean density of the whole halo equal 0.3 GeV cm⁻³ (tolerance 2%)? Is that a reasonable collapse redshift for the Milky Way's halo?

<Answer id="e3-zc" label="$z_c$:" value="9.29" tolerance="2%" calc="e3-zc" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Invert part A: $(1 + z_c)^3 = 0.3/(2.75 \times 10^{-4})$.

<Step title="The redshift">

$1 + z_c = (0.3/2.752 \times 10^{-4})^{1/3} = 10.29$, so $z_c = 9.29$.

</Step>

<Step title="Is it reasonable?">

Hardly. Cold dark matter grows hierarchically: small halos collapse first and massive ones later, by merging (lesson [Dark matter halos](/courses/fundamentals-of-dark-matter/dark-matter-halos)), so a Milky Way halo already in place at redshift 9 does not fit that picture. Besides, the mean density of the whole halo is not the right quantity: the Sun lies deep inside the halo, where the density is far above the mean. That is what the scale radius is for (part C).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="C" points={4}>

Model the halo as an NFW profile, $\rho = \rho_S/[x(1 + x)^2]$ with $x = r/r_S$, truncated at the radius $r_h$ inside which its mean density is the value of part A. Take the mass inside $r_h$ to be $10^{12}\,M_\odot$, collapse at $z_c = 1$ and the concentration $c = r_h/r_S = 10$ (our choices). What is the density at $r = 8$ kpc, near the Sun's distance from the Galactic centre, in GeV cm⁻³ (tolerance 3%)?

<Hint>

First $r_h$ from $M = \frac43\pi r_h^3\bar\rho$, then $r_S = r_h/c$. The mass of the profile inside $r_h$ is $4\pi\rho_Sr_S^3\left[\ln(1 + c) - c/(1 + c)\right]$, which fixes $\rho_S$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="e3-rho8" label="Density at 8 kpc, GeV cm⁻³:" value="0.439" tolerance="3%" calc="e3-rho8" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The NFW profile concentrates mass towards the centre; at 8 kpc the density is some 200 times the mean of the whole halo.

<Step title="Radius and scale radius">

$\bar\rho = 2.20 \times 10^{-3}$ GeV cm⁻³ and $M = 10^{12}\,M_\odot$ give $r_h = 160.3$ kpc, so $r_S = 16.03$ kpc and $x = 8/16.03 = 0.499$ at the Sun.

</Step>

<Step title="The normalisation">

$\ln 11 - 10/11 = 1.489$. Mean density and $\rho_S$ are related by $\bar\rho = 3\rho_S\left[\ln(1 + c) - c/(1 + c)\right]/c^3$, so $\rho_S = 2.201 \times 10^{-3} \times 1000/(3 \times 1.489) = 0.493$ GeV cm⁻³.

</Step>

<Step title="The density at 8 kpc">

$\rho = 0.493/[0.499 \times 1.499^2]$ GeV cm⁻³ $= 0.439$ GeV cm⁻³, within 50% of the adopted 0.3 GeV cm⁻³. Our numbers are rough: with $c = 8$ the result is 0.367 GeV cm⁻³, with $c = 12$ it is 0.504 GeV cm⁻³. Spherical collapse plus a cuspy profile with a scale radius of 13 to 20 kpc ($c = 12$ to 8) does explain the local value.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="D" points={4}>

Now the rate of events. Use the book's annihilation rate per unit volume as printed, $\Gamma_a \sim n_\chi^2\,\sigma v$ (Eq. 6.18, p. 154, with no factor ½), the local density $\rho_0 = 0.3$ GeV cm⁻³, the book's $\sigma$ and $m_\chi$, and a relative speed $v = 230$ km/s (our choice). How many annihilations happen per cubic centimetre per second, in units of $10^{-42}$ cm⁻³ s⁻¹ (tolerance 2%)?

<Answer id="e3-gamma" label="$\Gamma_a$ in units of $10^{-42}$ cm⁻³ s⁻¹:" value="2.07" tolerance="2%" calc="e3-gamma"
  alsoAccept={[{ value: "1.035", tolerance: "2%", calc: "e3-gamma-also", note: "That is the rate with the factor ½ for identical particles, which the book's Eq. 6.18 leaves out; both readings are accepted." }]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two particles must meet: the rate per volume goes as the density squared.

<Step title="The rate">

$n_\chi = \rho_0/m_\chi = 0.003$ cm⁻³, $\sigma v = 10^{-44} \times 2.3 \times 10^7 = 2.3 \times 10^{-37}$ cm³ s⁻¹, so $\Gamma_a = (0.003)^2 \times 2.3 \times 10^{-37} = 2.07 \times 10^{-42}$ cm⁻³ s⁻¹. (Counting each pair of identical particles once adds a factor ½, $1.04 \times 10^{-42}$.)

</Step>

<Step title="What it means">

One particle annihilates at the rate $n_\chi\sigma v = 6.9 \times 10^{-40}$ s⁻¹: in 13.8 billion years, a fraction $3.0 \times 10^{-22}$ of the particles. A cross-section of $10^{-44}$ cm² is far below the thermal-relic benchmark of Eq. 6.10, $\langle\sigma v\rangle \sim 10^{-26}$ cm³ s⁻¹, which at 230 km/s would need $\sigma = 4.3 \times 10^{-34}$ cm². With the relic value the rate would be about $4 \times 10^{10}$ times higher, still tiny per particle; the signal searched for comes from the integral of $n_\chi^2$ over a whole halo.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Exercise 6.4

<Exercise id="e4" title="How hard does a WIMP kick a nucleus?" points={12} mit="Exercise 6.4">

A dark matter particle $\chi$ of mass $m_\chi$ moves slowly, at speed $v$, and scatters elastically off a nucleus $N$ of mass $m_N$ at rest, $\chi + N \to \chi + N$. Treat the collision non-relativistically and show that the recoil energy of the nucleus is

**The idea.** An elastic collision in the centre-of-mass frame only rotates the velocities; the change in the nucleus's velocity is the same in every frame.

<Step title="Centre-of-mass frame">

</Step>

</Step>

<Formula label="Evolution of a w-fluid" tex="\frac{d\ln\rho}{d\ln a} = -3(1 + w)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (8.9), p. 180" />

and discuss dark matter, dark energy and a photon gas (book, Exercise 8.1, p. 180). [The book prints the left side as $d\ln\rho/da$; the derivative with respect to $\ln a$ is the one that equals $-3(1 + w)$.]

<Part label="A" points={3}>

Show it, and find how $\rho$ depends on $a$ for constant $w$. Mark yourself against the solution.

<Hint>

The first law of thermodynamics for a comoving volume $V \propto a^3$ with no heat flow: $d(\rho V) = -p\,dV$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** As a comoving volume expands, its energy changes by the work its pressure does.

<Step title="The derivation">

$d(\rho a^3) = -p\,d(a^3)$ gives $a^3d\rho + 3\rho a^2da = -3w\rho a^2da$, so $d\rho/\rho = -3(1 + w)\,da/a$, that is $d\ln\rho/d\ln a = -3(1 + w)$. For constant $w$, $\rho \propto a^{-3(1 + w)}$. (The same follows from the fluid equation $\dot\rho + 3H(\rho + p) = 0$, which comes from the two Friedmann equations.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Matter, $w = 0$: $a^{-3}$, the volume dilution alone. The 8.962 lesson [Cosmology from symmetry](/courses/8-962/frw-cosmology-from-symmetry) gets the same law from energy conservation in general relativity.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="B" points={2}>

In a universe of matter and radiation, how does the ratio of the radiation density to the matter density change with the scale factor?

<Answer id="e8-ratio" label="The ratio of radiation to matter density:" choices={["Grows as a, so radiation takes over at late times", "Falls as 1/a, so radiation dominated at early times", "Stays constant, since both dilute with the volume", "Falls as 1/a², one factor for volume, one for redshift"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Compare the exponents for $w = 1/3$ and $w = 0$.

<Step title="The cases">

Dark matter, pressureless ($w = 0$): $\rho \propto a^{-3}$. Photons ($w = 1/3$): $a^{-4}$, the extra factor $1/a$ being the redshift of each photon's energy. So radiation over matter goes as $a^{-1}$: radiation dominated early, matter later. Dark energy as a cosmological constant ($w = -1$): the exponent is 0, and the density stays the same however much space expands, so it wins at late times. For $w < -1$ (the "Big Rip" of the book, p. 179) the density even grows.

</Step>

<Step title="A remark on w = 1">

The book lists $w = 1$ as "an ideal gas, at constant temperature" (p. 179). Our remark: an ideal gas at constant temperature does have $p \propto \rho$, but with $w = kT/mc^2$, tiny for any gas of non-relativistic particles (it behaves like matter). $w = 1$, pressure equal to energy density, is the stiffest fluid; a scalar field with only kinetic energy and no potential has it (its density and pressure in the lesson on quintessence are then equal), and its density falls as $a^{-6}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="C" points={2}>

(Our addition.) A dark energy with constant $w = -0.8$ (our choice): how many times denser was it at redshift $z = 2$ than today (tolerance 1%)?

<Answer id="e8-z2" label="$\rho(z = 2)/\rho_0$:" value="1.933" tolerance="1%" calc="e8-z2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $a = 1/(1 + z)$, so $\rho/\rho_0 = (1 + z)^{3(1 + w)}$.

<Step title="The number">

$3^{3 \times 0.2} = 3^{0.6} = 1.933$: almost twice as dense, against $3^3 = 27$ times for matter.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Hint>

The ratio of the dark energy density to the matter density goes as $a^{-3w}$, and is $(1 - \Omega_{m,0})/\Omega_{m,0}$ today.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Set $\rho_w/\rho_m = \frac{1 - \Omega_{m,0}}{\Omega_{m,0}}(1 + z)^{3w} = 1$.

<Step title="The redshift">

<Formula label="Redshift of matter–dark energy equality" tex="1 + z_\text{eq} = \left(\frac{1 - \Omega_{m,0}}{\Omega_{m,0}}\right)^{-1/3w}" cite="mit" at="Exercise 8.1, p. 180 (our own addition)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

For a cosmological constant, $w = -1$, and $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.3$: $1 + z_\text{eq} = (7/3)^{1/3} = 1.326$, $z_\text{eq} = 0.33$. For $w = -0.8$: $(7/3)^{1/2.4} = 1.423$, $z_\text{eq} = 0.42$: a dark energy closer to $w = 0$ dilutes more like matter, so the two were equal further in the past.
