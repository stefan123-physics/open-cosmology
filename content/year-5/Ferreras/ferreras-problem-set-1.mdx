## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The spider pattern of a rotating disc" points={10} mit="Exercise 2.1">

A thin disc galaxy rotates on circles, with speed $v(R)$ at radius $R$. Its rotation axis is tilted by the inclination $i$ from our line of sight ($i = 0$ face-on, $i = 90°$ edge-on). The angle $\varphi$ is measured in the plane of the disc, from the major axis (the line where the disc plane cuts the plane of the sky). The galaxy's own (systemic) velocity has been subtracted.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Show that the velocity along the line of sight at the point $(R, \varphi)$ of the disc is

<Formula label="Line-of-sight velocity of a rotating disc" tex="v_\text{los} = v(R)\cos\varphi\,\sin i" cite="mit" at="Exercise 2.1, p. 21" />

(This part is worked in the lesson [rotation curves](/courses/fundamentals-of-dark-matter/rotation-curves#measuring-a-rotation-curve), worked example "The spider pattern". Parts b and c are our additions.)

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write the rotation velocity as a vector in the disc, tilt the disc, and take the component along the line of sight.

<Step title="The velocity in the disc">

Take two directions in the disc: one along the major axis, the other in the disc and perpendicular to it. On circular orbits the velocity is perpendicular to the radius, so at $(R, \varphi)$ it has the component $-v(R)\sin\varphi$ along the major axis and $v(R)\cos\varphi$ along the second direction, up to the sense of rotation.

</Step>

<Step title="Tilting the disc">

The disc is tilted about the major axis. The major axis stays in the plane of the sky, so motion along it adds nothing along the line of sight. The second direction is turned towards us by the tilt: it has the component $\sin i$ along the line of sight (none face-on, all of it edge-on). The line-of-sight velocity is therefore $v(R)\cos\varphi\,\sin i$, with a sign that tells which side approaches.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Face-on ($i = 0$) nothing moves along the line of sight; along the minor axis ($\varphi = 90°$) the motion is across the sky; along the major axis an edge-on disc shows the full $v(R)$. All three come out of the formula.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

(Our addition.) Along the major axis of a disc galaxy tilted by $i = 50°$, the largest line-of-sight velocity measured on the flat part of the rotation curve is 180 km/s. What is the rotation speed? Give it in km/s (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

Where on the disc is the line-of-sight velocity largest, and what fraction of $v(R)$ is seen there?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$v$ in km/s:" value="235" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On the major axis $\cos\varphi = \pm1$, so only the factor $\sin i$ hides part of the rotation.

<Step title="Deproject">

$v = v_\text{los}/\sin i = 180/\sin 50° = 180/0.7660 = 235$ km/s.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The answer is larger than 180 km/s, as it must be: the tilt can only hide motion. Near face-on, the correction $1/\sin i$ becomes large and uncertain, which is why rotation curves are best measured on discs that are well inclined.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

(Our addition.) The lines on the sky along which the line-of-sight velocity is the same give the "spider" its legs. What shape do they have (i) where the rotation curve is flat, $v(R) = $ constant, and (ii) in the inner part, where the disc turns like a solid body, $v(R) \propto R$?

<Hint>

In each case, ask what $v_\text{los}$ depends on. With $v$ constant, only $\varphi$ is left. With $v \propto R$, what does $R\cos\varphi$ measure in the disc?

</Hint>

<Hint>

A tilted disc is a linear projection onto the sky: straight lines in the disc stay straight lines on the sky.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c1" label="(i) In the flat part, lines of equal velocity are" choices={["circles around the centre, one for each radius", "straight lines through the centre of the galaxy", "straight lines parallel to the minor axis", "straight lines parallel to the major axis"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p1-c2" label="(ii) In the solid-body part, they are" choices={["straight lines through the centre of the galaxy", "ellipses with the shape of the tilted disc", "straight lines parallel to the minor axis", "curves bending away from the major axis"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Find the quantity that is fixed along each line, then see what that line looks like in the disc and on the sky.

<Step title="Flat part">

With $v$ constant, $v_\text{los} = v\cos\varphi\,\sin i$ depends on $\varphi$ alone. A line of constant $\varphi$ in the disc is a straight line from the centre outwards, and the tilt keeps it straight. So the lines of equal velocity fan out from the centre, the legs of the spider.

</Step>

<Step title="Solid-body part">

With $v = \omega R$, $v_\text{los} = \omega\,(R\cos\varphi)\sin i$, and $R\cos\varphi$ is the distance from the minor axis, measured along the major axis. It is fixed along lines at a constant distance from the minor axis, which run parallel to the minor axis, in the disc and on the sky.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

A real galaxy rises like a solid body near its centre and then flattens: its lines of equal velocity are parallel near the centre and bend over into a fan further out, the familiar spider shape. Circles around the centre would mean a velocity that depends on $R$ alone, which the factor $\cos\varphi$ rules out.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Weighing a galaxy cluster with the virial theorem" points={15} mit="Exercise 2.2">

The galaxies of a cluster move on random orbits, and the cluster's dynamical mass is written

<Formula label="Dynamical mass of a pressure-supported system" tex="M_\text{dyn} = \alpha\,\frac{\sigma^2 r}{G}" cite="mit" at="Section 2.3, p. 23" />

where $\sigma$ is the velocity dispersion (the standard deviation of the galaxies' line-of-sight velocities), $r$ the size of the system and $\alpha$ a dimensionless factor of order one that depends on its structure. (The book calls this factor $\gamma$; the lesson calls it $\alpha$, and so do we, since $\gamma$ is the shear in Problem 7.) Treat the cluster as a sphere of mass $M$ and radius $R$ in equilibrium, with isotropic random motions, so that $\sigma$ is the same in every direction.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find $\alpha$ for a truncated isothermal sphere: density $\rho \propto 1/r^2$ inside $R$, nothing outside. (Worked in the lesson [galaxy clusters](/courses/fundamentals-of-dark-matter/galaxy-clusters#zwicky-and-the-coma-cluster), worked example "Weighing a cluster"; try it first without looking.) Give $\alpha$ as a number (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

Use the virial theorem, $2K + W = 0$. With random motions in three directions, each with dispersion $\sigma$, what is $K$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $\rho \propto 1/r^2$ the enclosed mass grows in proportion to $r$. Build the sphere shell by shell to find $W$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$\alpha =$" value="3" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The virial theorem ties the kinetic energy of the random motions to the potential energy, which depends on how the mass is arranged.

<Step title="Kinetic energy">

Each of the three directions carries a mean square velocity $\sigma^2$, so $K = \frac12 M \cdot 3\sigma^2 = \frac32 M\sigma^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Potential energy">

For $\rho \propto 1/r^2$, $M(r) = Mr/R$ and the shell from $r$ to $r + dr$ holds $dm = (M/R)\,dr$. Adding the shells one by one,

<Formula label="Potential energy of a truncated isothermal sphere" tex="W = -\int_0^R \frac{G M(r)}{r}\,dm = -\int_0^R \frac{G M^2}{R^2}\,dr = -\frac{G M^2}{R}" cite="mit" at="Exercise 2.2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

$2K + W = 0$ gives $3M\sigma^2 = GM^2/R$, so $M = 3\sigma^2 R/G$ and $\alpha = 3$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

If $\sigma$ were the full three-dimensional dispersion (so that $K = \frac12 M\sigma^2$), the factor would be 1. The book only says that $\alpha$ is of order one; which dispersion is meant must always be stated. Galaxy surveys measure line-of-sight velocities, so the one-dimensional $\sigma$ is the natural reading.

If you found a smaller factor from the Jeans equation, you solved a different sphere: the self-gravitating isothermal sphere (Problem 5 works with it), which does not end at $R$, so the stars outside press on the sphere. The virial theorem then has a surface term: $2K + W$ equals three times that pressure times the volume. The truncated sphere here has nothing outside and no surface term, so it needs more mass for the same $\sigma$, and the factor rises to 3.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

A typical cluster has $\sigma = 1000$ km/s and a radius of 1.5 Mpc (the book's numbers). With $\alpha$ from part (a), what is its mass? Give it in units of $10^{15}\,M_\odot$ (tolerance 2%).

<Hint>

Work in pc, km/s and $M_\odot$, with $G$ in the same units (see the constants above).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" scale="1e15" label="$M$ in $10^{15}\,M_\odot$:" value="1.05" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Insert the numbers into $M = 3\sigma^2R/G$, in units that make $G$ simple.

<Step title="The number">

$M = 3 \times (1000)^2 \times 1.5 \times 10^6 / (4.3009 \times 10^{-3})\,M_\odot = 1.05 \times 10^{15}\,M_\odot$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

A thousand billion solar masses times a thousand: about the mass of a thousand Milky Way halos, the right size for a rich cluster.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

The galaxies of such a cluster emit about $10^{13}\,L_\odot$ in visible light. What is the cluster's mass-to-light ratio, in solar units (tolerance 2%)? Then estimate the fraction of the cluster's mass that is in stars. For the stars, take the mass-to-light ratio $\Upsilon_V = 5\,M_\odot/L_\odot$ (our choice: the book's Figure 2.4 shows model populations several billion years old with stellar mass-to-light ratios of a few, rising with age, and higher for a Salpeter IMF). Give the fraction as a percentage (tolerance 2%).

<Hint>

The stellar mass is the light times the stellar mass-to-light ratio.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-ml" label="$M/L$ in solar units:" value="105" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-ml" />

<Answer id="p2-fstar" scale="0.01" label="Fraction in stars, in %:" value="4.8" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-fstar" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Compare the mass the dynamics needs with the mass the light can account for.

<Step title="Mass-to-light ratio">

$M/L = 1.05 \times 10^{15}/10^{13} = 105$ in solar units.

</Step>

<Step title="Mass in stars">

Stars: $5 \times 10^{13}\,M_\odot$, a fraction $5 \times 10^{13}/1.05 \times 10^{15}$, or 4.8%, of the total.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Even the most generous stellar mass-to-light ratio in the book's figure (a Salpeter IMF at the oldest ages, below 10) would leave the stars under a tenth of the mass. This is Zwicky's problem: the cluster holds an order of magnitude more mass than its stars. Part of the difference is hot gas, which the X-rays reveal (the lesson on galaxy clusters); the rest is dark matter.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

(Our addition.) How much does $\alpha$ depend on the density profile? Find it for a sphere of uniform density, with the same definitions as in part (a) (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

Only $W$ changes. Again build the sphere shell by shell, now with $M(r) = M(r/R)^3$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-d" label="$\alpha$ (uniform sphere) $=$" value="5" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-d" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same virial argument, with the potential energy of a uniform sphere.

<Step title="Potential energy">

With $M(r) = M r^3/R^3$ and $dm = 3M r^2\,dr/R^3$, $W = -\int_0^R G M(r)\,dm/r = -(3GM^2/R^6)\int_0^R r^4\,dr = -\frac35\,GM^2/R$.

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

$3M\sigma^2 = \frac35 GM^2/R$ gives $M = 5\sigma^2R/G$: $\alpha = 5$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

A uniform sphere is less bound than the isothermal one of the same mass and radius (its mass sits further out), so the same $\sigma$ needs more mass to hold it together: $\alpha$ is larger. A factor between 3 and 5 from the profile alone is why such estimates are good to tens of percent, not better, and why a factor of ten between dynamical and stellar mass is robust.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Where the stars' mass lies: the Salpeter IMF" points={10} mit="Exercise 2.3">

Take a Salpeter initial mass function, a single power law in the number of stars born per unit mass:

<Formula label="Salpeter initial mass function" tex="\frac{dN}{dM} \propto M^{-2.35}" cite="mit" at="Exercise 2.3, p. 27" />

[The book writes this law per logarithmic mass interval, $dN/d\log M$. Salpeter's slope 2.35 belongs to the number per unit mass, as in the lesson [counting ordinary matter](/courses/fundamentals-of-dark-matter/counting-ordinary-matter#stellar-mass-from-light), and that is the form used here.] Compare the massive stars, between 10 and 20 $M_\odot$, with the low-mass stars, between 0.1 and 0.5 $M_\odot$.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

How many more low-mass stars than massive stars are born? Give the ratio of numbers, low-mass to massive (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

The number in an interval is the integral of $dN/dM$ over it. The unknown constant of proportionality cancels in the ratio.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-n" label="Ratio of numbers:" value="731" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-n" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Integrate the power law over each interval and divide.

<Step title="The integrals">

$\int_{M_1}^{M_2} M^{-2.35}\,dM = (M_1^{-1.35} - M_2^{-1.35})/1.35$. Low-mass stars: $(0.1^{-1.35} - 0.5^{-1.35})/1.35 = (22.39 - 2.549)/1.35 = 14.69$. Massive stars: $(10^{-1.35} - 20^{-1.35})/1.35 = (0.04467 - 0.01752)/1.35 = 0.02011$.

</Step>

<Step title="The ratio">

$14.69/0.02011 = 731$: for every massive star, more than 700 low-mass stars are born.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Now compare their masses: what is the ratio of the total mass born in low-mass stars to that born in massive stars (tolerance 1%)?

<Hint>

The mass in an interval is the integral of $M\,dN/dM$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-m" label="Ratio of masses:" value="10.0" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-m" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Weight each star by its mass: the integrand becomes $M^{-1.35}$.

<Step title="The integrals">

$\int M^{-1.35}\,dM = -M^{-0.35}/0.35$. Low-mass stars: $(0.1^{-0.35} - 0.5^{-0.35})/0.35 = (2.239 - 1.275)/0.35 = 2.755$. Massive stars: $(10^{-0.35} - 20^{-0.35})/0.35 = (0.4467 - 0.3505)/0.35 = 0.2749$.

</Step>

<Step title="The ratio">

$2.755/0.2749 = 10.0$. A narrow band of faint stars holds ten times the mass of the massive stars, and an old population has lost its massive stars altogether (they leave lighter remnants). Stellar masses therefore depend above all on the low-mass and intermediate stars.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

(Our addition.) Light tells a different story. Take each star's luminosity to scale as $L \propto M^3$ (the rough law of Problem 4). What is the ratio of the light of the massive stars to that of the low-mass stars, at birth (tolerance 1%)?

<Hint>

The integrand is now $M^3\,dN/dM$. Which end of each interval dominates?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-l" label="Ratio of light:" value="322" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-l" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Weight each star by its luminosity: the integrand becomes $M^{0.65}$, which grows with mass.

<Step title="The integrals">

$\int M^{0.65}\,dM = M^{1.65}/1.65$. Massive stars: $(20^{1.65} - 10^{1.65})/1.65 = (140.2 - 44.67)/1.65 = 57.89$. Low-mass stars: $(0.5^{1.65} - 0.1^{1.65})/1.65 = (0.3186 - 0.02239)/1.65 = 0.1795$.

</Step>

<Step title="The ratio">

$57.89/0.1795 = 322$. The massive stars hold a tenth of the low-mass stars' mass but give over 300 times their light. This is why stellar masses are measured in red or near-infrared light, which traces the cool, low-mass stars that carry the mass.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Why the mass-to-light ratio grows with age" points={12} mit="Exercise 2.4">

Generalise Problem 3 to an IMF $dN/dM \propto M^{-x}$ with a free slope $x$ (Salpeter: $x = 2.35$; as in Problem 3, the book writes its slope per logarithmic interval). Consider a simple stellar population, all its stars born at once. Ignore all details of stellar evolution: a star of mass $M$ shines with $L \propto M^3$ as long as it lives, and lives for a time $\tau \propto M^{-3}$, then stops shining. Assume $2 < x < 4$ and that the lowest stellar mass is much smaller than the mass of the stars now dying.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

At age $t$, the stellar mass-to-light ratio grows as a power of the age, $M/L \propto t^p$. Find $p$ as a formula in $x$.

<Hint>

Which stars are still shining at age $t$? Write the largest surviving mass, the turnoff mass, as a power of $t$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The light is $\int L\,dN$ up to the turnoff mass, and the mass is $\int M\,dN$. For $2 < x < 4$, which end of each integral dominates?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-p" label="$p =$" expr="(4 - x)/3"
  vars={{ x: [2.1, 3.5] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(3 - x)/3", hint: "This is what a slope per logarithmic interval, dN/dlog M, would give. Here x is the slope per unit mass, dN/dM." },
    { expr: "(x - 4)/3", hint: "This makes the mass-to-light ratio fall with age. As the brightest stars die, the light drops while the mass hardly changes: which way should the ratio go?" },
    { expr: "4 - x", hint: "This is the power of the turnoff mass, not of the age. How does the turnoff mass depend on t when the lifetime scales as M to the power −3?" }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** As the population ages, the brightest stars die first. The light, dominated by the stars near the turnoff, falls; the mass, held by the faint stars, stays put.

<Step title="The turnoff mass">

A star still shines if its lifetime exceeds $t$: $M^{-3} \gtrsim t$ (in the units of the scaling law), so the stars alive are those below the turnoff mass $M_\text{TO} \propto t^{-1/3}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Light and mass">

The light is $\int^{M_\text{TO}} M^3 M^{-x}\,dM \propto M_\text{TO}^{4-x}$, dominated by the upper end since $4 - x > 0$. The mass in stars is $\int M\,M^{-x}\,dM$: for $x > 2$ the integrand falls steeply, so the integral is dominated by the lowest masses and hardly depends on $M_\text{TO}$. (Counting the remnants of dead stars would only add a little mass, and none of light.)

</Step>

<Step title="The power of the age">

<Formula label="Mass-to-light ratio of an ageing population" tex="\frac{M}{L} \propto M_\text{TO}^{-(4-x)} \propto t^{(4-x)/3}" cite="mit" at="Exercise 2.4 (our own solution)" />

so $p = (4 - x)/3$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

For $x = 4$ every mass interval gives equal light per logarithm and nothing would change; a flatter IMF (smaller $x$, more massive stars) puts more of the light in the stars that die, so the ratio grows faster with age. Both trends come out of the formula.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

For the Salpeter slope, by what factor does the stellar mass-to-light ratio grow between the ages of 2 and 12 billion years (tolerance 1%)?

<Hint>

First find $p$ for $x = 2.35$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="Growth factor:" value="2.68" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A power law turns a ratio of ages into a ratio of mass-to-light ratios.

<Step title="The number">

$p = (4 - 2.35)/3 = 0.55$, and $(12/2)^{0.55} = 6^{0.55} = 2.68$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Compare with the book's Figure 2.4 (p. 24), which shows the V-band mass-to-light ratio of model stellar populations against age, from two sets of population models. Their curves start near zero for the youngest populations and rise steadily, roughly in proportion to age after the first billion years, to values of a few at the oldest ages. What does the toy model get right, and what does it miss?

<Solution>

**The idea.** Compare the shape of $t^{0.55}$ with the near-proportional rise of the models.

<Step title="What it gets right">

The ratio rises with age and keeps rising, for the reason found in part (a): the light comes from the short-lived massive stars, the mass from the long-lived faint ones. A Salpeter IMF, richer in faint stars, gives a higher ratio than a Chabrier IMF at every age, as the book's figure shows; in the toy model the mass integral grows with the number of faint stars while the light hardly changes.

</Step>

<Step title="What it misses">

The toy model rises more slowly ($t^{0.55}$, a factor 2.7 from 2 to 12 billion years) than the models in the figure, which rise roughly in proportion to age. Real stars are not described by $L \propto M^3$ over the whole range of masses, and the toy model leaves out the giant phases (a dying star is briefly far brighter than on the main sequence, so much of an old population's light comes from giants) and the composition of the stars. Its slope is a guide to the trend, not to the numbers.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="The mass of the globular cluster M 13" points={13} mit="Exercise 2.5">

The globular cluster M 13 (NGC 6205) in Hercules has a central velocity dispersion $\sigma_0 = 7.1$ km/s, a core radius $r_c = 1.5$ pc and a tidal radius $r_t = 47$ pc, and its stars are estimated to weigh $M_s = 6 \times 10^5\,M_\odot$ (the book's values). ## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Bending light with Newton" points={10} mit="Exercise 2.6">

A particle passes a point mass $M$ on an almost straight line, with impact parameter $b$ (its closest distance to the mass if it were not deflected). Treat it with Newtonian mechanics, assume the deflection is small and that the particle moves at the speed $c$ all the time, so that gravity changes only its direction.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

By computing the change in its momentum, show that the deflection angle is

<Formula label="Newtonian deflection by a point mass" tex="\alpha = \frac{2GM}{c^2 b}" cite="mit" at="Exercise 2.6, p. 33" />

<Hint>

Along the undeflected straight path, write the position as $z = ct$, with the mass at distance $b$ from the line. Which component of the force changes the direction?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The integral $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} dz\,(b^2 + z^2)^{-3/2}$ equals $2/b^2$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a small deflection, add up the sideways pull along the unperturbed straight path; the sideways velocity gained, divided by $c$, is the angle.

<Step title="The sideways force">

At the point $z = ct$ on the path, the distance to the mass is $\sqrt{b^2 + z^2}$, and the component of the acceleration towards the line through the mass, perpendicular to the path, is $GM b/(b^2 + z^2)^{3/2}$. The component along the path speeds the particle up and then slows it down by the same amount, so it cancels.

</Step>

<Step title="The velocity gained">

<Formula label="Sideways velocity gained" tex="\Delta v_\perp = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{GMb}{(b^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\,\frac{dz}{c} = \frac{GMb}{c}\cdot\frac{2}{b^2} = \frac{2GM}{bc}" cite="mit" at="Exercise 2.6 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The angle">

The direction changes by $\Delta v_\perp/c = 2GM/(c^2 b)$, which is the result. The momentum view is the same: the momentum per unit mass changes by $\Delta v_\perp$ across a forward momentum per unit mass $c$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The angle is dimensionless ($GM/b$ is a squared speed), grows with $M$ and falls with $b$, as it should. General relativity gives twice this, $4GM/(c^2 b)$ (the lesson [gravitational lensing](/courses/fundamentals-of-dark-matter/gravitational-lensing#why-light-bends)).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 7

<Exercise id="p7" title="Lensing by a power-law density profile" points={15} mit="Exercise 2.7">

A circularly symmetric lens has a density profile $\rho(r) \propto r^{-n}$, with $1 < n < 3$. For a symmetric lens, the deflection angle $\alpha(\theta)$ (scaled, so that the lens equation reads $\beta = \theta - \alpha(\theta)$), the convergence $\kappa$ and the shear $\gamma$ are related by

<Formula label="Deflection of a symmetric lens" tex="\alpha(\theta) = \frac{2}{\theta}\int_0^\theta \theta'\,\kappa(\theta')\,d\theta'" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.22), p. 36" />

<Formula label="Convergence and shear of a symmetric lens" tex="\kappa = \frac12\left(\frac{\alpha}{\theta} + \frac{d\alpha}{d\theta}\right), \qquad \gamma = \frac12\left(\frac{\alpha}{\theta} - \frac{d\alpha}{d\theta}\right)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (2.23), p. 36" />

and the magnification of an image is $\mu = 1/[(1-\kappa)^2 - \gamma^2]$ (Eq. 2.21, p. 36; the book prints $1 - \kappa^2$ in its denominator, a slip, and $\alpha(\varphi)$ for $\alpha(\theta)$ on the left of Eq. 2.22). Define $\theta_0$ as the angle where $\alpha(\theta_0) = \theta_0$: a source exactly behind the lens ($\beta = 0$) is seen as a ring of radius $\theta_0$.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Show that the deflection is $\alpha(\theta) = \theta_0\,(\theta/\theta_0)^{2-n}$ and that

<Formula label="Convergence and shear of a power-law lens" tex="\kappa(\theta) = \frac{3-n}{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{\theta_0}\right)^{1-n}, \qquad \gamma(\theta) = \frac{n-1}{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{\theta_0}\right)^{1-n}" cite="mit" at="Exercise 2.7, Eq. (2.27), p. 37" />

<Hint>

Project the density along the line of sight: how does the surface density scale with the projected radius? Substituting $z = R\,u$ in the integral over $z$ helps.

</Hint>

<Hint>

With $\kappa = A\,\theta^{1-n}$, integrate Eq. (2.22). Then fix $A$ with $\alpha(\theta_0) = \theta_0$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** A power law in three dimensions projects to a power law on the sky, one power less steep; the rest is integration.

<Step title="The convergence is a power law">

$\Sigma(R) = \int \rho\,dz \propto \int (R^2 + z^2)^{-n/2}\,dz = R^{1-n}\int (1 + u^2)^{-n/2}\,du$, and the last integral is a finite number for $n > 1$. So $\kappa = A\,\theta^{1-n}$ with some constant $A$.

</Step>

<Step title="The deflection">

$\alpha = \frac{2}{\theta}\int_0^\theta A\,\theta'^{2-n}\,d\theta' = \frac{2A}{3-n}\,\theta^{2-n}$, which converges at 0 for $n < 3$. Setting $\alpha(\theta_0) = \theta_0$ gives $A = \frac{3-n}{2}\,\theta_0^{n-1}$, so $\alpha = \theta_0(\theta/\theta_0)^{2-n}$ and $\kappa = \frac{3-n}{2}(\theta/\theta_0)^{1-n}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The shear">

$d\alpha/d\theta = (2-n)(\theta/\theta_0)^{1-n}$ and $\alpha/\theta = (\theta/\theta_0)^{1-n}$. Then $\kappa = \frac12(1 + 2 - n)(\theta/\theta_0)^{1-n}$, as found, and $\gamma = \frac12(1 - 2 + n)(\theta/\theta_0)^{1-n} = \frac{n-1}{2}(\theta/\theta_0)^{1-n}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

For $n = 2$, the isothermal sphere, $\alpha = \theta_0$ is constant, and $\kappa = \gamma = \theta_0/2\theta$. As $n \to 3$ the convergence vanishes away from the centre and $\gamma = (\theta_0/\theta)^2$: all the mass looks concentrated in the middle, the point-mass lens.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

(Our addition.) For the isothermal sphere, $n = 2$, find the magnification $\mu$ of an image at $\theta > \theta_0$, as a formula in $\theta$ and $\theta_0$.

<Hint>

Insert $\kappa$ and $\gamma$ for $n = 2$ into $\mu = 1/[(1-\kappa)^2 - \gamma^2]$, and factor the difference of two squares.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-b" label="$\mu =$" expr="th/(th - th0)"
  vars={{ th: [1.5, 3], th0: [0.7, 1] }}
  names={{ th: "\\theta", th0: "\\theta_0" }}

    { expr: "th/(th + th0)", hint: "This is below 1, so the image would be fainter than the source. Outside the ring an isothermal lens always brightens the image: check the sign of the term in θ₀/θ." }

**The idea.** For $n = 2$ the convergence and the shear are equal, so one factor of the determinant is exactly 1.

With $\kappa = \gamma = \theta_0/2\theta$: $(1-\kappa)^2 - \gamma^2 = (1 - \kappa - \gamma)(1 - \kappa + \gamma) = (1 - \theta_0/\theta) \times 1$.

</Step>

<Step title="The magnification">

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Far from the lens, $\theta \gg \theta_0$, the magnification tends to 1: no lensing. Near the ring it diverges, as the source approaches the caustic. </Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Show that $1 - \kappa - \gamma = 0$ at $\theta = \theta_0$ for every $n$. Then, for a profile shallower than isothermal ($1 < n < 2$), find the radius $\theta_r$ where $1 - \kappa + \gamma = 0$, as a formula in $n$ and $\theta_0$.

<Hint>

Add and subtract the expressions of part (a): both factors are 1 minus a constant times $(\theta/\theta_0)^{1-n}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-c" label="$\theta_r =$" expr="th0*(2 - n)^(1/(n - 1))"
  vars={{ n: [1.2, 1.8], th0: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ n: "n", th0: "\\theta_0" }}

</Step>

</Solution>
