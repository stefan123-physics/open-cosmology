The previous page ruled out ordinary neutrinos and showed that dark matter must be cold. Ferreras's Sections 6.4 and 6.5 describe the two candidates that have dominated the search: **WIMPs** and **axions**.

## WIMPs and the "miracle"

A **weakly interacting massive particle** (WIMP) is one of the simplest additions to the particle zoo. To be dark matter it must be non-relativistic at freeze-out (cold), very stable, with a lifetime longer than the age of the universe, and interact through gravity and, very weakly, the other forces.

A species that falls out of equilibrium while non-relativistic is left with a relic density set by its annihilation cross-section (on this site derived in [freeze-out](/courses/8-902/dark-matter-freeze-out)). Solving numerically for the freeze-out point gives

<Formula label="WIMP relic density" tex="\Omega_\text{WIMP}\,h^2 \sim \frac{10^{-26}\ \text{cm}^3\,\text{s}^{-1}}{\langle\sigma v\rangle} \simeq 0.1\left(\frac{0.01}{\alpha}\right)^2\left(\frac{m_\chi}{100\ \text{GeV}}\right)^2" cite="mit" at="Eq. (6.10), p. 144" />

[The book writes the mass to the first power, $m/100$ GeV, and speaks of a degeneracy in $m/\alpha^2$. With its own assumption $\langle\sigma v\rangle \propto \alpha^2/m^2$ the relic density, $\propto 1/\langle\sigma v\rangle$, scales as $m^2/\alpha^2$, so the square is used here and the degeneracy is in $m/\alpha$.]

where the second form assumes $\langle\sigma v\rangle \propto \alpha^2/m_\chi^2$, with $\alpha$ a coupling constant. With weak-scale values (a mass near 100 GeV and a coupling typical of the weak force), the result lands on the observed dark matter density. This coincidence is the **WIMP miracle**.

But the relic density depends only on the combination $m_\chi/\alpha$. A heavier particle needs a stronger coupling to annihilate efficiently and leave the same density; stronger couplings would also show up in detectors. A lighter particle with a weaker coupling can give the same density while escaping detection. This freedom away from the weak scale is called the **WIMPless miracle**.

<WorkedExample title="Mass and coupling go together">
<Problem>
Using the second form of the relic-density formula, what coupling would a 1 TeV particle need to give the same relic density as a 100 GeV particle with $\alpha = 0.01$?
</Problem>
<Step title="Keep the ratio fixed">
The density depends on $(m_\chi/\alpha)^2$, so the same density needs the same $m_\chi/\alpha = 100\ \text{GeV}/0.01 = 10^4$ GeV.
</Step>
<Step title="The coupling">
For $m_\chi = 1000$ GeV: $\alpha = 1000/10^4 = 0.1$, ten times stronger. Such a particle would interact much more readily in a detector, which is how experiments can test the idea.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

**Supersymmetry.** Several extensions of the Standard Model contain WIMP-like particles: a massive sterile neutrino, for instance, or a stable **supersymmetric** particle. Supersymmetry, proposed partly to help unify the forces at high energies, gives every particle a **superpartner** with the opposite statistics: each fermion a boson, each boson a fermion. The classes of superpartners are squarks, gluinos, charginos, neutralinos and sleptons. If a quantity called **R-parity** is conserved, a superpartner cannot decay into Standard Model particles alone, so the **lightest supersymmetric particle** (LSP) must be stable. To be dark matter it must be electrically neutral and avoid other detectable interactions; sneutrinos, for example, are excluded because their neutral-current interactions would already have been seen. The **neutralino** remained a natural WIMP.

The discovery of the **Higgs boson** at 125 GeV in 2012 (on this site: [the Higgs field](/courses/8-286/higgs-field)), with no other new particles in the mass range of the Large Hadron Collider, has complicated this picture: a typical WIMP mass of 0.1–1 TeV should have been within reach. Either the superpartners are much heavier, which weakens their case as dark matter, or they somehow escape detection.

## Axions

When a symmetry is broken spontaneously, new particles appear: **Nambu–Goldstone bosons**, or **pseudo**-Nambu–Goldstone bosons when the breaking is only approximate and the particles have a small mass. The **QCD axion** is one of these. The Lagrangian of the strong force can contain a term, with a parameter $\theta$, that violates **CP symmetry** and would give the neutron an electric dipole moment. None is found: $\theta \lesssim 10^{-10}$. Why should it be so small? Peccei and Quinn proposed a symmetry-breaking mechanism that drives $\theta$ to zero by turning it into a field, and the quantum of that field is a new particle, the **axion**.

Its mass is not fixed by the theory, but it must be small, typically well below 1 eV, with very weak interactions. Similar particles in other theories are called **axion-like particles** (ALPs). Produced thermally, axions would be light, hot relics and could not be the main dark matter. But they can also be made **non-thermally** (by "vacuum realignment" of the field as the universe cools, or by the decay of topological defects) as a cold population that could be all the dark matter, $\Omega_\text{dm}h^2 \approx 0.12$.

The symmetry-breaking scale is unknown, so in principle the axion mass could lie anywhere from about $10^{-12}$ eV to 1 MeV. Astrophysics narrows it. Stars would produce axions through Compton-like scattering ($\gamma + e \to a + e$), the Primakoff effect (a photon converting into an axion in the field of a nucleus) and bremsstrahlung-like processes. Axions escape at once, while photons diffuse slowly out, so axion emission drains stars and shortens their lives:

- the long lives of **red giants**, which dominate the light of old elliptical galaxies, rule out masses between about 0.5 eV and 200 keV;
- axions with stronger couplings to electrons are excluded above about $10^{-2}$ eV;
- **supernovae** would lose too much energy to axions, rather than to neutrinos, down to about $10^{-3}$ eV.

Together with laboratory searches, axions must be lighter than about $10^{-3}$ eV.

## Summary

- WIMPs are stable, cold, weakly interacting particles. The relic density $\Omega h^2 \sim (10^{-26}\ \text{cm}^3/\text{s})/\langle\sigma v\rangle$ comes out right for weak-scale masses and couplings: the WIMP miracle.
- Only $m/\alpha$ matters, so lighter–weaker or heavier–stronger particles also work (the WIMPless miracle).
- Supersymmetry with conserved R-parity gives a stable lightest superpartner, such as the neutralino. The LHC has found the Higgs boson (125 GeV, 2012) but no superpartners in the expected 0.1–1 TeV range.
- Axions solve the strong CP problem ($\theta \lesssim 10^{-10}$, Peccei–Quinn). Produced non-thermally they are cold dark matter. Stellar and laboratory limits put their mass below about $10^{-3}$ eV.
