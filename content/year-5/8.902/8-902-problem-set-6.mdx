This set practises the end of section 2 of the 8.902 notes: [the spherical collapse model](/courses/8-902/spherical-collapse) and [the Press–Schechter mass function](/courses/8-902/press-schechter), both in the Lecture 16 notes, with the [virial theorem](/courses/8-902/virial-theorem) from the start of the course. The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 6 (Fall 2023), the last set of the course, in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

Several things differ from MIT's sheet. Its due date is left out. The sheet says that, unless stated otherwise, the universe is flat with $\Omega_M = 0.3$, $\Omega_\Lambda = 0.7$ and $H_0 = 71$ km/s/Mpc. **Problem 1 states otherwise**: it asks for an Einstein–de Sitter universe (flat, matter only). We keep MIT's $H_0 = 71$ km/s/Mpc there, so in Problem 1 the background is matter only with the critical density for that $H_0$ (our reading); a check in Problem 1(A) says what the flat model with $\Omega_M = 0.3$ would change. Problem 2 needs no cosmological numbers. Problem 1 asks for estimates, and an estimate depends on choices the sheet leaves open (which time is "the" free-fall time, what the gas is made of, how the cooling rate is defined): each part states the choice we make ("our reading", "our assumption"), and its answer box accepts that route within a few percent. MIT points to a textbook (Sparke and Gallagher) for cooling curves; the textbook is not used here, and the cooling time is defined in Problem 1(C) by us. Problem 2(B) asks the reader to read about the excursion-set formalism of Bond, Cole, Efstathiou and Kaiser (1991); our solution explains it in our own words, from the paper's bibliographic record and standard results, without the paper's text. The answer boxes of Problem 2 are our additions. MIT's Problem 3 is a bonus for a drawing; it is optional here.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it; type decimals, for example `0.25` or `340`, in the unit the box names. A choice box takes one click.

MIT's sheet gives points only for the bonus (five points). The other points are ours: 60 for Problem 1 and 40 for Problem 2, 100 points in all, plus MIT's 5 bonus points for Problem 3, which is optional here; every split between parts is ours too. Parts that ask you to show or explain something have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons. In Problem 1, $z$ is the redshift, $H_0$ the Hubble constant, $\bar\rho$ the mean (background) density, $\xi$ the sphere's density at turnaround in units of the background density then, $\rho_\text{ta}$ and $R_\text{ta}$ the sphere's density and radius at turnaround, $R_\text{vir}$ its radius after virialisation, $M$ its total mass, $t_\text{ff}$ the free-fall time, $T$ the gas temperature, $\mu$ the mean mass of a gas particle in units of the proton mass $m_p$, $k_B$ Boltzmann's constant, $f_b$ the fraction of the mass in baryons, $n_\text{H}$ the number density of hydrogen nuclei, $\Lambda(T)$ the cooling function (MIT's letter: here it is not the cosmological constant) and $t_\text{cool}$ the cooling time. In Problem 2, $\bar\delta$ is the density contrast smoothed on the scale of a mass $M$, $\sigma_R$ its standard deviation today, $D$ the growth factor, $\delta_c \approx 1.69$ the critical linear density contrast, $F(M, z)$ the collapsed fraction and $S = \sigma_R^2$ the variance used as a "time" in the excursion-set picture.

Constants (CODATA 2018 for $G$, $k_B$ and $m_p$): $G = 6.67430 \times 10^{-11}$ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻², $k_B = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K, $m_p = 1.672622 \times 10^{-27}$ kg; the solar mass $M_\odot = 1.98841 \times 10^{30}$ kg (from $GM_\odot = 1.3271244 \times 10^{20}$ m³/s² of NASA's Sun fact sheet, divided by $G$); 1 Mpc $= 3.0857 \times 10^{22}$ m and 1 Myr $= 3.15576 \times 10^{13}$ s (Julian years).

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A galaxy forming in a spherical top hat: free fall, heating and cooling" points={60} mit="Problem 1">

A galaxy forms from a spherical top hat, a uniform overdense sphere, in an Einstein–de Sitter universe. The sphere breaks away from the Hubble flow and reaches its maximum radius (turnaround) at redshift $z = 8$. As MIT allows, you need not derive the sphere's overdensity at turnaround again: the notes find

<Formula label="Overdensity of the sphere at turnaround" tex="\xi = \frac{\rho_\text{ta}}{\bar\rho(t_\text{ta})} = \left(\frac{3\pi}{4}\right)^2 \approx 5.55" cite="reading1" at="eq. (436), p. 94" />

and, from the virial theorem, that the sphere settles at half its turnaround radius, $R_\text{vir} = R_\text{ta}/2$ (reading 1, eq. 446 and the line after it, p. 96). Our reading of the background: matter only, with the critical density for MIT's $H_0 = 71$ km/s/Mpc, so $\bar\rho = 3H_0^2(1+z)^3/8\pi G$.

<Part label="A" points={12}>

What is the dynamical (free-fall) time of this forming galaxy? Our reading: the free-fall time is the time the sphere takes to fall from rest at turnaround to the centre. Give it in Myr (tolerance 2%).

<Hint>

At turnaround the sphere is at rest. A cold uniform sphere of density $\rho$ falling from rest reaches its centre after the time $\sqrt{3\pi/32G\rho}$ (stated by us; the solution derives it from the notes). So you need $\rho_\text{ta}$ in kg/m³.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\rho_\text{ta} = \xi\,\bar\rho$ at $z = 8$, and $1 + z = 9$. Convert $H_0$ to s⁻¹ first.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-tff" label="$t_\text{ff}$ in Myr:" value="340" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-tff" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The overdensity sets the sphere's density at turnaround, and the density alone sets how long a cold sphere takes to fall in.

<Step title="The density at turnaround">

$H_0 = 71$ km/s/Mpc $= 2.301 \times 10^{-18}$ s⁻¹, so the critical density today is $3H_0^2/8\pi G = 9.469 \times 10^{-27}$ kg/m³. At $z = 8$ the background is $9^3 = 729$ times denser, $\bar\rho = 6.903 \times 10^{-24}$ kg/m³, and the sphere is $\xi$ times denser still: $\rho_\text{ta} = 3.832 \times 10^{-23}$ kg/m³.

</Step>

<Step title="The free-fall time, from the notes' solution">

The notes' solution, eq. (435), reaches turnaround ($y = 1$) at $\tau\sqrt\xi = \pi/2$, and the motion is symmetric about turnaround, so the fall from $y = 1$ back to $y = 0$ also takes $\Delta\tau = \pi/(2\sqrt\xi)$, with $\tau = H_\text{ta}t$. With eq. (427), $\xi H_\text{ta}^2 = 8\pi G\rho_\text{ta}/3$, and

<Formula label="Free-fall time of the sphere" tex="t_\text{ff} = \frac{\pi}{2\sqrt\xi\,H_\text{ta}} = \sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{32\,G\rho_\text{ta}}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(A), p. 1 (our own solution, from eqs. (427) and (435) of reading 1)" />

$t_\text{ff} = \sqrt{3\pi/(32 \times 6.6743 \times 10^{-11} \times 3.832 \times 10^{-23})}$ s $= 1.073 \times 10^{16}$ s $= 340$ Myr.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

An independent route: turnaround happens at $\tau = 2/3$ and collapse at $\tau = 4/3$ (reading 1, p. 94), so the fall takes exactly as long as the time from the Big Bang to turnaround, the age of an Einstein–de Sitter universe at $z = 8$: $t_\text{ta} = 2/[3H_0(1+z)^{3/2}] = 2/(3 \times 2.301 \times 10^{-18} \times 27)$ s $= 340$ Myr. The two routes agree because $\xi = (3\pi/4)^2$: this is where the overdensity enters. The galaxy collapses at $2t_\text{ta} = 680$ Myr, when $1 + z = 9/4^{1/3} = 5.67$. A cruder estimate, $1/\sqrt{G\rho_\text{ta}} = 627$ Myr, is 1.84 times longer; for an order-of-magnitude argument that would do too, but the box asks for the time defined above. (Our remark on MIT's default cosmology: in a flat universe with $\Omega_M = 0.3$ the universe at $z = 8$ is still almost matter-only, but the matter density there is only 0.3 times the Einstein–de Sitter value for the same $H_0$; the time $2/3H(z)$ is then 620 Myr, 1.82 times longer.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="B" points={16}>

Suppose all the gravitational potential energy released in the collapse heats the gas, and nothing cools it. What is the gas temperature after virialisation? Take the density inside the sphere to be uniform and the gas to be ideal. Our reading and assumptions: the energy released is the potential energy given up as the sphere falls from turnaround to its virial radius; per unit mass it becomes the thermal energy per unit mass of the gas, $\frac32k_BT/(\mu m_p)$; and the gas is fully ionised hydrogen, so $\mu = 1/2$. Write the result as $T = T_{12}\,(M/10^{12}M_\odot)^p$. Give $T_{12}$ in units of $10^6$ K (tolerance 2%) and the exponent $p$ as a decimal (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

A uniform sphere of mass $M$ and radius $R$ has the potential energy $-\frac35GM^2/R$ (stated by us). How much is released between $R_\text{ta}$ and $R_\text{ta}/2$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

$R_\text{ta}$ follows from $M = \frac{4\pi}{3}R_\text{ta}^3\rho_\text{ta}$, with $\rho_\text{ta}$ from part (A). How does $M/R_\text{ta}$ grow with $M$ when $\rho_\text{ta}$ is fixed?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-t12" label="$T_{12}$ in $10^6$ K:" value="1.39" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-t12" />

<Answer id="p1-p" label="$p =$" value="0.667" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-p" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Falling from $R_\text{ta}$ to $R_\text{ta}/2$ releases as much energy as the sphere had bound at turnaround; shared over all the mass, it sets the temperature.

<Step title="The energy released">

$\frac35GM^2\left(\frac{1}{R_\text{ta}/2} - \frac{1}{R_\text{ta}}\right) = \frac35\frac{GM^2}{R_\text{ta}}$. Per unit mass this is $\frac35GM/R_\text{ta}$. Setting it equal to $\frac32k_BT/(\mu m_p)$:

<Formula label="Temperature after virialisation" tex="k_BT = \frac25\,\frac{G M \mu m_p}{R_\text{ta}}, \qquad R_\text{ta} = \left(\frac{3M}{4\pi\rho_\text{ta}}\right)^{1/3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(B), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

For $M = 10^{12}M_\odot = 1.988 \times 10^{42}$ kg: $R_\text{ta} = 2.314 \times 10^{21}$ m $= 75.0$ kpc (and $R_\text{vir} = 37.5$ kpc), and $k_BT = 0.4 \times 6.6743 \times 10^{-11} \times 1.988 \times 10^{42} \times 0.5 \times 1.6726 \times 10^{-27}/2.314 \times 10^{21}$ J, so $T = 1.39 \times 10^6$ K.

Since $R_\text{ta} \propto M^{1/3}$ at fixed $\rho_\text{ta}$, $T \propto M/M^{1/3} = M^{2/3}$:

<Formula label="Virial temperature of the galaxy" tex="T \approx 1.39\times10^{6}\ \text{K}\left(\frac{M}{10^{12}M_\odot}\right)^{2/3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(B), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

so $p = 2/3 = 0.667$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The virial theorem gives the same: after virialisation the kinetic (here thermal) energy is half the magnitude of the potential energy, $\frac12 \times \frac35GM^2/R_\text{vir} = \frac35GM^2/R_\text{ta}$, the released energy above. If instead the whole potential energy at the virial radius, $\frac35GM^2/R_\text{vir}$, went into heat, $T$ would be twice as large, $2.78 \times 10^6$ K; that breaks energy conservation, because the sphere already had the energy $-\frac35GM^2/R_\text{ta}$ at turnaround. With $\mu = 0.6$ (ionised gas with helium) instead of our $\frac12$, $T$ would be $1.67 \times 10^6$ K. A million kelvin is the temperature of the hot gas seen in X-rays around massive galaxies: at these temperatures hydrogen is fully ionised, as assumed.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="C" points={16}>

If the gas starts at this temperature, estimate its cooling time. A fraction $f_b = 0.05$ of the mass is baryons, and the cooling function is $\Lambda(T) = 10^{-23}$ erg cm³ s⁻¹ at these temperatures (MIT's values). Our assumptions: the gas, still fully ionised hydrogen, fills the virialised sphere uniformly; it radiates the energy $n_\text{H}^2\Lambda$ per unit volume and time (with the electron density equal to $n_\text{H}$); and the cooling time is its thermal energy per unit volume divided by that rate. Give $n_\text{H}$ in units of $10^{-3}$ cm⁻³ and $t_\text{cool}$ for $M = 10^{12}M_\odot$ in Myr (tolerance 2% each).

<Hint>

The virialised sphere has 8 times the density at turnaround (half the radius). The gas density is $f_b$ times that.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Each hydrogen nucleus brings one electron: the thermal energy per unit volume is $\frac32(n_\text{H} + n_e)k_BT$. Work in cgs units, where $\Lambda$ is given.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-nh" label="$n_\text{H}$ in $10^{-3}$ cm⁻³:" value="9.16" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-nh" />

<Answer id="p1-tcool" label="$t_\text{cool}$ in Myr:" value="199" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-tcool" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The gas holds $3n_\text{H}k_BT$ of heat per unit volume and loses $n_\text{H}^2\Lambda$ per unit volume and time: the ratio is the time it takes to radiate its heat away.

<Step title="The gas density">

$\rho_\text{vir} = 8\rho_\text{ta} = 3.066 \times 10^{-22}$ kg/m³, so the gas has $f_b\rho_\text{vir} = 1.533 \times 10^{-23}$ kg/m³ and $n_\text{H} = 1.533 \times 10^{-23}/1.6726 \times 10^{-27}$ m⁻³ $= 9.16 \times 10^3$ m⁻³ $= 9.16 \times 10^{-3}$ cm⁻³. It does not depend on $M$.

</Step>

<Step title="The cooling time">

<Formula label="Cooling time of the gas" tex="t_\text{cool} = \frac{\tfrac32(n_\text{H} + n_e)k_BT}{n_\text{H}^2\Lambda} = \frac{3k_BT}{n_\text{H}\Lambda}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(C), p. 1 (our own definition and solution)" />

With $k_B = 1.380649 \times 10^{-16}$ erg/K and $T = 1.39 \times 10^6$ K: $t_\text{cool} = 3 \times 1.380649 \times 10^{-16} \times 1.39 \times 10^6/(9.16 \times 10^{-3} \times 10^{-23})$ s $= 6.28 \times 10^{15}$ s $= 199$ Myr.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The units: erg/(cm⁻³ × erg cm³ s⁻¹) $=$ s. Another check of $\rho_\text{vir}$: it should be $18\pi^2 \approx 178$ times the background density at collapse (reading 1, eq. 447, p. 96), and the background then is $\bar\rho(z{=}8)/4$; $178 \times 6.903 \times 10^{-24}/4 = 3.066 \times 10^{-22}$ kg/m³, the same. Since $n_\text{H}$ is fixed and $T \propto M^{2/3}$, $t_\text{cool} \propto M^{2/3}$ too: heavier galaxies are hotter and cool more slowly.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="D" points={16}>

One criterion for galaxy formation is that the gas must cool faster than the sphere collapses, $t_\text{cool} < t_\text{ff}$. Comparing parts (A) and (C), what is the largest mass of a galaxy that meets it? Give it in units of $10^{12}M_\odot$ (tolerance 3%).

<Hint>

$t_\text{ff}$ does not depend on $M$; $t_\text{cool}$ grows as a power of $M$ that part (C) gives.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-mmax" label="$M_\text{max}$ in $10^{12}M_\odot$:" value="2.23" tolerance="3%" calc="p1-mmax" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Below some mass the gas cools before the sphere has finished falling in, and it can sink and form stars; above it, the gas stays hot.

<Step title="The mass">

$t_\text{cool} = 199\ \text{Myr}\,(M/10^{12}M_\odot)^{2/3}$ equals $t_\text{ff} = 340$ Myr when

<Formula label="Largest mass that cools within a free-fall time" tex="M_\text{max} = 10^{12}M_\odot\left(\frac{t_\text{ff}}{t_\text{cool}(10^{12}M_\odot)}\right)^{3/2} = 10^{12}M_\odot\left(\frac{340}{199}\right)^{3/2} \approx 2.2\times10^{12}\,M_\odot" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(D), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At $M_\text{max}$ the temperature is $1.39 \times 10^6\ \text{K} \times 2.23^{2/3} = 2.37 \times 10^6$ K and $t_\text{cool} = 199 \times 2.23^{2/3}$ Myr $= 340$ Myr $= t_\text{ff}$, as it should be. The answer is about $10^{12}M_\odot$, the mass of the halos of the largest galaxies such as the Milky Way: heavier systems (groups and clusters) keep most of their gas hot, which is why they hold hot X-ray gas rather than one giant galaxy. The exact number depends on our choices ($\mu$, the cooling function, the free-fall time); only its order of magnitude is meant.

</Step>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The Press–Schechter normalisation and the excursion-set formalism" points={40} mit="Problem 2">

In the Press–Schechter argument the fraction of space where the smoothed linear density contrast exceeds $\delta_c$ is taken to be the fraction of the mass in halos of mass $M$ or more:

<Formula label="Collapsed fraction" tex="F(M, z) = \frac12\,\text{erfc}\!\left(\frac{\delta_c}{\sqrt2\,\sigma_R D(z)}\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (452), p. 97" />

where $\sigma_R$ falls as $M$ grows. Every bit of mass should belong to a halo of some mass, so the fractions in all mass intervals should add up to one (reading 1, eq. 457, p. 97).

<Part label="A" points={20}>

Show that the normalisation is wrong: integrate the volume fraction $|\partial F/\partial M|\,dM$ over all masses and show that it accounts for only half of the mass. Then (our addition) find the fraction of all mass that plain Press–Schechter puts in halos heavier than the characteristic mass $M_*$, where $\sigma_R D = \delta_c$ (as a decimal, tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

You do not need the derivative: the integral of $|\partial F/\partial M|$ is a difference of two values of $F$. What does $\sigma_R$ do as $M \to 0$ and as $M \to \infty$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-mstar" label="Fraction of mass above $M_*$:" value="0.159" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-mstar" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $F$ is a cumulative fraction, so integrating its derivative only gives its two end values.

<Step title="The integral">

$F$ falls as $M$ grows, so $|\partial F/\partial M| = -\partial F/\partial M$ and

<Formula label="Total fraction of the mass in halos" tex="\int_0^\infty \left|\frac{\partial F}{\partial M}\right| dM = F(M \to 0) - F(M \to \infty) = \frac12\,\text{erfc}(0) - \frac12\,\text{erfc}(\infty) = \frac12" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(A), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

As $M \to \infty$, the smoothing scale grows, $\sigma_R \to 0$, the argument of erfc goes to infinity and $F \to 0$. As $M \to 0$, $\sigma_R \to \infty$ (true for a power spectrum $P \propto k^n$ with $n > -3$ on small scales, where $\sigma_R^2 \propto M^{-(n+3)/3}$; for cold dark matter it grows only slowly towards small masses, and the argument assumes that it keeps growing), the argument goes to zero and $F \to \frac12\text{erfc}(0) = \frac12$. Only half the mass is ever counted as collapsed.

</Step>

<Step title="Why half">

A Gaussian with zero mean is above any threshold at most half of the time, however wide it is: as $\sigma_R \to \infty$ the threshold $\delta_c$ becomes negligible, and exactly the half of space with $\bar\delta > 0$ is counted. The underdense half, $\bar\delta < 0$ on the small scale, is never counted, although much of it lies inside larger regions that have collapsed.

</Step>

<Step title="Our addition: the mass above M_*">

At $M_*$, $\delta_c/(\sigma_RD) = 1$, so $F = \frac12\text{erfc}(1/\sqrt2) = 0.159$: the Gaussian tail beyond one standard deviation.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Numerically, with $\sigma_R \propto M^{-1/4}$ or $M^{-1/2}$, integrating $|\partial F/\partial \ln M|$ over $\ln M$ gives 0.5000 (our calculation). With the factor 2 of the notes the fraction above $M_*$ becomes 0.317.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="B" points={20}>

An alternative derivation of the mass function is the excursion-set formalism, or extended Press–Schechter (Bond et al. 1991; MIT's sheet spells it "Excusion"). Explain its basic idea, and how it fixes the "fudge factor" problem of part (A). Mark your explanation yourself against the solution. Then (our additions) answer the two questions in the boxes.

<Hint>

Fix one point in space and smooth the density field around it on a scale $R$ that starts very large and shrinks. Plot $\bar\delta$ against $S = \sigma_R^2$, which grows as $R$ shrinks. What does the curve look like?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Count a point as part of a halo of mass larger than $M$ if its curve has crossed $\delta_c$ at any $S$ smaller than $S(M)$, not only if it is above $\delta_c$ at $S(M)$. How many curves cross and come back down?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-filter" label="The steps of the walk are independent when the window function is" choices={["a top hat in real space, the sphere of radius R used for the mass", "a Gaussian of width R, which smooths all scales gently", "a sharp cut in Fourier space, keeping only waves with k below k_s", "any window at all, since a Gaussian field always gives independent steps"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="p2-factor" label="The missing factor 2 comes from" choices={["counting each halo twice, once at turnaround and again when it collapses and virialises", "walks that crossed δ_c at a smaller S and fell back below it, as many as the walks above δ_c", "lowering the collapse threshold to δ_c/2, which the walks reach twice as often as δ_c itself", "the window entering the variance squared, so that the collapsed fraction doubles at every mass"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Follow the smoothed density contrast at one point as the smoothing scale shrinks: it wanders like a random walk, and a point belongs to the largest halo whose scale first crosses $\delta_c$.

<Step title="The walk">

Fix a point and smooth the linear density field around it on a scale $R$, from $R = \infty$ (where $\bar\delta = 0$ and $S = \sigma_R^2 = 0$) down to small $R$ (large $S$). Each step to a smaller $R$ adds the Fourier modes in a new shell of $k$. With a window that is a sharp cut in $k$-space, each step adds new modes only, which are independent of the earlier ones in a Gaussian field: $\bar\delta(S)$ is then a random walk (Brownian motion) in the "time" $S$, with $\langle\bar\delta^2\rangle = S$. Every point of space has its own walk.

</Step>

<Step title="The rule">

A point is in a halo of mass larger than $M$ if its walk has crossed the barrier $\delta_c$ (evolved with $D$) at **some** $S < S(M)$, that is on some scale larger than $R(M)$. The mass of the halo it belongs to is set by the **first** crossing, the largest scale on which the region has collapsed. Plain Press–Schechter only asks whether the walk is above $\delta_c$ at $S(M)$ itself, and misses walks that crossed on a larger scale and then wandered back below: the cloud-in-cloud problem.

</Step>

<Step title="The factor 2">

For a walk with independent steps, take any walk that has crossed $\delta_c$ before $S$ and reflect the part after its first crossing in the line $\bar\delta = \delta_c$. Reflection maps the walks that end below $\delta_c$ onto walks that end above it, with equal probability. So the walks that crossed and are now below are exactly as many as those now above, and the fraction that has crossed is twice the plain answer:

<Formula label="Excursion-set collapsed fraction" tex="F_\text{ES}(M, z) = 2 \times \frac12\,\text{erfc}\!\left(\frac{\delta_c}{\sqrt{2S}\,D(z)}\right) = \text{erfc}\!\left(\frac{\delta_c}{\sqrt2\,\sigma_R D(z)}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(B), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

As $M \to 0$ it goes to 1: every walk eventually crosses the barrier, and all the mass is in halos. The factor 2 that the notes put in by hand (reading 1, eq. 458, p. 97) now follows from the argument, for a sharp-$k$ window. This is the result of Bond, Cole, Efstathiou and Kaiser (1991) as it is usually stated.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

With a top-hat or Gaussian window in real space the steps are correlated, and the reflection argument is no longer exact, and the first-crossing fraction has to be found numerically. The formalism also gives more than the mass function: following the same walk to two barriers (two redshifts) gives the probability that a halo of mass $M_1$ at $z_1$ was part of a halo of mass $M_2$ at $z_2$, the merger histories of halos. That is why it is called "extended".

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
