## The G-dwarf problem

The previous page derived the closed-box model: a box of gas, with no inflow or outflow, turning into stars and enriching itself, with gas metallicity $Z = y\ln(1/u)$ for gas fraction $u$. **G dwarfs**, stars like the Sun, live longer than the age of the Galaxy, so every one ever formed is still on the main sequence. Their metallicities record the whole enrichment history of the gas, which makes them a clean test of the model.

The stellar mass formed while the gas fraction was still above $u$ is

<Formula label="Stars formed while the gas fraction was above u" tex="M_s(\geq u) = (1 - u)M \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{M_s(\geq u)}{M_{s,0}} = \frac{1 - u}{1 - u_0}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (254), p. 56" />

where the subscript 0 means today. Those stars formed from gas with metallicity at most $y\ln(1/u)$. Writing $u = e^{-Z/y}$ and $u_0 = e^{-Z_0/y}$:

<Formula label="Stellar mass with metallicity below Z" tex="\frac{M_s(\leq Z)}{M_{s,0}} = \frac{1 - e^{-Z/y}}{1 - u_0} = \frac{1 - u_0^{Z/Z_0}}{1 - u_0}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (255), p. 56" />

which is the fraction of stellar mass with metallicity at most $Z$:

<Formula label="Closed-box metallicity distribution" tex="f(\leq Z) = \frac{M_s(\leq Z)}{M_{s,0}} = \frac{1 - u_0^{Z/Z_0}}{1 - u_0}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (256), p. 56" />

The notes sketch the comparison: the closed box predicts a cumulative distribution that already contains a substantial fraction of stars at low metallicity, while the observed G dwarfs rise steeply only near solar metallicity. There are far too few metal-poor G dwarfs: the **G-dwarf problem**. The model is incomplete: real discs take in fresh gas over time, the IMF may vary, and so on.

## Active galactic nuclei

An **active galactic nucleus** (AGN) is the centre of a galaxy whose total light is dominated by radiation **not** produced by stars. Stars emit roughly black-body light in the near-ultraviolet, optical and near-infrared; AGN also shine strongly in radio and X-rays. The energy comes from a point-like source at the nucleus: a black hole of $10^6$ to $10^9\,M_\odot$. The main types, as listed in the notes:

- **Radio galaxies**: very luminous in radio, from two outer **lobes** powered by jets, in which electrons accelerated to about $10^{12}$ eV emit synchrotron radiation; about half are E/S0 galaxies and half quasars. [The notes give $L_\text{radio} \geq 10^{18}L_\odot$; that is about $4\times10^{51}$ erg/s, far more than any galaxy's total output, so it cannot be meant as a luminosity in solar units.]
- **Quasars and QSOs**: a quasar (quasi-stellar radio source) is an optical point source with a radio jet; a QSO (quasi-stellar object) looks the same but is radio quiet. They are one phenomenon: about 90% of optically found QSOs are radio quiet and 10% radio loud. They live mostly in elliptical galaxies, emit $10^{45}$ to $10^{48}$ erg/s, have jets from 0.1 pc to 1 Mpc long, and were most common at $z \approx 2$ to 3.
- **BL Lac objects**: quasars with enhanced continuum emission, highly variable, extremely luminous and highly polarized, because the jet points towards us.
- **Seyfert galaxies**: spiral galaxies with bright unresolved nuclei, less luminous than quasars, $10^{42}$ to $10^{45}$ erg/s.

## How big is the engine?

A source cannot change its brightness faster than light can cross it, so a variation on a timescale $\Delta t$ limits the size:

<Formula label="Size from variability" tex="\Delta t_\text{variable}\,c \sim r_\text{emission}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (257), p. 58" />

In the radio and optical, AGN vary over 1 to 10 days, so $r_\text{emission} \sim 10^{-3}$ to $10^{-2}$ pc; at TeV energies, over a day, $r_\text{emission} \sim 10^{-3}$ pc. Compare the **Schwarzschild radius** of a black hole:

<Formula label="Schwarzschild radius" tex="R_s = \frac{2GM_\text{BH}}{c^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (258), p. 58" />

which is about $10^{-7}$ pc for $10^6\,M_\odot$, $10^{-6}$ pc for $10^7\,M_\odot$ and $10^{-4}$ pc for $10^9\,M_\odot$ (eq. 259). The emitting regions are only about ten to a hundred thousand Schwarzschild radii across, depending on the black-hole mass: the variability comes from the immediate surroundings of a supermassive black hole.

**What could power it?** The notes rule out the alternatives:

- **Stars**: about $3\times10^8$ O stars (each about $10^{5.5}L_\odot$) would be needed in the central region, a density far too high to be stable.
- **Supernovae**: at $\sim10^{52}$ erg each, $10^{10}$ supernovae within $10^{-3}$ pc over $10^7$ years, which again requires impossibly many stars.
- **Accretion onto a supermassive black hole**: the only option left.

## Accretion and the Eddington limit

A supermassive black hole of $10^6$ to $10^{9.5}\,M_\odot$ accreting $10^{-4}$ to $10\,M_\odot$/yr converts gravitational energy into heat and radiation in its **accretion disc**, and also drives jets. The radiative efficiency is about $1/16$:

<Formula label="Accretion luminosity" tex="L_\text{acc} \approx \frac{1}{16}\dot mc^2" cite="reading1" at="eq. (260), p. 59" />

so 1 gram of matter yields about $10^6$ kWh, compared with an efficiency of only 0.007 for hydrogen burning. (For a non-rotating black hole the efficiency is about 5.7%, close to 1/16; a spinning one can do much better.)

There is a maximum luminosity. Radiation carries momentum: a photon of energy $E$ has momentum $E/c$, so a luminosity $L$ crossing a sphere of radius $r$ exerts a pressure

<Formula label="Photon momentum and radiation pressure" tex="P_\gamma = \frac Ec = \frac{h\nu}{c}, \qquad P_\text{total} = \frac{L}{4\pi r^2c}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (261)–(262), p. 59" />

on the plasma around the black hole. Electrons feel it through Thomson scattering (cross-section $\sigma_T$), and drag the protons with them; gravity pulls mainly on the protons. Accretion stops when radiation wins:

<Formula label="Radiation force on an electron exceeds gravity on a proton" tex="\frac{L}{4\pi r^2c}\,\sigma_T > \frac{GM_\text{BH} m_p}{r^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (263), p. 59" />

The distance cancels, giving the **Eddington luminosity**:

<Formula label="Eddington luminosity" tex="L_\text{Edd} = \frac{4\pi cGM_\text{BH} m_p}{\sigma_T} = 1.3\times10^{38}\,\frac{M_\text{BH}}{M_\odot}\ \text{erg/s}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (264), p. 59" />

Turned around, a quasar of $10^{46}$ erg/s needs a black hole of at least $10^8\,M_\odot$. Setting $L_\text{acc} = L_\text{Edd}$ gives the maximum, **Eddington accretion rate**:

<Formula label="Eddington accretion rate" tex="\frac{1}{16}\dot mc^2 = 1.3\times10^{38}\,\frac{M_\text{BH}}{M_\odot}\ \text{erg/s} \;\Rightarrow\; \dot m_\text{Edd} = \frac{16\,L_\text{Edd}}{c^2}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (265)–(266), p. 59" />

[The notes give $\dot m_\text{Edd} = 5\times10^{-10}\,(M_\text{BH}/M_\odot)\,M_\odot$/yr. Working it out with their efficiency of 1/16 gives $3.6\times10^{-8}\,(M_\text{BH}/M_\odot)\,M_\odot$/yr, about 70 times more; with the common choice of 10% efficiency it is $2.2\times10^{-8}$.]

The disc forms as gas spirals in from large distances down to the **innermost stable circular orbit**; viscosity in the disc heats it to about $10^8$ K, releasing energy very efficiently. [A thin accretion disc itself reaches about $10^5$ K; temperatures near $10^8$ K belong to the hot corona above it. Also, the notes call $P_\gamma = E/c$ in eq. (261) a pressure; it is the momentum of one photon, and the pressure is $P_\text{total}$.]

<WorkedExample title="Feeding a quasar">
<Problem>
A quasar has a black hole of $10^8\,M_\odot$. (a) What is its Eddington luminosity? (b) How much mass must it swallow per year to shine at that luminosity with efficiency 1/16? (1 solar mass $= 1.99\times10^{30}$ kg, 1 year $= 3.16\times10^7$ s.)
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Eddington luminosity">
$L_\text{Edd} = 1.3\times10^{38} \times 10^8 = 1.3\times10^{46}$ erg/s $= 1.3\times10^{39}$ W (more precisely $1.26\times10^{39}$ W, the value used below).
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Accretion rate">
$\dot m = 16L/c^2 = 16 \times 1.26\times10^{39}/(3\times10^8)^2 = 2.2\times10^{23}$ kg/s, which is about **3.6 solar masses per year**: the black hole doubles its mass in a few tens of millions of years at this rate.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The unified model

The different AGN types are thought to be one phenomenon seen from different directions and at different accretion rates. In the **unified model**:

- a supermassive black hole of $10^6$ to $10^{10}\,M_\odot$ sits at the centre;
- an **accretion disc** reaching out to about 100 to 1000 Schwarzschild radii emits X-ray, ultraviolet and optical radiation (the notes add TeV radiation; very-high-energy gamma rays come mainly from the jets);
- **jets** emit radio synchrotron radiation from electrons in strong magnetic fields;
- a **dusty torus**, from about 1 pc to 50 to 100 pc, absorbs light and re-emits it in the infrared;
- a **broad-line region** of dense gas clouds within about 0.1 to 1 pc moves fast, $v \sim 10^4$ km/s, giving broad emission lines;
- a **narrow-line region** of thinner clouds out to a few parsecs moves more slowly, 100 to 1000 km/s, giving narrower lines.

What we see depends on the viewing angle and the accretion rate. Looking straight down the jet, for example, gives a BL Lac object; looking through the torus hides the disc and the broad-line region.

## Summary

- Closed-box G dwarfs: $f(\leq Z) = (1 - u_0^{Z/Z_0})/(1 - u_0)$ predicts too many metal-poor long-lived stars, the **G-dwarf problem**; real galaxies are not closed boxes.
- **AGN** are galaxy centres whose light is not from stars: radio galaxies, quasars and QSOs, BL Lac objects and Seyferts.
- Variability over days limits the source to $\lesssim 10^{-3}$–$10^{-2}$ pc, near the Schwarzschild radius $2GM/c^2$ of a supermassive black hole. Stars and supernovae cannot supply the power; accretion can.
- Accretion converts about 1/16 of the rest mass into radiation, $L \approx \dot mc^2/16$, up to the **Eddington luminosity** $1.3\times10^{38}(M_\text{BH}/M_\odot)$ erg/s.
- In the **unified model**, a black hole, accretion disc, jets, dusty torus and broad- and narrow-line regions are seen differently depending on viewing angle and accretion rate.
