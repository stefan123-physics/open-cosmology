The questions follow MIT's Midterm Problem Set (Fall 2023) in our own words. MIT set it in place of a midterm exam, as an ordinary problem set; in MIT's course it came between Problem Sets 3 and 4, and it is numbered 7 here, after MIT's six numbered sets. MIT publishes no solutions, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

Several things differ from MIT's sheet. Its due date and its notes on how the set was used are left out. MIT asks for short written answers in several places; those parts are marked by you against our solution, and each question also has answer boxes that check a piece of it. Several boxes ask for numbers that MIT's sheet does not ask for (a disc near the Sun, the Milky Way's mass and size, a galaxy of a given mass): each says "our addition", and every number it needs is stated as "our choice". MIT's sheet writes the gas fraction of the closed box as $\mu$; we use the notes' letter $u$.

## How to answer

Work each question on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it; type decimals, for example `0.25` or `1.5e3`, in the unit the box names. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `sqrt(c*x/(d + y))^3`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `Sigma` stands for $\Sigma$, `Omega` for $\Omega$, `Z0` for $Z_0$, `pi` is π, `sqrt` the square root, `exp` the exponential and `log` the natural logarithm. A choice box takes one click.

The points are MIT's: 25 each for Questions 1 and 2, 15 each for Questions 3 and 4, and 5 for each of the four short answers, 100 points in all. Every split between parts is ours. Parts that ask you to explain or derive something have no box: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons. $G$ is the gravitational constant. In Question 1, $\Sigma$ is the surface density of the disc, $R$ its radius, $\Omega$ its angular speed, $\kappa$ the radial epicyclic frequency, $a_g$ and $a_c$ the gravitational and centrifugal accelerations at the edge, $\sigma$ a velocity dispersion and $\lambda_J$ the Jeans length. In Question 2, $Z$ is the metallicity (mass fraction of heavy elements), $y$ the yield, $u$ the gas fraction of the box, $Z_0$ and $u_0$ today's values, and $M_s$ the stellar mass. In Questions 3 and 4 and the short answers, $M$ is the mass of a galaxy, $R$ its radius, $N$ the number of bodies in it, $m$ the mass of one body, $v$ a typical speed, $n$ a number density, $T$ and $U$ the kinetic and potential energies, $\rho$ a density, $\phi$ the potential, $f$ the distribution function and $b$ an impact parameter.

## Question 1

<Exercise id="q1" title="A rotating gas disc: epicycles, collapse and why the Milky Way survives" points={25} mit="Question 1">

A gas disc has surface density $\Sigma$ and radius $R$ and rotates with angular speed $\Omega$. Follow a test particle at the edge of the disc. As in the notes, the mass of the disc, $\pi R^2\Sigma$, and the particle's angular momentum per unit mass, $\Omega R^2$, stay the same when the particle moves in or out a little (reading 6, p. 46).

<Part label="A" points={8}>

Find the square of the radial epicyclic frequency, $\kappa^2$, for nearly circular orbits at the edge $R$: the particle, displaced radially by a small $x$, obeys $\ddot x = -\kappa^2x$. Type $\kappa^2$ in the box, with `G`, `Sigma`, `Omega` and `R`.

<Hint>

Write the outward acceleration at a radius $R'$ near $R$ as the centrifugal term minus the gravitational one. The gravity of the disc at its edge: treat its mass as if it sat at the centre, as the notes do.

</Hint>

<Hint>

With the angular momentum fixed, the angular speed at $R'$ is $\Omega R^2/R'^2$, so the centrifugal acceleration is $\Omega^2R^4/R'^3$. Differentiate both terms with respect to $R'$ and set $R' = R$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="q1-kappa" label="$\kappa^2 =$" expr="3*Omega^2 - 2*pi*G*Sigma/R"
  vars={{ G: [0.5, 2], Sigma: [0.5, 2], Omega: [1.2, 3], R: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ Sigma: "\\Sigma", Omega: "\\Omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "4*Omega^2 - 2*pi*G*Sigma/R", hint: "That borrows the 4Ω² of the epicycle formula for a fixed rotation curve. Here the spin of the particle changes with its radius: differentiate Ω²R⁴/R'³ itself." },
    { expr: "3*Omega^2 - pi*G*Sigma/R", hint: "The gravitational acceleration falls as 1/R'². Its derivative brings down a factor 2." },
    { expr: "3*Omega^2 + 2*pi*G*Sigma/R", hint: "Check the sign of the gravity term: moving inwards makes gravity stronger, which pushes the particle further in." },
    { expr: "-Omega^2 - 2*pi*G*Sigma/R", hint: "That keeps Ω fixed as the particle moves. It is the angular momentum ΩR² that is conserved, so Ω grows as R' shrinks." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Move the particle from $R$ to a nearby $R'$ and compare how the outward centrifugal acceleration and the inward pull of gravity change: the difference is the restoring force per unit displacement.

<Step title="The two accelerations">

The disc's mass $\pi R^2\Sigma$ pulls the particle at $R'$ inwards with $a_g = G\pi R^2\Sigma/R'^2$ (reading 6, eq. 207, p. 46). Its angular momentum per unit mass is fixed, $\Omega R^2 = \Omega'R'^2$, so its centrifugal acceleration is $a_c = \Omega'^2R' = \Omega^2R^4/R'^3$ (eqs. 208 and 209).

</Step>

<Step title="The equation of motion">

The outward acceleration is $\ddot R' = a_c - a_g$. Put $R' = R + x$ and expand to first order in $x$:

<Formula label="Radial epicyclic frequency at the edge of the disc" tex="\ddot x = \left(\frac{da_c}{dR'} - \frac{da_g}{dR'}\right)_{R} x = \left(-3\Omega^2 + \frac{2\pi G\Sigma}{R}\right)x \;\Rightarrow\; \kappa^2 = 3\Omega^2 - \frac{2\pi G\Sigma}{R}" cite="mit" at="Question 1(A), p. 1 (our own solution, from eqs. (207)–(209) of reading 6)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Units: $G\Sigma$ is an acceleration (m/s²), so $G\Sigma/R$ is s⁻², like $\Omega^2$. The spin term is positive: moving in, the particle spins faster and is thrown back out. (Our remark: if the disc's own gravity were all that kept the particle in its orbit, $\Omega^2R = \pi G\Sigma$, then $\kappa^2 = \Omega^2$, positive at every $R$; this is also what the epicycle formula for a star in a fixed potential, $\kappa^2 = R\,d\Omega^2/dR + 4\Omega^2$ (reading 4, eq. 151, p. 35), gives around a point mass, where $\Omega^2 \propto R^{-3}$. The notes' argument, and MIT's question, treat $\Omega$ and $\Sigma$ as independent: the disc is a patch of a galaxy, carried round by the galaxy's gravity and spinning at $\Omega$.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="B" points={6}>

What is the minimum radius $R_\text{min}$ for which $\kappa^2 > 0$? Type it with `G`, `Sigma` and `Omega`. Then (our addition) evaluate it for a patch of cold gas near the Sun, with our choices $\Sigma = 10\,M_\odot$/pc² and $\Omega = 27.2$ km/s/kpc (the Sun's angular speed found on the [epicycle page](/courses/8-902/orbits-and-epicycles)). Give $R_\text{min}$ in pc (tolerance 2%).

<Hint>

Set $\kappa^2 = 0$ in part (A) and solve for $R$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In astronomical units, $G = 0.0043009$ pc (km/s)² $M_\odot^{-1}$ and $\Omega = 0.0272$ km/s per pc.

</Hint>

<Answer id="q1-rmin" label="$R_\text{min} =$" expr="2*pi*G*Sigma/(3*Omega^2)"
  vars={{ G: [0.5, 2], Sigma: [0.5, 2], Omega: [1.2, 3] }}
  names={{ Sigma: "\\Sigma", Omega: "\\Omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*pi*G*Sigma/(3*Omega^3)", hint: "That is the notes' eq. (211) as printed, with Ω³: check the units, or solve eq. (210) again." },
    { expr: "pi*G*Sigma/(2*Omega^2)", hint: "That follows from 4Ω² in part (A). Go back to the centrifugal term with conserved angular momentum." },
    { expr: "2*pi*G*Sigma/Omega^2", hint: "Check the factor 3: it comes from differentiating the centrifugal term Ω²R⁴/R'³." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="q1-rmin-pc" label="$R_\text{min}$ in pc:" value="122" tolerance="2%" calc="q1-rmin-pc" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The gravity term in $\kappa^2$ grows as the disc gets smaller, the rotation term does not: below some radius gravity wins.

<Step title="The radius">

$\kappa^2 > 0$ when $3\Omega^2 > 2\pi G\Sigma/R$:

<Formula label="Smallest radius stabilised by rotation" tex="R > R_\text{min} = \frac{2\pi G\Sigma}{3\Omega^2}" cite="reading6" at="eqs. (210)–(211), p. 46" />

This is the notes' $R_\text{rot}$. (The notes print $\Omega^3$ in eq. (211); eq. (210) and the units show it is $\Omega^2$.)

</Step>

<Step title="Our addition: a patch near the Sun">

$G\Sigma = 0.0043009 \times 10 = 0.043009$ (km/s)²/pc and $3\Omega^2 = 3 \times 0.0272^2 = 2.2195 \times 10^{-3}$ (km/s/pc)², so $R_\text{min} = 2\pi \times 0.043009/2.2195 \times 10^{-3}$ pc $= 122$ pc.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At $R = R_\text{min}$ the two terms cancel and $\kappa^2 = 0$. A larger $\Sigma$ (more mass) or a slower spin makes $R_\text{min}$ larger: more of the small scales are then left without the protection of rotation.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="C" points={4}>

What happens if $R < R_\text{min}$? Choose the best description.

<Answer id="q1-collapse" label="If R < R_min:" choices={["the orbit only becomes a wider epicycle around R, because a negative κ² changes the frequency but not the bounded motion", "a small inward displacement grows exponentially: the patch keeps contracting and collapses into a dense clump", "the particle is thrown outwards, because the spin-up of the contracting patch now exceeds its gravity", "nothing changes, because conserving the angular momentum alone keeps a circular orbit stable at every radius"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With $\kappa^2 < 0$ the equation $\ddot x = -\kappa^2x = |\kappa^2|\,x$ has growing solutions, $x \propto e^{|\kappa|t}$.

<Step title="What it means">

A patch smaller than $R_\text{min}$ that is squeezed a little gains more inward pull from gravity than outward push from its spin-up, so it keeps contracting: rotation cannot stop it, and the gas collapses into a dense clump. The disc fragments on those scales. This is the large-scale half of the Toomre argument (reading 6, p. 46).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The growth time is $1/|\kappa|$; at $R = R_\text{min}/2$, for example, $\kappa^2 = 3\Omega^2 - 6\Omega^2 = -3\Omega^2$, so a perturbation grows by a factor $e$ in $1/(\sqrt3\,\Omega)$, a fraction of a rotation period.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="D" points={7}>

Why does this not happen all the time in the Milky Way for disc structures smaller than $R_\text{min}$? Answer in a few sentences first, then choose the best reason in the box. Then (our addition) evaluate the notes' Jeans length of a thin disc, $\lambda_J = \frac{\pi}{8}\,\sigma^2/(G\Sigma)$, for the gas of part (B) with our choice of velocity dispersion $\sigma = 8$ km/s, in pc (tolerance 2%).

<Hint>

Rotation is not the only thing that resists collapse. What does the Jeans criterion say about small scales?

</Hint>

<Answer id="q1-why" label="The main reason:" choices={["the disc spins faster than Ω on small scales, so the spin-up of a small patch always beats its gravity", "the dark matter halo adds to the disc's Σ, which makes R_min smaller than any real patch", "random motions of gas and stars resist collapse below the Jeans length, which is larger than R_min", "small patches pass their angular momentum to the rest of the disc faster than they can contract"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="q1-lamj" label="$\lambda_J$ in pc:" value="584" tolerance="2%" calc="q1-lamj" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Rotation protects the large scales and random motions protect the small ones; a disc is unstable only on scales between the two, and in the Milky Way that range is empty.

<Step title="The reason">

Our test particle had no random motion. Real gas has a sound speed and turbulent motions, and stars have a velocity dispersion. They act as a pressure: a region smaller than the Jeans length is crossed by these motions faster than it can fall in, and it bounces back (reading 6, p. 45). So small scales, $R < \lambda_J$, are stable for this reason, and large scales, $R > R_\text{min}$, are stable because of rotation. The disc is unstable only if $\lambda_J < R < R_\text{min}$, and it is stable on all scales when $\lambda_J \geq R_\text{min}$, which is Toomre's criterion $Q > 1$ (reading 6, eqs. 212 and 217, p. 47). The Milky Way's disc has $Q$ somewhat above 1, so the dangerous range does not exist.

</Step>

<Step title="Our addition: the numbers">

<Formula label="Jeans length of a thin disc" tex="\lambda_J = \frac{\pi}{8}\,\frac{\sigma^2}{G\Sigma}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (212), p. 47" />

$\lambda_J = (\pi/8) \times 64/0.043009$ pc $= 584$ pc, larger than $R_\text{min} = 122$ pc: the gas near the Sun is stable on all scales.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The same comparison in the notes' form: $\sigma_\text{crit} = \frac{4}{\sqrt3}\,G\Sigma/\Omega = 4 \times 0.043009/(1.7321 \times 0.0272)$ km/s $= 3.65$ km/s, and $\sigma/\sigma_\text{crit} = 2.2 > 1$. Equivalently $\lambda_J/R_\text{min} = (\sigma/\sigma_\text{crit})^2 = 4.8$. Where the gas is denser or colder, in giant molecular clouds and spiral arms, $Q$ drops locally and gas does collapse: that is where stars form.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Question 2

<Exercise id="q2" title="The metallicity distribution of stars in the closed box" points={25} mit="Question 2">

In the closed-box model of chemical evolution, with a constant yield $y$ and the instantaneous recycling approximation, the metallicity of the gas depends only on the gas fraction $u$ of the box (the fraction of the total mass of stars plus gas that is in gas):

<Formula label="Gas metallicity in the closed box" tex="Z = -y\ln u" cite="mit" at="Question 2, p. 1" />

Here we look at the metallicities of all the stars the box has made.

<Part label="A" points={13}>

Derive, and explain in two or three sentences, why the fraction of the stellar mass with metallicity below $Z$ is

<Formula label="Cumulative metallicity distribution of the stars" tex="M_s(<Z) = \frac{1 - e^{-Z/y}}{1 - e^{-Z_0/y}}" cite="mit" at="Question 2(A), p. 1" />

where $Z_0$ is today's metallicity of the gas. Mark yourself: MIT gave full marks only with the explanation, and half for the formula alone.

<Hint>

The gas metallicity only grows as the gas is used up. Which stars have a metallicity below $Z$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

In a closed box the total mass $M$ stays fixed, so the stellar mass made by the time the gas fraction has fallen to $u$ is $(1 - u)M$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** In a closed box the gas gets richer in metals all the time, so the metallicity of a star tells when it formed: the stars below $Z$ are exactly those made before the gas reached $Z$.

<Step title="The derivation">

Each star keeps the metallicity of the gas it formed from. With instantaneous recycling the stellar mass grows as the gas is used, $M_s = M - M_g = (1 - u)M$, and $Z = -y\ln u$ increases steadily as $u$ falls. So the stars with metallicity below $Z$ formed while the gas fraction was above $u = e^{-Z/y}$, and their mass is $(1 - e^{-Z/y})M$. Today the gas fraction is $u_0 = e^{-Z_0/y}$ and all the stars together have the mass $(1 - u_0)M$. Dividing gives MIT's formula:

<Formula label="Stellar mass below Z as a fraction of all stars" tex="\frac{M_s(<Z)}{M_{s,0}} = \frac{(1 - e^{-Z/y})M}{(1 - e^{-Z_0/y})M} = \frac{1 - e^{-Z/y}}{1 - e^{-Z_0/y}}" cite="reading8" at="eqs. (254)–(256), p. 56" />

</Step>

<Step title="The explanation, in three sentences">

In a closed box the gas metallicity rises monotonically as gas turns into stars, $Z = y\ln(1/u)$, so a star's metallicity is a clock for when it formed. The stars with metallicity below $Z$ are therefore all the stars formed while the gas fraction was still above $e^{-Z/y}$, which have mass $M(1 - e^{-Z/y})$ because the total mass is conserved. Dividing by the mass of all stars made until today, $M(1 - e^{-Z_0/y})$, gives the fraction.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At $Z = 0$ the fraction is 0, and at $Z = Z_0$ it is 1: no star is richer than today's gas. For $Z \ll y$ the fraction is $\approx (Z/y)/(1 - u_0)$, a straight line: the box makes many stars of low metallicity, the root of the G-dwarf problem ([the G-dwarf lesson](/courses/8-902/g-dwarf-problem-agn)).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="B" points={12}>

Observers measure metallicities on a logarithmic scale. Find the differential distribution $dM_s/d(\ln Z)$, with $M_s$ the fraction of part (A) (our choice of logarithm: the natural one), and type it with `Z`, `y` and `Z0`. Sketch it. At what $Z$ does it peak? Assume (our assumption) that $Z_0 > y$, which holds for a gas fraction today below $1/e$. Then (our addition) give the peak metallicity as a fraction of $Z_0$ for a box with $u_0 = 0.1$ today (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

$dM_s/d\ln Z = Z\,dM_s/dZ$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write the distribution as a function of $x = Z/y$ and set its derivative with respect to $x$ to zero.

</Hint>

<Answer id="q2-dist" label="$dM_s/d\ln Z =$" expr="(Z/y)*exp(-Z/y)/(1 - exp(-Z0/y))"
  vars={{ Z: [0.002, 0.02], y: [0.005, 0.02], Z0: [0.025, 0.05] }}
  names={{ Z0: "Z_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(-Z/y)/(y*(1 - exp(-Z0/y)))", hint: "That is dM_s/dZ. On a logarithmic scale, multiply by Z." },
    { expr: "(Z/y)*exp(-Z/y)", hint: "Keep the normalisation: M_s of part (A) is a fraction of today's stellar mass." },
    { expr: "log(10)*(Z/y)*exp(-Z/y)/(1 - exp(-Z0/y))", hint: "That is per unit of log₁₀ Z. The box asks for the natural logarithm." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="q2-peak" label="$Z_\text{peak} =$" expr="y"
  vars={{ y: [0.005, 0.02], Z0: [0.025, 0.05] }}
  names={{ Z0: "Z_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Z0", hint: "The distribution reaches Z₀ but does not peak there when Z₀ > y: find where its derivative vanishes." },
    { expr: "y*log(10)", hint: "The logarithm only scales the height of the curve: per unit of ln Z or of log₁₀ Z, the peak is at the same Z." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="q2-peak-ratio" label="$Z_\text{peak}/Z_0$ for $u_0 = 0.1$:" value="0.434" tolerance="1%" calc="q2-peak-ratio" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On a logarithmic axis the distribution is the cumulative fraction's slope times $Z$; the box's yield sets where it peaks.

<Step title="The distribution">

Differentiating the result of part (A):

<Formula label="Differential metallicity distribution" tex="\frac{dM_s}{d\ln Z} = Z\,\frac{dM_s}{dZ} = \frac{(Z/y)\,e^{-Z/y}}{1 - e^{-Z_0/y}}, \qquad 0 < Z \leq Z_0" cite="mit" at="Question 2(B), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

It rises in proportion to $Z$ at low metallicity, peaks, and falls off exponentially; it ends abruptly at $Z_0$, where star formation has got to today.

</Step>

<Step title="The peak">

$\frac{d}{dZ}\left[(Z/y)e^{-Z/y}\right] = \frac1y\,e^{-Z/y}(1 - Z/y) = 0$ at $Z = y$. So the distribution peaks at the yield, $Z_\text{peak} = y$, as long as $Z_0 > y$; otherwise it keeps rising up to $Z_0$ and peaks there, at the edge. Per unit of $\log_{10}Z$ the curve is $\ln 10$ times higher everywhere, with the peak at the same $Z$.

</Step>

<Step title="Our addition: the numbers">

$u_0 = e^{-Z_0/y} = 0.1$ gives $Z_0 = y\ln 10$, so $Z_\text{peak}/Z_0 = y/Z_0 = 1/\ln 10 = 0.434$.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-2, 0]} range={[0, 1]} xLabel="log₁₀ (Z/Z₀)" yLabel="dM_s / d(log₁₀ Z)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "log(10)*(10^x*log(10))*exp(-10^x*log(10))/0.9", label: "u₀ = 0.1" }
  ]}
  caption="Computed from the formula above with u₀ = 0.1, so Z₀ = y ln 10, per unit of log₁₀ Z. The curve peaks at Z = y = 0.434 Z₀, at a height of ln 10 × e⁻¹/0.9 = 0.94, and stops at today's gas metallicity Z₀." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The area under $dM_s/d\ln Z$ from $\ln Z \to -\infty$ to $\ln Z_0$ is $M_s(<Z_0) = 1$, as it must be. The closed box puts the peak of the stars' metallicities at the yield, with a long tail of metal-poor stars to the left: the tail that the G dwarfs near the Sun do not show.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Question 3

<Exercise id="q3" title="Relaxation in a galaxy made of globular clusters" points={15} mit="Question 3">

Two galaxies have the same mass and the same size. One is made of individual stars; the other of globular clusters of $10^6$ solar masses each. Taking only two-body relaxation into account, which galaxy relaxes faster? For numbers (our choice) take a galaxy mass $M = 10^{11}M_\odot$ and stars of one solar mass, and use the notes' estimate $t_\text{relax}/t_\text{cross} \approx N/(6\ln N)$, with $N$ the number of bodies.

<Part label="A" points={10}>

Find the ratio of the relaxation time of the cluster galaxy to that of the star galaxy, in units of $10^{-6}$ (tolerance 1%), and (our addition) the relaxation time of the cluster galaxy in crossing times (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

The two galaxies have the same mass and size, so by the virial theorem the same speeds and the same crossing time.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$N_* = 10^{11}$ stars against $N_c = 10^5$ clusters.

</Hint>

<Answer id="q3-ratio" label="Cluster galaxy over star galaxy, in $10^{-6}$:" value="2.20" tolerance="1%" calc="q3-ratio" />

<Answer id="q3-ncross" label="Cluster galaxy, in crossing times:" value="1448" tolerance="1%" calc="q3-ncross" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a fixed mass and size the crossing time is the same, and the relaxation time in crossing times grows almost in proportion to the number of bodies: fewer, heavier bodies relax the system faster.

<Step title="The relaxation times">

<Formula label="Relaxation time in crossing times" tex="\frac{t_\text{relax}}{t_\text{cross}} \approx \frac{N}{6\ln N}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (74), p. 19" />

The star galaxy has $N_* = 10^{11}$, the cluster galaxy $N_c = 10^{11}/10^6 = 10^5$, and $t_\text{cross} = R/v$ is the same for both. So

<Formula label="Ratio of the relaxation times" tex="\frac{t_\text{relax}(\text{clusters})}{t_\text{relax}(\text{stars})} = \frac{N_c/\ln N_c}{N_*/\ln N_*} = 10^{-6}\,\frac{\ln 10^{11}}{\ln 10^5} = 10^{-6} \times \frac{11}{5} = 2.2\times10^{-6}" cite="mit" at="Question 3, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The cluster galaxy relaxes about $4.5 \times 10^5$ times faster. It needs $10^5/(6\ln 10^5) = 10^5/69.1 = 1448$ crossing times, against $6.6 \times 10^8$ for the stars.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

In years (our addition): with the speed of Question 4, $v = 207$ km/s, and $R = 10$ kpc, $t_\text{cross} = 47$ Myr, so the cluster galaxy relaxes in about 68 billion years and the star galaxy in $3 \times 10^{16}$ years. Even made of clusters, such a galaxy would not be relaxed by two-body encounters within the age of the universe (68 billion years is about five times it), though it gets much closer than a galaxy of stars, which never does.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="B" points={5}>

Explain in a few sentences why the result turns out this way, and choose the best reason.

<Answer id="q3-why" label="The cluster galaxy relaxes faster because" choices={["each velocity kick grows with the mass of the passing body, so the summed squared kicks, n m² = ρ m, grow at fixed ρ", "heavier bodies move faster in the same potential, so they cross the galaxy more often and meet more partners per year", "clusters are extended objects, so they collide physically, while stars are points that never touch one another", "a galaxy of clusters has a larger Coulomb logarithm, and the relaxation time is proportional to that logarithm"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Relaxation is the random walk of a body's velocity under many small kicks; the kick from one encounter grows with the mass of the body that passes.

<Step title="The reason">

One encounter at impact parameter $b$ changes a velocity by $\delta v = 2Gm/(bv)$ (reading 2, eq. 61, p. 17), proportional to the mass $m$ of the passing body. The kicks add in quadrature, so the squared velocity change per unit time is proportional to $n m^2 \ln\Lambda$ (eq. 69, p. 18). At a fixed mass density $\rho = nm$ this is $\rho\,m\ln\Lambda$: replacing stars by clusters $10^6$ times heavier, with $10^6$ times fewer of them, makes each kick $10^6$ times larger, and the sum of the squared kicks $10^6$ times larger too. The smooth potential, and so the speeds and the crossing time, do not change. The weak dependence on $\ln N$ shifts the factor slightly, from $10^{-6}$ to $2.2 \times 10^{-6}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The second choice is wrong because the virial speed $\sqrt{GM/R}$ does not depend on the mass of the bodies; the third because two-body relaxation, as defined here, is gravitational deflection, not contact; the fourth because $\ln N$ is smaller for the clusters (11.5 against 25.3), and $t_\text{relax}$ is inversely proportional to it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

### A. The Navarro–Frenk–White profile

<Exercise id="sa" title="What is the Navarro–Frenk–White (NFW) profile?" points={5} mit="Short answer A">

<Part label="a" points={2}>

(Our addition.) The NFW density falls as a power of $r$ well inside and well outside its scale radius $a$. Choose the two power laws, and give the logarithmic slope $d\ln\rho/d\ln r$ exactly at $r = a$ (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

$\ln\rho = \ln\rho_0 - \ln(r/a) - 2\ln(1 + r/a)$: differentiate with respect to $\ln r$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="sa-slopes" label="Inside and outside the scale radius:" choices={["ρ ∝ r⁻² everywhere, as for an isothermal sphere", "ρ flat (a core) at small r and ρ ∝ r⁻³ at large r", "ρ ∝ r⁻¹ at small r and ρ ∝ r⁻² far out", "ρ ∝ r⁻¹ at small r and ρ ∝ r⁻³ at large r"]} answer={3} />

<Answer id="sa-slope" label="$d\ln\rho/d\ln r$ at $r = a$:" value="-2" tolerance="1%" calc="sa-slope" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The profile is the product of two power laws joined at the scale radius.

<Step title="The slopes">

<Formula label="NFW profile" tex="\rho(r) = \frac{\rho_0}{\left(\frac{r}{a}\right)\left(1 + \frac{r}{a}\right)^2}" cite="reading3" at="eq. (99), p. 25" />

$d\ln\rho/d\ln r = -1 - 2(r/a)/(1 + r/a) = -(1 + 3r/a)/(1 + r/a)$: $-1$ for $r \ll a$, $-3$ for $r \gg a$, and $-4/2 = -2$ at $r = a$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At $r = a$ the density is $\rho_0/4$, and the local slope $-2$ is that of an isothermal sphere. The rotation curve, which depends on all the mass inside $r$, peaks a little further out, at about $2.16\,a$, and at $r = a$ its speed is already only about 5.5 per cent below that peak: near the scale radius an NFW halo's rotation curve is nearly flat.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Now answer MIT's question in a few sentences: what is the NFW profile, where does it come from, and why does it matter? Mark yourself.

<Solution>

**The idea.** A good answer gives the formula, its two slopes, its origin in simulations and one consequence.

<Step title="A model answer">

The NFW profile is the density profile that simulations of cold dark matter find for halos of all masses: $\rho = \rho_0/[(r/a)(1 + r/a)^2]$, with a central density scale $\rho_0$ and a scale radius $a$ (reading 3, eq. 99, p. 25). It is empirical, a fit to simulated halos rather than a solution of an equation. It has a central cusp, $\rho \propto r^{-1}$, and falls as $r^{-3}$ outside the scale radius, so its mass grows logarithmically and has to be cut off, usually at the virial radius. It matters because it is the standard model of the dark matter halos around galaxies and clusters, used to fit rotation curves and lensing; observations of some small galaxies prefer a flatter core, the cusp–core problem. A sketch: $\log\rho$ against $\log r$, a straight line of slope $-1$ bending to slope $-3$ around $r = a$.

</Step>

</Solution>

### B. Violent relaxation

<Exercise id="sb" title="What is violent relaxation? How does it work? Why is it important?" points={5} mit="Short answer B">

<Part label="a" points={2}>

(Our addition.) Choose what changes a star's energy during violent relaxation, and on what time scale it acts.

<Hint>

Look at how the energy per unit mass $E = \frac12v^2 + \phi$ of a star changes along its orbit.

</Hint>

<Answer id="sb-cause" label="A star's energy changes because" choices={["close encounters with single neighbouring stars give it a long series of small velocity kicks", "the collective potential changes with time, which changes every star's energy per unit mass alike", "dynamical friction drags the star towards the centre of the forming galaxy", "radiation from young stars heats the gas, and the gas then pulls on the star"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="sb-time" label="It acts on about" choices={["a free-fall (dynamical) time of the collapsing system", "the two-body relaxation time, N/(6 ln N) crossing times", "the age of the universe, whatever the system", "the main-sequence lifetime of the stars involved"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In a collisionless system only a changing potential can change a star's energy.

<Step title="The cause and the time">

Along an orbit, $dE/dt = \partial\phi/\partial t$ (reading 2, eq. 76, p. 20): in a static potential the energy is conserved. During the collapse of a galaxy or halo the potential changes quickly, so the energies change, on the time scale on which the potential changes, the free-fall time (eq. 77, p. 21).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The change per unit mass, $\partial\phi/\partial t$, does not depend on the star's mass, so violent relaxation treats stars of all masses alike: unlike two-body relaxation, it does not sort them by mass (our remark, from eq. 76).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Now answer MIT's question in a few sentences. Mark yourself.

<Solution>

**The idea.** A good answer says what it is, its mechanism and why it is needed.

<Step title="A model answer">

Violent relaxation is the way a collisionless system of stars or dark matter settles into equilibrium after it collapses. Since two-body encounters are far too slow (Question 3), stars can only exchange energy through the collective potential: a star's energy changes only if the potential changes with time, $dE/dt = \partial\phi/\partial t$ (reading 2, eq. 76, p. 20). In a collapse the potential changes strongly within a free-fall time; a star crossing a deepening well loses energy, one crossing a shallowing well gains it, and the energies are reshuffled until the potential stops changing (eq. 77, p. 21). It is important because it explains why galaxies and dark matter halos are relaxed, with smooth, similar profiles, although they are far too young for two-body relaxation, and because it acts equally on all masses, so it does not segregate heavy stars. A sketch: a star crossing a potential well whose depth changes while the star is inside.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

### C. The collisionless Boltzmann equation and the Jeans equations

<Exercise id="sc" title="The collisionless Boltzmann equation, and how to get the Jeans equations from it" points={5} mit="Short answer C">

<Part label="a" points={2}>

(Our addition.) Choose how the Jeans equation follows from the collisionless Boltzmann equation, and why it cannot be solved on its own.

<Hint>

The density, mean velocity and dispersion are integrals of $f$ over velocity, weighted by $1$, $v_i$ and $v_iv_j$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="sc-how" label="The Jeans equation follows by" choices={["integrating the equation over all positions, which leaves the evolution of the velocity distribution", "multiplying the equation by f and integrating over all of phase space", "multiplying the equation by a velocity component v_j and integrating over all velocities", "setting ∂f/∂t = 0 and solving for f as a function of the energy"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="sc-closure" label="It cannot be solved alone because" choices={["it contains the unknown dispersions σ²ᵢⱼ: more unknowns than equations", "it is nonlinear in the potential φ, which has no solution in closed form", "it holds only for systems in which collisions between stars are frequent", "it needs the full distribution function f as an input at every time"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Taking velocity moments of the equation turns one equation in six dimensions into fluid-like equations in three.

<Step title="The moments">

The zeroth moment (integrate over velocity; the notes count this one as the first) gives the continuity equation; the first (multiply by $v_j$, then integrate) gives the Jeans equation, which contains $n$, $\bar v_j$ and the dispersions $\sigma_{ij}^2$ (reading 5, eqs. 173–181, pp. 39–41).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Four equations (continuity and three Jeans equations) cannot fix the density, three mean velocities and six independent dispersions: one needs an assumption, such as isotropy or a given anisotropy $\beta$, to close the system (reading 5, pp. 41–42).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Now write down the collisionless Boltzmann equation and explain in a few sentences how the Jeans equation follows from it. Mark yourself.

<Solution>

**The idea.** A good answer writes the equation with its meaning and names the steps of the moment method.

<Step title="A model answer">

For a distribution function $f(\vec x, \vec v, t)$ of stars moving in a smooth potential $\phi$, with no encounters, the number of stars in a phase-space volume moving with the flow is conserved:

<Formula label="Collisionless Boltzmann equation" tex="\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \vec v\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial\vec x} - \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\vec x}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial\vec v} = 0" cite="reading5" at="eq. (169), p. 38" />

It is the continuity equation of $f$ in six-dimensional phase space. Multiplying by $v_j$, integrating over all velocities (the term with $\partial f/\partial\vec v$ is integrated by parts, and $f$ vanishes at infinite speed) and subtracting $\bar v_j$ times the continuity equation gives the Jeans equation

<Formula label="The Jeans equation" tex="\frac{\partial\bar v_j}{\partial t} + \sum_i\bar v_i\frac{\partial\bar v_j}{\partial x_i} = -\frac1n\sum_i\frac{\partial\left(n\sigma_{ij}^2\right)}{\partial x_i} - \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_j}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (181), p. 41" />

the analogue of the Euler equation, with the "pressure" $n\sigma_{ij}^2$ of the random motions. It needs a closure assumption, and it is used to weigh galaxies from measured dispersions.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

### D. Strong encounters

<Exercise id="sd" title="How likely is a strong encounter between two stars in the Milky Way?" points={5} mit="Short answer D">

Give the impact parameter $b_\text{strong}$ for a strong encounter in AU (tolerance 2%), and the expected number of strong encounters of one star in 10 billion years, in units of $10^{-9}$ (tolerance 2%).

<Hint>

An encounter is strong when its kick $2Gm/(bv)$ is as large as $v$ itself. With $v^2 = GNm/R$ that gives $b$ in terms of $R$ and $N$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The notes find the chance of a strong encounter per crossing of the galaxy, $3/N$. How many crossings of time $R/v$ fit in 10 billion years?

</Hint>

**The idea.** A strong encounter needs a near miss; the target area is tiny compared with the space between stars.

<Step title="The impact parameter">

<Formula label="Strong-encounter impact parameter" tex="\frac{2Gm}{b\,v} \sim v \;\Rightarrow\; b_\text{strong} = \frac{2Gm}{v^2} = \frac{2R}{N}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (62)–(64), p. 17" />

</Step>

</Step>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Now answer MIT's question in a few sentences. Mark yourself.

<Solution>

**The idea.** A good answer defines a strong encounter, estimates its probability and draws the conclusion.

<Step title="A model answer">

An encounter is strong when it changes the star's velocity by about as much as the velocity itself, which needs an impact parameter below $b_\text{strong} = 2Gm/v^2 \approx 2R/N$ (reading 2, eqs. 62–64, p. 17).
