The Friedmann equations with matter, radiation, curvature and a cosmological constant describe the expansion well. But are they enough to explain everything we see? The 8.902 Lecture 12 notes (section 1.D) list four problems, show that one idea solves them all, and sketch how a scalar field can do it. The 8.286 pages treat inflation at much greater length, from Guth's own perspective; this page follows the shorter route of 8.902, ending with the notes' summary of the whole cosmological story (section 1.E).

## Recombination and the CMB

The horizon problem starts with the CMB. Radiation density scales as $(1+z)^4$ and as $T^4$, so

<Formula label="Temperature and redshift" tex="\rho_r \propto (1 + z)^4 \quad\text{and}\quad \rho_r \propto T^4 \;\Rightarrow\; T \propto (1 + z)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (345), p. 75" />

As the universe cools, protons and electrons combine into neutral hydrogen (**recombination**), in a balance

<Formula label="Recombination balance" tex="H^+ + e^- \;\rightleftharpoons\; H^0 + \chi" cite="reading1" at="eq. (346), p. 75" />

with ionization energy $\chi = 13.6$ eV. The state of the gas is described by the ionization fraction $x$ (free electrons per proton) and the baryon-to-photon ratio $\eta$:

<Formula label="Ionization fraction and baryon-to-photon ratio" tex="x = \frac{n_e}{n_p}, \qquad \eta = \frac{n_b}{n_\gamma}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (347), p. 75" />

[The notes add $\eta \approx 5\times10^{-10}\,(\Omega_{b,0}h^2/0.01)$; the usual relation is $\eta \approx 2.7\times10^{-10}\,(\Omega_{b,0}h^2/0.01)$, which gives $6.1\times10^{-10}$ for today's $\Omega_{b,0}h^2 = 0.0224$.] In equilibrium the **Saha equation** relates them:

<Formula label="Saha equation for hydrogen" tex="\frac{1 - x}{x^2} \approx 3.84\,\eta\left(\frac{k_BT}{m_ec^2}\right)^{3/2} e^{\chi/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (348), p. 76" />

[The notes print the exponent with a minus sign; it must be $+\chi/k_BT$, so that the gas becomes neutral, $1 - x$ large, as the temperature falls.] Since 13.6 eV corresponds to about $10^5$ K, one might expect hydrogen to be neutral below $10^5$ K. But the factor $\eta \sim 10^{-9}$, the enormous number of photons per baryon, keeps the gas ionized until $T \approx 3000$ K: recombination happens at $z_\text{recomb} \approx 1090$ (the notes use $\Omega_{b,0} = 0.045$ and $T_0 = 2.73$ K). After that the photons stream freely, and we see them as the **cosmic microwave background**, uniform to $\Delta T/T \lesssim 10^{-5}$.

## The horizon problem

A **horizon** is the largest region connected by light since the Big Bang. Its comoving size follows from $c\,dt = a\,d\chi$:

<Formula label="Comoving horizon" tex="\chi_\text{horizon} = \int_0^t\frac{c\,dt}{a(t)} = \int_0^{a = (1+z)^{-1}}\frac{c\,da}{a^2H(a)}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (349)–(350), p. 76" />

and its physical size is $l_\text{horizon} = a\,\chi_\text{horizon}$. For a flat universe dominated by radiation or by matter:

<Formula label="Physical horizon size" tex="\text{radiation:}\ l_\text{horizon} = \frac{c}{H(z)} = \frac{c}{H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{r,0}}}\,\frac{1}{(1 + z)^2}, \qquad \text{matter:}\ l_\text{horizon} \approx \frac{2c}{H(z)} = \frac{2c}{H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}}}\,\frac{1}{(1 + z)^{3/2}}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (351)–(352), p. 76" />

[The notes write $1/(1+z)$ in the radiation case; with $H = H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{r,0}}(1+z)^2$ it is $1/(1+z)^2$.] In the matter case the brief radiation era at the start adds little. The notes treat recombination as matter-dominated, estimating $\rho_r/\rho_m \sim 5\times10^{-2}$ there. [In fact $\Omega_{r,0}(1 + z)/\Omega_{m,0} \approx 9.2\times10^{-5} \times 1091/0.315 \approx 0.3$ at $z = 1090$: radiation is still about a quarter of the total, so the matter formula is only a rough approximation.]

<Formula label="Horizon at recombination" tex="l_\text{horizon} = \frac{2c}{H_0}\,\Omega_{m,0}^{-1/2}(1 + z)^{-3/2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (353), p. 76" />

To see how big that looks today, divide by the angular diameter distance (previous page). For a flat matter universe at large $z$:

<Formula label="Angular size of the horizon" tex="\varphi_\text{horizon} = \frac{l_\text{horizon}}{D_A}, \qquad D_A = \frac{2c}{H_0(1 + z)\,\Omega_{m,0}^{1/2}}\left[1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + z}}\right] \approx \frac{2c}{H_0\,\Omega_{m,0}^{1/2}}\,\frac1z" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (354)–(355), pp. 76–77" />

Dividing, the factors $2c/(H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}})$ cancel and

<Formula label="Horizon angle at recombination" tex="\varphi_\text{horizon,recomb} \approx \sqrt{\frac{1}{z_\text{recomb}}} \sim 1.7^\circ" cite="reading1" at="eq. (356), p. 77" />

The notes add the more general form $1.7^\circ\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}}$ (eq. 357), which the CMB page also uses. [In the flat derivation above $\Omega_{m,0}$ cancels; the $\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}}$ form belongs to an open universe containing only matter, where the curvature shrinks the angle.] Either way, 1.7° is tiny compared with the whole sky. Regions on opposite sides of the sky were never in causal contact, so **how can the CMB have the same temperature everywhere?**

## The flatness problem

At high redshift $\Lambda$ is irrelevant, and the Friedmann equation with $\rho = \rho_m + \rho_r$ reads

<Formula label="Friedmann equation in terms of Ω" tex="H^2(a) = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho - \frac{kc^2}{a^2} = H^2(a)\left[\Omega(a) - \frac{kc^2}{a^2H^2(a)}\right]" cite="reading1" at="eq. (358), p. 77" />

so the departure from flatness is

<Formula label="Departure from flatness" tex="|\Omega(a) - 1| = \frac{|k|c^2}{a^2H^2(a)}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (359), p. 77" />

With $a \propto t^{1/2}$ and $H \propto 1/t$ in the radiation era, $a^2H^2 \propto 1/t$; with $a \propto t^{2/3}$ in the matter era, $a^2H^2 \propto t^{-2/3}$:

<Formula label="Growth of the departure from flatness" tex="|\Omega(t) - 1| \propto \begin{cases} t, & \text{radiation dominated} \\ t^{2/3}, & \text{matter dominated} \end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (360), p. 77" />

Any small departure from $\Omega = 1$ at early times grows rapidly. Since $\Omega$ is close to 1 today, it must have been extraordinarily close to 1 early on: a **fine-tuning problem**.

<WorkedExample title="How flat at one second?">
<Problem>
Suppose $|\Omega - 1| < 0.01$ today, about 13.8 billion years ($4.4\times10^{17}$ s) after the Big Bang. Ignoring $\Lambda$, use the scalings above to estimate how close to 1 the density parameter had to be at $t = 1$ s. Take matter–radiation equality at about 50,000 years ($1.6\times10^{12}$ s).
</Problem>
<Step title="Back through the matter era">
From $4.4\times10^{17}$ s to $1.6\times10^{12}$ s, $|\Omega - 1| \propto t^{2/3}$ falls by $(1.6\times10^{12}/4.4\times10^{17})^{2/3} = (3.6\times10^{-6})^{2/3} = 2.4\times10^{-4}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Back through the radiation era">
From $1.6\times10^{12}$ s to 1 s, $|\Omega - 1| \propto t$ falls by another $6\times10^{-13}$. Altogether $|\Omega - 1| < 0.01 \times 2.4\times10^{-4} \times 6\times10^{-13} \approx 1.5\times10^{-18}$ at one second: Ω had to equal 1 to about seventeen decimal places (the same estimate as on the 8.286 flatness-problem page).
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Two more problems

- **Monopoles.** Grand unified theories predict that many magnetic monopoles were produced in the early universe. None is seen, so their density must somehow have been reduced.
- **Seeds of structure.** Galaxies grew from small perturbations. What created those?

## Inflation: one idea, four solutions

Suppose that for a short period the quantity $kc^2/(a^2H^2)$ **decreased**. Then:

- **flatness:** $|\Omega - 1|$ would be driven towards zero, like a balloon blown up so much that its surface looks flat;
- **horizon:** the comoving Hubble length $c/(aH)$ would shrink. A region small enough to have been in causal contact before this period would be stretched to cover everything we can see today, explaining why the CMB is so smooth.

So a decreasing $1/(aH)$ solves both. Since $aH = \dot a$, the condition is

<Formula label="Condition for a shrinking comoving horizon" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{c}{aH}\right) < 0 \;\iff\; \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{c}{\dot a}\right) = -\frac{c\,\ddot a}{\dot a^2} < 0 \;\iff\; \ddot a > 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (361), p. 78" />

an era of **accelerated expansion**. The second Friedmann equation (with $\Lambda = 0$ early on) shows what that takes:

<Formula label="Negative pressure for acceleration" tex="\frac{\ddot a}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left(\rho + \frac{3p}{c^2}\right) > 0 \;\Rightarrow\; p < -\frac{\rho c^2}{3} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{p}{\rho c^2} < -\frac13" cite="reading1" at="eq. (362), p. 78" />

Sufficiently negative pressure. The same rapid expansion also **dilutes the monopoles** until there is hardly one left in our observable universe, and **stretches tiny fluctuations** into the seeds of structure. All four problems are solved.

## The cosmological constant as negative pressure

The notes show that $\Lambda$ already behaves like this. Combining the two Friedmann equations gives energy conservation, the first law of thermodynamics for an expanding volume:

<Formula label="Energy conservation" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(\rho c^2a^3\right) + p\,\frac{d}{dt}\left(a^3\right) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \dot\rho = -3H\left(\rho + \frac{p}{c^2}\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (363), p. 79" />

A constant density $\rho_\Lambda$ requires

<Formula label="Pressure of a cosmological constant" tex="\rho + \frac{p}{c^2} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; p = -\rho c^2, \qquad w = \frac{p}{\rho c^2} = -1 < -\frac13" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (364)–(365), p. 79" />

so $\Lambda$ accelerates the expansion and shrinks the comoving horizon. Once it dominates,

<Formula label="Expansion dominated by Λ" tex="H^2(a) = H_0^2\,\Omega_\Lambda = \left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 \;\Rightarrow\; a \propto e^{\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda}\,H_0t}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (366), p. 79" />

exponential growth. [The notes say that $w = -1/3$ "is needed"; strictly $w < -1/3$ is needed, as they derived.] But the observed $\Lambda$ is useless for inflation: it acts far too late, and being constant, it would never stop.

## A slowly rolling scalar field

Inflation therefore needs something that behaves like a large cosmological constant early on and then switches off. The notes take a homogeneous **scalar field** $\phi$, the **inflaton**, with Lagrangian density

<Formula label="Scalar field Lagrangian" tex="\mathcal{L} = \frac12\,\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi - V(\phi)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (367), p. 79" />

Its energy–momentum tensor gives the energy density and pressure

<Formula label="Energy density and pressure of the field" tex="T_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\phi\,\partial_\nu\phi - g_{\mu\nu}\mathcal{L} \;\Rightarrow\; \rho c^2 = \frac12\dot\phi^2 + V(\phi), \qquad p = \frac12\dot\phi^2 - V(\phi)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (368), p. 79" />

(in units where the field's kinetic term has these simple forms). Demanding $p < -\rho c^2/3$:

<Formula label="Condition for inflation" tex="\frac12\dot\phi^2 - V(\phi) < -\frac13\left(\frac12\dot\phi^2 + V(\phi)\right) \;\Rightarrow\; \dot\phi^2 < V(\phi)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (369), p. 79" />

[The notes drop the square on $\dot\phi$ in the first line here and in the pressure on p. 80.] The field must **move slowly** compared with the height of its potential: the potential must be **flat**, and it must have a **minimum** where inflation can stop.

How does the field move? Put $\rho$ and $p$ into the energy conservation equation: with $\dot\rho c^2 = \dot\phi\ddot\phi + \dot\phi\,dV/d\phi$,

<Formula label="Field evolution equation" tex="\dot\phi\ddot\phi + \dot\phi\frac{dV}{d\phi} = -3H\left(\frac12\dot\phi^2 + V\right) - 3H\left(\frac12\dot\phi^2 - V\right) \;\Rightarrow\; \ddot\phi + 3H\dot\phi = -\frac{dV}{d\phi}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (370), p. 80" />

This is a ball rolling down the potential $V(\phi)$, with a friction term $3H\dot\phi$, the **Hubble drag**, coming from the expansion. In a static universe ($H = 0$) there would be no drag. The slope $dV/d\phi$ sets how fast energy is extracted from the field.

## Slow roll and reheating

During **slow roll** the field's kinetic energy is negligible, so

<Formula label="Slow-roll Friedmann equation" tex="H^2 \approx \frac{8\pi G}{3}V(\phi)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (371), p. 80" />

which is nearly constant while $V \approx V_0$: the expansion is close to exponential. And the acceleration $\ddot\phi$ is negligible, so

<Formula label="Slow-roll field equation" tex="3H\dot\phi \approx -\frac{dV}{d\phi}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (372), p. 80" />

These two approximations amount to

<Formula label="Slow-roll conditions" tex="\dot\phi^2 \ll V, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\dot\phi^2 \ll \frac{dV}{dt} \;\Rightarrow\; \ddot\phi \ll \frac{dV}{d\phi}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (373), p. 80" />

and can be written with two dimensionless **slow-roll parameters** measuring the slope and curvature of the potential:

<Formula label="Slow-roll parameters" tex="\epsilon := \frac{1}{24\pi G}\left(\frac{V'}{V}\right)^2 \ll 1, \qquad \eta := \frac{1}{8\pi G}\left(\frac{V''}{V}\right) \ll 1" cite="reading1" at="eq. (374), p. 80" />

(in units with $\hbar = c = 1$; this $\eta$ is unrelated to the baryon-to-photon ratio). [The usual definition of $\epsilon$ has $16\pi G$ in place of $24\pi G$; the conclusion, that the potential must be flat, does not depend on the factor.] As long as both are small, inflation continues.

The notes sketch the shape: a long, nearly flat plateau at height $V_0$, down which the field rolls slowly (inflation), followed by a drop into a minimum at $\phi_0$. There the field oscillates and decays, through its coupling to ordinary particles, into a hot gas of matter and radiation: **reheating**. Since $H^2 \approx \frac{8\pi G}{3}V_0$ during inflation (eq. 375, p. 81), the notes conclude that a higher plateau and a longer path give more inflation. [More precisely, what matters is how flat the potential is compared with its height: the number of e-folds in slow roll is about $8\pi G\int(V/V')\,d\phi$, which does not change if $V$ is multiplied by a constant.]

## The story so far

The notes close the cosmology part (section 1.E) with its ingredients: the **metric** (geometry), the **Friedmann equations** (dynamics), **distances** (the link to observations) and **horizons** (the evidence for inflation). The emerging history:

1. $t = 0$: the Big Bang;
2. $t \sim 10^{-34}$ s: inflation;
3. the temperature falls as $T \propto 1 + z$;
4. $z \approx 3200$: matter takes over from radiation;
5. $z \approx 1100$: recombination, the CMB is released;
6. structure formation turns nonlinear: the first stars and galaxies;
7. $z \approx 0.33$: $\Lambda$ takes over from matter.

The first five stages are hidden behind the opaque early universe; from recombination on, the universe is transparent and can be observed. (The value 0.33 for the last transition is the one for $\Omega_m = 0.3$, $\Omega_\Lambda = 0.7$; the Planck values give about 0.3, as on the density-parameters page, where the Lecture 10 notes' $z \approx 1.3$ is corrected.)

## Summary

- Recombination happens at $T \approx 3000$ K, $z \approx 1090$, late because of the huge number of photons per baryon; the CMB we see is uniform to $10^{-5}$.
- **Horizon problem:** the horizon at recombination spans only about $1/\sqrt{z_\text{recomb}} \approx 1.7°$ on our sky, yet the whole sky has the same temperature.
- **Flatness problem:** $|\Omega - 1|$ grows as $t$ or $t^{2/3}$, so Ω had to be fine-tuned to 1 early on. Monopoles and the seeds of structure are two more puzzles.
- **Inflation**, a period with $\ddot a > 0$ and so $p < -\rho c^2/3$, shrinks the comoving horizon, flattens space, dilutes monopoles and stretches fluctuations into seeds.
- A scalar field with $\dot\phi^2 < V$ provides it. It obeys $\ddot\phi + 3H\dot\phi = -dV/d\phi$; during slow roll $H^2 \approx \frac{8\pi G}{3}V$ and the slow-roll parameters are small. Inflation ends when the field reaches its minimum and reheats the universe.
