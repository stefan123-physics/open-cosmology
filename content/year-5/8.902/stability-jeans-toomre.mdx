The Jeans equations of the previous page describe galaxies in equilibrium. But an equilibrium can be unstable: real stellar systems are always perturbed, and the question is whether the perturbations die away or grow. Lecture 7 of 8.902 (section 3.D of the notes) answers this on small scales, where random motions resist collapse (the **Jeans instability**), and on large scales in rotating discs, where rotation resists it (leading to the **Toomre criterion**). (The notes open by repeating the end of section 3.C, which the previous page covers.)

## A uniform, static medium

Start with a nearly uniform, non-rotating distribution of stars (or gas). Write the Jeans equation with the mass density $\rho = mn$ and assume isotropic random motions, so that the "pressure" is $P = \rho\sigma^2$:

<Formula label="Jeans equation as a fluid equation" tex="\frac{\partial\vec v}{\partial t} + \left(\vec v\cdot\nabla\right)\vec v = -\nabla\phi - \frac1\rho\nabla P" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (187)–(188), p. 43" />

<Formula label="Continuity equation" tex="\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\vec v) = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (189), p. 43" />

where $\vec v$ now stands for the mean velocity. [The notes write $\sigma_{ij}$ in eq. (187) where $\sigma_{ij}^2$ is meant.] These are the Euler and continuity equations of a fluid, together with Poisson's equation.

## Small perturbations and the Jeans swindle

Perturb each quantity slightly about a uniform background, with a small parameter $\epsilon$:

<Formula label="Perturbed quantities" tex="\rho = \rho_0 + \epsilon\rho_1(\vec x, t), \quad \vec v = \vec v_0 + \epsilon\vec v_1(\vec x, t), \quad P = P_0 + \epsilon P_1(\vec x, t), \quad \phi = \phi_0 + \epsilon\phi_1(\vec x, t)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (190), p. 43" />

The background is static, $\vec v_0 = 0$, with constant $\rho_0$ and $P_0$, and the notes choose $\phi_0 = 0$. Strictly that is inconsistent: Poisson's equation $\nabla^2\phi_0 = 4\pi G\rho_0$ would then require $\rho_0 = 0$. Ignoring the problem is known as the **Jeans swindle**. (In an expanding universe the swindle is not needed: the expansion itself provides the background, as on the [linear-perturbations page](/courses/8-902/linear-perturbations).)

Keeping only terms of first order in $\epsilon$, the continuity equation (eq. 191) becomes

<Formula label="Linearized continuity equation" tex="\frac{\partial\rho_1}{\partial t} + \rho_0\nabla\cdot\vec v_1 = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (192), p. 44" />

and the Jeans equation (eqs. 193–194) becomes $\partial\vec v_1/\partial t = -\nabla\phi_1 - \nabla P_1/\rho_0$. The pressure changes with the density at the **sound speed** $v_s$, the speed at which perturbations propagate:

<Formula label="Pressure perturbation and sound speed" tex="\nabla P_1 = \left.\frac{\partial P}{\partial\rho}\right|_{\rho_0}\nabla\rho_1 = v_s^2\nabla\rho_1" cite="reading1" at="eq. (195), p. 44" />

<Formula label="Linearized Jeans equation" tex="\frac{\partial\vec v_1}{\partial t} = -\nabla\phi_1 - \frac{v_s^2}{\rho_0}\nabla\rho_1" cite="reading1" at="eq. (196), p. 44" />

Take the time derivative of the continuity equation, insert the divergence of the Jeans equation, and use Poisson's equation for the perturbation, $\nabla^2\phi_1 = 4\pi G\rho_1$ (eqs. 197–198). The velocity drops out, leaving a single equation for the density:

<Formula label="Wave equation for the density perturbation" tex="\frac{\partial^2\rho_1}{\partial t^2} - 4\pi G\rho_0\,\rho_1 - v_s^2\nabla^2\rho_1 = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (199), p. 44" />

Without gravity this is an ordinary sound wave. Gravity adds the term $-4\pi G\rho_0\rho_1$, which tries to make the perturbation grow.

## The Jeans length

Try a plane wave:

<Formula label="Plane-wave ansatz and dispersion relation" tex="\rho_1 = C\exp\left[i\left(\vec k\cdot\vec x - \omega t\right)\right] \;\Rightarrow\; \omega^2 = v_s^2k^2 - 4\pi G\rho_0" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (200)–(201), p. 45" />

[The notes write $\vec k\cdot\vec c$ in the exponent; the position $\vec x$ is meant.] Two cases:

- $\omega^2 > 0$: $\omega$ is real, and the perturbation **oscillates**: a stable sound wave.
- $\omega^2 < 0$: $\omega$ is imaginary, the time dependence is a real exponential, and the perturbation **grows** (or decays): unstable.

The borderline $\omega = 0$ defines the **Jeans length** and the **Jeans mass**:

<Formula label="Jeans length and mass" tex="\lambda_J^2 = \left(\frac{2\pi}{k}\right)^2 = \frac{\pi v_s^2}{G\rho_0} = \frac{\pi\sigma^2}{G\rho_0}, \qquad M_J = \frac43\pi\rho_0\lambda_J^3" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (202)–(203), p. 45" />

Perturbations smaller than $\lambda_J$ (and lighter than $M_J$) are stable; larger ones collapse. For collisional gas the sound speed sets $\lambda_J$; for collisionless stars and dark matter the velocity dispersion $\sigma$ plays the same role.

**Why?** Pressure can only stop a collapse if a pressure wave can cross the region before it has collapsed. The free-fall time and the crossing time are

<Formula label="Free-fall and crossing times" tex="t_\text{ff} \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{G\rho}}, \qquad t_\text{cross} \sim \frac{r}{v_s}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (204)–(205), p. 45" />

and collapse wins when

<Formula label="Condition for collapse" tex="t_\text{cross} > t_\text{ff} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{r}{v_s} > \frac{1}{\sqrt{G\rho}} \;\Rightarrow\; r^2 > \frac{v_s^2}{G\rho}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (206), p. 45" />

the Jeans criterion up to a factor of $\pi$. Random motions and pressure stabilize the **small** scales.

<WorkedExample title="The Jeans mass of a cold cloud">
<Problem>
A cold molecular cloud has $T = 10$ K and $10^3$ molecules per cm³, with mean molecular mass $2.33\,m_\text{H}$ ($m_\text{H} = 1.67\times10^{-27}$ kg). Take $v_s = \sqrt{kT/(2.33\,m_\text{H})}$. Find $v_s$, $\lambda_J$, $M_J$ and the free-fall time.
</Problem>
<Step title="Sound speed and density">
$v_s = \sqrt{1.38\times10^{-23} \times 10/(2.33 \times 1.67\times10^{-27})} = 188$ m/s. $\rho_0 = 10^9\ \text{m}^{-3} \times 2.33 \times 1.67\times10^{-27}$ kg $= 3.9\times10^{-18}$ kg/m³.
</Step>
<Step title="Jeans length and mass">
$\lambda_J = \sqrt{\pi v_s^2/(G\rho_0)} = 2.1\times10^{16}$ m $= 0.67$ pc, and $M_J = \frac43\pi\rho_0\lambda_J^3 \approx 73\,M_\odot$. Clumps more massive than a few tens of solar masses cannot be held up by thermal pressure: this is why star formation happens in such cold, dense clouds. (An application, not in the notes.)
</Step>
<Step title="Free-fall time">
$t_\text{ff} \sim 1/\sqrt{G\rho_0} = 6.2\times10^{13}$ s $\approx 2$ million years.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Large scales: rotation and the Toomre instability

Now take a **rotating** disc with surface density $\Sigma$. Consider a patch of radius $R$ that contracts to $R' = R - dr$, conserving its mass and angular momentum. Gravity pulls the patch together with an acceleration that grows as it shrinks:

<Formula label="Gravitational acceleration of the patch" tex="a_g = \frac{G\pi R^2\Sigma}{R'^2} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{da_g}{dR'} = -\frac{2G\pi R^2\Sigma}{R'^3}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (207), p. 46" />

($\pi R^2\Sigma$ is the patch's mass). But the patch also spins up, like a skater pulling in her arms, because its angular momentum is conserved:

<Formula label="Spin-up of the contracting patch" tex="L = \Omega R^2 = \Omega'R'^2 \;\Rightarrow\; \Omega' = \Omega\left(\frac{R}{R'}\right)^2" cite="reading1" at="eq. (208), p. 46" />

so its centrifugal acceleration grows even faster:

<Formula label="Centrifugal acceleration of the patch" tex="a_c = R'\Omega'^2 = \Omega^2\frac{R^4}{R'^3} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{da_c}{dR'} = -\frac{3\Omega^2R^4}{R'^4}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (209), p. 46" />

The patch is stable if the centrifugal push grows faster than gravity, $|da_g| < |da_c|$. With $R/R' \approx 1$:

<Formula label="Stability against rotation" tex="\frac{2\pi GR^2\Sigma}{R'^3} < \frac{3\Omega^2R^4}{R'^4} \;\Rightarrow\; R > R_\text{rot} = \frac{2\pi G\Sigma}{3\Omega^2}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (210)–(211), p. 46" />

[The notes print $\Omega^3$ in eq. (211); eq. (210) shows it is $\Omega^2$.] Rotation stabilizes the **large** scales.

## The Toomre criterion

Put the two together. Small patches ($R < \lambda_J$) are held up by random motions, large ones ($R > R_\text{rot}$) by rotation. In between, $\lambda_J < R < R_\text{rot}$, the disc is unstable, unless that range is empty: the disc is stable on **all** scales if $\lambda_J \geq R_\text{rot}$. Adapting the Jeans length to a thin disc (the notes use $\lambda_J = \frac{\pi}{8}\,\sigma^2/(G\Sigma)$):

<Formula label="Combining the two criteria" tex="\frac{\pi}{8}\,\frac{\sigma^2}{G\Sigma} \geq \frac{2\pi G\Sigma}{3\Omega^2} \;\Rightarrow\; \sigma_\text{crit} \geq \frac{4}{\sqrt3}\,\frac{G\Sigma}{\Omega}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (212), p. 47" />

The patch actually rotates with the epicycle frequency $\kappa$ rather than $\Omega$, and for a flat rotation curve $\kappa = \sqrt2\,\Omega$ (from $\kappa^2 = r\,d\Omega^2/dr + 4\Omega^2$, eqs. 213–214). Substituting $\Omega = \kappa/\sqrt2$:

<Formula label="Critical velocity dispersion" tex="\sigma_\text{crit} \geq \frac{4}{\sqrt3}\,\frac{G\Sigma}{\kappa/\sqrt2} = \sqrt{\frac{32}{3}}\,\frac{G\Sigma}{\kappa} \approx 3.26\,\frac{G\Sigma}{\kappa}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (213)–(216), p. 47" />

[The notes print $\kappa^2/\sqrt2$ in the denominator of eq. (215); it should be $\kappa/\sqrt2$, which gives their $\sqrt{32/3} = 3.27$. The notes round this down to 3.26 and credit that value to Toomre ("Toomre finds"); Toomre's exact calculation for a disc of stars actually gives 3.36 (Toomre 1964). For a gas disc the corresponding factor is $\pi$, a result due to Safronov (1960) and Goldreich and Lynden-Bell (1965).] The stability is summarized by the **Toomre parameter**:

<Formula label="Toomre criterion" tex="Q = \frac{\sigma}{\sigma_\text{crit}} \begin{cases} > 1: & \text{stable} \\ < 1: & \text{unstable} \end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (217), p. 47" />

The notes illustrate it with a measured profile of $Q$ against radius for a distant rotating disc galaxy (the "Wolfe Disk", seen 1.5 billion years after the Big Bang; Neeleman et al. 2020), not reproduced here. A model in which the gas density falls off exponentially gives $Q$ dipping below 1 over part of the disc; the measured points lie lower still, which the notes attribute to beam smearing inflating the measured surface density.

<WorkedExample title="Is the solar neighbourhood stable?">
<Problem>
Take a stellar disc patch with $\Sigma = 50\,M_\odot$/pc², $\kappa = 36$ km/s/kpc (the value found from the Oort constants on the epicycle page) and a radial velocity dispersion of 35 km/s. Is it stable? Use $\sigma_\text{crit} = 3.26\,G\Sigma/\kappa$ with $G = 0.0043$ pc (km/s)² $M_\odot^{-1}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Critical dispersion">
Convert $\kappa = 0.036$ km/s/pc. Then $\sigma_\text{crit} = 3.26 \times 0.0043 \times 50/0.036 = 19.5$ km/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Toomre Q">
$Q = 35/19.5 = 1.8 > 1$: stable, though not by a large margin. (Not in the notes: discs tend to settle near $Q \sim 1$–2, because unstable discs heat up, or form stars, until they become stable.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- An equilibrium can be unstable. Linearizing the fluid (Jeans) equations about a uniform, static background (the Jeans swindle) gives a wave equation with $\omega^2 = v_s^2k^2 - 4\pi G\rho_0$.
- Perturbations larger than the **Jeans length** $\lambda_J = \sqrt{\pi v_s^2/(G\rho_0)}$ (or with $\sigma$ in place of $v_s$ for stars and dark matter) grow; smaller ones oscillate as sound waves. Physically, pressure can resist collapse only if it can cross the region within a free-fall time.
- In a rotating disc a contracting patch spins up; rotation stabilizes scales larger than $R_\text{rot} = 2\pi G\Sigma/(3\Omega^2)$.
- The disc is stable on all scales if $\lambda_J \geq R_\text{rot}$, i.e. if $\sigma > \sigma_\text{crit} \approx 3.26\,G\Sigma/\kappa$ (3.36 in Toomre's exact treatment): the **Toomre criterion** $Q = \sigma/\sigma_\text{crit} > 1$.
