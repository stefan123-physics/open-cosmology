The previous pages followed one perturbation at a time. The real universe contains ripples on **every** scale at once, with random phases, so no theory can predict where a particular galaxy sits. What theory can predict is the **statistics**: how strong the fluctuations are on each scale. This page follows the end of the 8.902 Lecture 14 notes (section 2.C) and the start of Lecture 15 (section 2.D), where the notes introduce the power spectrum, the correlation function and the form of the primordial spectrum. [The Lecture 15 notes open by repeating p. 90 of the Lecture 14 notes; citations below use whichever PDF first contains the equation.]

## Fourier modes and the variance

Write the density contrast as a sum of plane waves, exactly as for the single modes on the earlier pages:

<Formula label="Fourier transform pair" tex="\delta(\vec x) = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\hat\delta(\vec k)\,e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec x}, \qquad \hat\delta(\vec k) = \int d^3x\,\delta(\vec x)\,e^{+i\vec k\cdot\vec x}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (402), p. 88" />

By construction the average of $\delta$ over space is zero: it measures departures from the mean density. The simplest non-trivial number is therefore the **variance**, the mean of $\delta^2$:

<Formula label="Mean and variance" tex="\langle\delta\rangle = 0, \qquad \sigma^2 = \langle\delta^2\rangle - \langle\delta\rangle^2 = \langle\delta^2\rangle = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,|\hat\delta(\vec k)|^2" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (403)–(404), p. 88" />

[The notes write the averages as plain integrals $\int d^3x$; an average needs a factor $1/V$, the volume, so strictly $P(k) = |\hat\delta(k)|^2/V$; the same applies to $\xi$ in eq. (408) below. The step to $k$-space is Parseval's theorem.]

If the universe is statistically **homogeneous and isotropic**, $|\hat\delta|^2$ can depend only on the length $k = |\vec k|$, not on direction, and $d^3k = 4\pi k^2\,dk$. Then

<Formula label="Power spectrum" tex="\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\int|\hat\delta(k)|^2\,k^2\,dk \;=:\; \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\int P(k)\,k^2\,dk, \qquad P(k) = |\hat\delta(k)|^2" cite="reading1" at="eq. (405), p. 89" />

using $4\pi/(2\pi)^3 = 1/(2\pi^2)$. The **power spectrum** $P(k)$ says how much of the total variance sits in waves of wavenumber $k$: that is, on length scales around $2\pi/k$. Two remarks from the notes:

- $P(k)$ and $\sigma$ change with time, because every $\hat\delta(k)$ grows. In the linear regime all large-scale modes grow by the same growth factor $D$ from the earlier page, so $\sigma = D\,\sigma_0$.
- The starting point is the **primordial** power spectrum left at the end of inflation. Its general form is a power law,

<Formula label="Primordial power spectrum" tex="P(k) = A\,k^n" cite="reading1" at="eq. (406), p. 89" />

which has no preferred scale ("scale-free"). Inflation predicts $n \approx 1$; section 2.D below shows why that value is natural.

## The two-point correlation function

We cannot measure $\delta$ for the dark matter directly, but we can count galaxies. Suppose galaxies **trace the mass**, with mean number density $n_0$. The probability of finding one galaxy in a small volume $dV_1$ and another in $dV_2$ a distance $r$ away is

<Formula label="Pair probability (averaged over positions)" tex="dP = n_0[1 + \delta(\vec x)]\,dV_1 \cdot n_0[1 + \delta(\vec x + \vec r)]\,dV_2 = n_0^2\,[1 + \xi(r)]\,dV_1\,dV_2" cite="reading1" at="eq. (407), p. 89" />

On averaging, the terms linear in $\delta$ vanish, and the product $\langle\delta(\vec x)\,\delta(\vec x + \vec r)\rangle$ is left. It depends only on the distance $r$ (isotropy), and is called the **two-point correlation function** $\xi(r)$. For a random (Poisson) scattering of galaxies $\xi = 0$; $\xi > 0$ means pairs at that separation are **more** common than random: the galaxies cluster.

Substituting the Fourier expansion twice and using $\int d^3x\,e^{i(\vec k - \vec k')\cdot\vec x} = (2\pi)^3\delta_D(\vec k - \vec k')$ collapses the double integral to a single one:

<Formula label="Correlation function and power spectrum" tex="\xi(r) = \langle\delta(\vec x)\,\delta(\vec x + \vec r)\rangle = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^3}\int P(k)\,e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r}\,d^3k" cite="reading1" at="eq. (408), p. 89" />

So **ξ and P are a Fourier pair**: the same information, one in real space and one in wavenumber space. (Here $\delta_D$ is the Dirac delta, not the density contrast.) At $r = 0$ the correlation function is just the variance, $\xi(0) = \sigma^2$.

Galaxy surveys find, over a wide range of separations, a power law:

<Formula label="Observed galaxy correlation function" tex="\xi(r) \approx \left(\frac{r}{r_0}\right)^{-1.8}, \qquad r_0 \approx 5\,h^{-1}\ \text{Mpc}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (409), p. 90" />

The **correlation length** $r_0$ is where $\xi = 1$: pairs are twice as common as random. ($h \approx 0.7$ is the Hubble constant in units of 100 km/s/Mpc.) Different kinds of object have different $r_0$, and **more massive objects cluster more strongly**: the notes quote $\xi_{cc} \approx 20\,\xi_{gg}$ for the cluster–cluster compared with the galaxy–galaxy correlation function.

<WorkedExample title="Reading a correlation function">
<Problem>
Using $\xi_{gg}(r) = (r/5\,h^{-1}\,\text{Mpc})^{-1.8}$: (a) how many times more likely than random is a galaxy pair at $2\,h^{-1}$ Mpc, and at $20\,h^{-1}$ Mpc? (b) If $\xi_{cc} = 20\,\xi_{gg}$ with the same slope, what is the clusters' correlation length?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Small and large separations">
At $2\,h^{-1}$ Mpc: $\xi = (0.4)^{-1.8} = 5.2$, so $1 + \xi = 6.2$ times the random rate. At $20\,h^{-1}$ Mpc: $\xi = 4^{-1.8} = 0.082$, only 8% above random. Clustering is strong on small scales and fades on large ones.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Cluster correlation length">
We need $20\,(r_0^{cc}/5)^{-1.8} = 1$, so $r_0^{cc} = 5 \times 20^{1/1.8} = 5 \times 5.28 \approx 26\,h^{-1}$ Mpc. Clusters are correlated over distances five times larger than galaxies.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Fluctuations on a given mass scale

Section 2.D asks what $n$ and $A$ are. First, how big are the fluctuations on a given scale? Smoothing the field over a region of size $R$ removes waves shorter than $R$, so roughly only modes up to $k_\text{max} \sim 1/R$ contribute (small $k$ = large scales):

<Formula label="Variance up to a maximum wavenumber" tex="\sigma^2 \approx \int_0^{k_\text{max}} P(k)\,k^2\,dk = \int_0^{k_\text{max}} A\,k^{n+2}\,dk \propto k_\text{max}^{\,n+3} \;\Rightarrow\; \sigma \propto k^{\frac12(n+3)}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (410), p. 90" />

(the integral converges at $k = 0$ only for $n > -3$). A region of size $R$ holds a mass $M \propto R^3 \propto k^{-3}$, so $k \propto M^{-1/3}$ and

<Formula label="Fluctuation amplitude versus mass" tex="\sigma \propto \begin{cases} k^{\frac12(n+3)} \\[0.5ex] M^{-\frac16(n+3)} \end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (411)–(412), p. 90" />

For $n = 1$, $\sigma \propto M^{-2/3}$: a region with 1000 times more mass has fluctuations 100 times smaller. Small masses fluctuate most, the root of **hierarchical** ("bottom-up") structure formation, in which small objects form first.

## Why n ≈ 1: the Harrison–Zel'dovich spectrum

Outside the horizon every mode grows. The notes' argument is that no mass scale should be **special**: every mode should have the same fluctuation strength $\sigma$ at the moment it **enters** the horizon. What does that demand of $n$?

The mass inside the horizon is $M_h \propto \rho_m r_h^3$, with $\rho_m \propto (1+z)^3$. The physical horizon grows as $r_h \propto t$, i.e. as $a^2$ in the radiation era and $a^{3/2}$ in the matter era, so

<Formula label="Horizon mass" tex="M_h \propto \rho_m r_h^3, \quad \rho_m \propto (1+z)^3, \quad r_h \propto \begin{cases} a^2 = (1+z)^{-2} & \text{radiation} \\ a^{3/2} = (1+z)^{-3/2} & \text{matter} \end{cases} \;\Rightarrow\; M_h \propto \begin{cases} (1+z_h)^{-3} & \text{radiation} \\ (1+z_h)^{-3/2} & \text{matter} \end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (413)–(414), p. 90" />

Meanwhile, outside the horizon, $\sigma$ grows like $\delta$, as $a^2$ in the radiation era and $a$ in the matter era (the super-horizon growth of the previous page):

<Formula label="Super-horizon growth of σ" tex="\sigma \propto \delta \propto \begin{cases} a^2 = (1+z)^{-2} & \text{radiation} \\ a = (1+z)^{-1} & \text{matter} \end{cases}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (415), p. 91" />

[The notes print $a^{3/2}$ for the matter case; $(1+z)^{-1}$, which they also print, is $a$, consistent with $\delta \propto a$ in the matter era.]

Take a mode of mass $M$ that enters during the radiation era, at redshift $z_h$. From the end of inflation, at $z_p$, until then it grows as $a^2$:

<Formula label="Amplitude at horizon entry (radiation era)" tex="\sigma(z_h) = \sigma(z_p)\left(\frac{1+z_p}{1+z_h}\right)^2 \propto \sigma(z_p)\,(1+z_h)^{-2}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (416), p. 91" />

The primordial amplitude is $\sigma(z_p) \propto M^{-(n+3)/6}$ from eq. (412), and the mode enters when $M_h = M \propto (1+z_h)^{-3}$, so $(1+z_h)^{-2} \propto M^{2/3}$:

<Formula label="Horizon-entry amplitude" tex="\sigma(z_h) \propto M^{-\frac16(n+3)}\,M^{2/3} = M^{-\left(\frac12 + \frac{n-4}{6}\right)} = M^{-\frac{n-1}{6}}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (417)–(418), p. 91" />

For a mode entering in the matter era the notes say only that the calculation is the same, giving the same result (eq. 419). Filling in the step (this page's own): $M_h \propto (1+z_h)^{-3/2}$ gives $(1+z_h)^{-1} \propto M^{2/3}$ again, and the earlier growth through the radiation era is the same factor for every such mode, so it does not change the $M$-dependence. The last form, $M^{-(n-1)/6}$, is this page's simplification of the notes' exponent. For the horizon-entry amplitude to be the **same for every mass**, the exponent must vanish: no special scale means $n = 1$.

This is the **Harrison–Zel'dovich** (scale-invariant) spectrum, proposed around 1970 by Edward Harrison and Yakov Zel'dovich (and independently Peebles and Yu), before inflation. [The notes say σ(z_h) "should not depend on n"; the requirement is that it should not depend on $M$, which then fixes $n$.] Inflation, on the 8.286 pages, produces almost exactly this spectrum.

## The amplitude: σ₈ and bias

Inflation predicts the shape, but in practice the amplitude $A$ is measured. The standard yardstick is **$\sigma_8$**, the rms density fluctuation in spheres of radius $8\,h^{-1}$ Mpc. Galaxies need not trace the mass exactly; their fluctuations differ by the **bias** $b$:

<Formula label="Galaxy bias" tex="\sigma_{8,\text{gal}} = b\,\sigma_{8,\text{mass}}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (420), p. 91" />

Observed galaxies have $\sigma_{8,\text{gal}} \approx 1$. The notes quote the combined WMAP and SDSS values

<Formula label="Measured index and amplitude (WMAP + SDSS, as quoted in the notes)" tex="n = 0.953 \pm 0.016, \qquad \sigma_8 = 0.756 \pm 0.035" cite="reading2" at="eq. (421), p. 91" />

So $n$ is close to 1 but slightly smaller, a small "tilt" that simple inflation models predict. Since the mass $\sigma_8$ is below 1 while $\sigma_{8,\text{gal}} \approx 1$, these galaxies are biased, $b > 1$. (A standard explanation, not given in the notes: galaxies form preferentially in the higher peaks of the density field, and clusters, in the highest peaks, are even more biased.)

<Callout kind="note" title="Later measurements">
The values in the notes come from an early WMAP + SDSS analysis. Planck's final (2018) results (Planck 2018 VI, Table 2) give $n_s = 0.965 \pm 0.004$ and $\sigma_8 = 0.811 \pm 0.006$ for the ΛCDM fit, so $n < 1$ is now established at more than eight standard deviations. Some weak-lensing surveys find a somewhat lower clustering amplitude than Planck (the "$S_8$ tension"), whose significance is still debated.
</Callout>

## The transfer function: bending the spectrum

The primordial spectrum is not what we see today. On the previous page, dark matter modes that entered the horizon during the radiation era **stagnated** until matter–radiation equality, while those entering later grew without interruption. Small scales entered earliest and lost the most growth. The notes describe this with a **transfer function** $T(k)$:

<Formula label="Processed power spectrum" tex="P_0(k) = (A\,k)\,T^2(k), \qquad T(k) \approx \begin{cases} 1, & 1/k \gg L_0 \\[0.5ex] 1/k^2, & 1/k \ll L_0 \end{cases}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (422), p. 92" />

where $L_0$ is the comoving horizon at matter–radiation equality, and $n = 1$ has been used. [The notes write $T(K)$; the second case is meant up to normalisation, $T \propto (kL_0)^{-2}$.] Large scales ($k \ll 1/L_0$) entered after equality and keep the primordial $P \propto k$. Small scales are suppressed by $T^2 \propto k^{-4}$, giving $P_0 \propto k^{-3}$. The spectrum therefore **rises, peaks near $k_0 \sim 1/L_0$, and falls**: the turnover is a fossil of the moment matter took over from radiation.

## Summary

- The density field is described statistically: $\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\int P(k)\,k^2\,dk$ with the **power spectrum** $P(k) = |\hat\delta(k)|^2$. It grows in time as $\sigma = D\sigma_0$.
- The **two-point correlation function** $\xi(r)$ is the excess probability of finding pairs at separation $r$; it is the Fourier transform of $P(k)$. Galaxies show $\xi \approx (r/5\,h^{-1}\text{Mpc})^{-1.8}$; massive objects cluster more.
- For $P = Ak^n$, $\sigma \propto M^{-(n+3)/6}$, and the amplitude at horizon entry is $\propto M^{-(n-1)/6}$. Requiring no special scale gives the **Harrison–Zel'dovich** spectrum $n = 1$; measurements give $n$ slightly below 1.
- The amplitude is measured as **$\sigma_8$**; galaxies are **biased** tracers, $\sigma_{8,\text{gal}} = b\,\sigma_{8,\text{mass}}$.
- Stagnation of modes that entered the horizon before matter–radiation equality bends the spectrum: $P_0 = Ak\,T^2(k)$ rises as $k$ on large scales and falls as $k^{-3}$ on small ones, peaking near the horizon scale at equality.
