Lecture 3 showed that stars move in the smooth, collective potential of their galaxy. Lecture 4 of 8.902 opens section 3, "Modelling galaxies", whose ingredients are listed at its start: potential–density pairs (the common potential), orbits, the phase-space distribution function and the Vlasov equation, stability (the Jeans criterion), stellar populations, star formation and the initial mass function, chemical evolution, and active galaxies. This page covers the first, section 3.A. (The notes then begin section 3.B on orbits, which the next page covers.)

## Potential and density

The gravitational force per unit mass is the gradient of a scalar potential:

<Formula label="Force from a potential" tex="-\nabla\phi = \frac1m\vec F" cite="reading1" at="eq. (78), p. 22" />

so that $m\phi$ is the potential energy. Poisson's equation, $\nabla^2\phi = 4\pi G\rho$, links $\phi$ to the density, with the general solution

<Formula label="Potential of a density distribution" tex="\phi(\vec r) = -G\int\frac{\rho(\vec r\,')}{|\vec r\,' - \vec r|}\,d^3r'" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (79)–(80), p. 22" />

[The notes drop the minus sign; the potential of attracting mass is negative.] Every density has its potential and vice versa: **potential–density pairs**.

## The point mass

The simplest is the Kepler potential of a point mass, whose gradient gives the inverse-square force:

<Formula label="Point mass" tex="\phi = -\frac{GM}{r}, \qquad \frac1m\vec F = -\frac{GM}{r^2}\,\hat e_r" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (81)–(82), p. 22" />

[The notes omit the minus sign of the force, which points inward.]

## The uniform sphere

For a sphere of uniform density, $\rho = M/(\frac43\pi R^3)$, the force is easier to find directly, with **Gauss's theorem** for gravity. Over a sphere $S_r$ of radius $r$ enclosing the volume $V_r$,

<Formula label="Gauss's theorem for gravity" tex="\oint_{S_r}\vec F\cdot d\vec S = \int_{V_r}(\nabla\cdot\vec F)\,dV = -m\int_{V_r}\nabla^2\phi\,dV = -4\pi Gm\int\rho\,dV = -4\pi GM(<r)\,m" cite="reading1" at="eq. (84), p. 22 (density from eq. 83)" />

By symmetry the force points inward with the same size $F(r)$ everywhere on the sphere, so the left side is $-4\pi r^2F(r)$:

<Formula label="Force at radius r" tex="\oint_{S_r}\vec F\cdot d\vec S = -4\pi r^2F(r) \;\Rightarrow\; 4\pi r^2F(r) = 4\pi GM(<r)\,m" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (85)–(86), p. 23" />

[The notes print "$r\pi$" for $4\pi$ in eq. (86).] Outside, all the mass is enclosed; inside, only the fraction $(r/R)^3$:

<Formula label="Force outside and inside a uniform sphere" tex="r > R:\ F(r) = \frac{GMm}{r^2}, \qquad r < R:\ F(r) = \frac{4\pi G\rho r}{3}\,m" cite="reading1" at="eq. (87), p. 23" />

and integrating $\vec F/m = -\nabla\phi$,

<Formula label="Potential outside and inside a uniform sphere" tex="r > R:\ \phi(r) = -\frac{GM}{r} + \text{const}, \qquad r < R:\ \phi(r) = \frac{2\pi G\rho r^2}{3} + \text{const}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (88), p. 23" />

[The notes write these with an extra factor $m$ and a plus sign outside; per unit mass, the outside potential is $-GM/r$. The line before eq. (88) also writes the Laplacian where the gradient is meant.] Inside, the force grows linearly with $r$, like a spring.

<WorkedExample title="Falling through a uniform sphere">
<Problem>
Inside a uniform sphere the force is $F = -\frac{4\pi G\rho}{3}rm$, a spring force. With $G = 0.0043$ pc (km/s)² $M_\odot^{-1}$, find the period of a star oscillating through the centre of a uniform ball of density $\rho = 0.1\,M_\odot$/pc³ (1 pc/(km/s) is 0.978 million years).
</Problem>
<Step title="The angular frequency">
$\omega^2 = \frac{4\pi G\rho}{3} = \frac{4\pi \times 0.0043 \times 0.1}{3} = 1.80\times10^{-3}$ (km/s/pc)², so $\omega = 0.0424$ km/s per pc.
</Step>
<Step title="The period">
$P = 2\pi/\omega = 148$ pc/(km/s) $\approx 145$ million years, the same for every amplitude. (An application of eq. 87, not in the notes.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The Mestel disc

A disc is a better model of a spiral galaxy. In spherical coordinates $(r, \theta, \varphi)$ the notes take the **Mestel disc** potential

<Formula label="Mestel disc potential" tex="\phi(r, \theta) = v_c^2\left[\ln\frac{r}{r_0} + \ln\frac{1 + |\cos\theta|}{2}\right]" cite="reading1" at="eq. (89), p. 23" />

Is this really a disc? Find the density from Poisson's equation, here with no $\varphi$ dependence:

<Formula label="Laplacian in spherical coordinates" tex="\nabla^2\phi = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^2\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta}\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (90), p. 23" />

With $\phi = v_c^2\phi_0$, the radial part gives $v_c^2/r^2$ and

<Formula label="Laplacian of the Mestel potential" tex="\nabla^2\phi = \frac{v_c^2}{r^2}\left[1 + \left(\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial\phi_0}{\partial\theta} + \frac{\partial^2\phi_0}{\partial\theta^2}\right)\right]" cite="reading1" at="eq. (91), p. 23" />

For $\cos\theta > 0$ (above the plane), $\phi_0 = \ln(r/r_0) + \ln\frac{1 + \cos\theta}{2}$ (eq. 92), and differentiating twice,

<Formula label="Angular derivatives" tex="\frac{\partial\phi_0}{\partial\theta} = -\frac{\sin\theta}{1 + \cos\theta}, \qquad \frac{\partial^2\phi_0}{\partial\theta^2} = -\frac{\cos\theta}{1 + \cos\theta} - \frac{\sin^2\theta}{(1 + \cos\theta)^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (93), p. 24" />

<Formula label="The bracket equals minus one" tex="\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial\phi_0}{\partial\theta} + \frac{\partial^2\phi_0}{\partial\theta^2} = -\frac{2\cos\theta + 2\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta}{(1 + \cos\theta)^2} = -\frac{(1 + \cos\theta)^2}{(1 + \cos\theta)^2} = -1" cite="reading1" at="eq. (94), p. 24" />

(using $\sin^2\theta = 1 - \cos^2\theta$). Below the plane the same holds with $\cos\theta \to -\cos\theta$. So $\nabla^2\phi = \frac{v_c^2}{r^2}(1 - 1) = 0$ everywhere **off** the plane $\theta = \pi/2$: there is no mass there. All the mass sits in an infinitely thin disc. Its **surface density** is the integral of $\rho = \nabla^2\phi/(4\pi G)$ across the plane:

<Formula label="Surface density as an integral" tex="\Sigma(r) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{4\pi G}\nabla^2\phi\,dz" cite="reading1" at="eq. (95), p. 24" />

Near the plane $z = r\cos\theta$, so $dz \approx -r\,d\theta$. Over the thin strip $\pi/2 - \epsilon < \theta < \pi/2 + \epsilon$ the smooth terms contribute nothing as $\epsilon \to 0$, and only the jump in $\partial\phi_0/\partial\theta$ across the plane survives (eqs. 96–97):

<Formula label="Surface density of the Mestel disc" tex="\Sigma(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi G}\frac{v_c^2}{r}\left(\left[\frac{\sin\theta}{1 - \cos\theta}\right]_{\pi/2 + \epsilon} - \left[\frac{-\sin\theta}{1 + \cos\theta}\right]_{\pi/2 - \epsilon}\right) = \frac{1}{4\pi G}\frac{v_c^2}{r}(1 + 1) = \frac{1}{2\pi G}\frac{v_c^2}{r}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (98), p. 25" />

A disc whose surface density falls as $1/r$ has a rotation speed $v_c$ that is the **same at every radius**: a flat rotation curve made by a disc alone (a remark not in the notes: in the plane, $v^2 = r\,\partial\phi/\partial r = v_c^2$).

<WorkedExample title="How dense is a Mestel disc?">
<Problem>
A Mestel disc has $v_c = 200$ km/s. What is its surface density at $r = 8$ kpc, in solar masses per square parsec? Use $G = 0.0043$ pc (km/s)² $M_\odot^{-1}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Surface density">
$\Sigma = \frac{v_c^2}{2\pi Gr} = \frac{(200)^2}{2\pi \times 0.0043 \times 8000} = \frac{40\,000}{216} \approx 185\,M_\odot$/pc². (In the real Milky Way, much of the mass that keeps the rotation curve flat is in the dark halo rather than the disc.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The NFW profile of dark matter halos

Simulations of cold dark matter find that halos of all masses share one empirical density profile, the **Navarro–Frenk–White (NFW)** profile:

<Formula label="NFW profile" tex="\rho(r) = \frac{\rho_0}{\left(\frac{r}{a}\right)\left(1 + \frac{r}{a}\right)^2} \propto \begin{cases} r^{-1}, & r \ll a \\ r^{-3}, & r \gg a \end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (99), p. 25" />

with a central **cusp** ($\rho \propto r^{-1}$) and a steeper outer fall-off. The simulations also show that $\rho_0$ and the scale radius $a$ are strongly correlated, so halos form approximately a **one-parameter family**. The parameter is usually the radius $r_{200}$ inside which the mean density is 200 times the critical density, $M_{200} = 200\rho_c\cdot\frac43\pi r_{200}^3$, and the shape is described by the **concentration**:

<Formula label="Concentration" tex="c = \frac{r_{200}}{a}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (100), p. 26" />

The central result is that $c$ depends only very weakly on mass. The corresponding potential is

<Formula label="NFW potential" tex="\phi = -4\pi G\rho_0a^2\,\frac{\ln\left(1 + \frac{r}{a}\right)}{\frac{r}{a}} + \text{constant}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (101), p. 26" />

[The notes omit $G$.]

## Problems on small scales

Comparing simulated halos with real galaxies has raised several puzzles, which the notes list:

- **Core–cusp problem.** Stellar dynamics suggests that many galaxies have a flat **core** in the centre (slope near 0) rather than the cusp (slope −1) of simulated halos. Supernova feedback may push dark matter out of the centre; alternatively, cold dark matter itself may need modifying. (The Ferreras page on the core–cusp problem goes further.)
- **Diversity of shapes.** Observed rotation curves show a range of shapes, some cuspier, some more cored, while simulated halos all follow the NFW form and look the same when scaled.
- **Missing satellites.** Simulations predict more small subhalos than there are observed satellite galaxies. Perhaps not every subhalo forms stars; "dark" subhalos might be found through gaps in stellar streams or through gravitational lensing. Improved surveys have since found many more satellites.
- **Too big to fail.** The largest predicted subhalos are deep enough that they should have gathered gas and formed bright galaxies, but there are too few such galaxies observed.

## Summary

- Stars move in a potential $\phi$ with $\vec F/m = -\nabla\phi$; Poisson's equation $\nabla^2\phi = 4\pi G\rho$ pairs each potential with a density.
- Point mass: $\phi = -GM/r$. Uniform sphere (from Gauss's theorem): $F \propto 1/r^2$ outside, $F \propto r$ inside, so stars oscillate through it with one period.
- The Mestel disc potential has no density off the plane: an infinitely thin disc with $\Sigma = v_c^2/(2\pi Gr)$ and a flat rotation curve.
- Cold dark matter halos follow the NFW profile, $\rho = \rho_0/[(r/a)(1 + r/a)^2]$, essentially a one-parameter family with weakly mass-dependent concentration $c = r_{200}/a$.
- Small-scale tensions with observations: cores versus cusps, diverse rotation-curve shapes, missing satellites and too-big-to-fail.
