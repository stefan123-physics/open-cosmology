The previous page derived the expansion law from a Newtonian sphere. This page follows the second half of the 8.902 Lecture 10 notes (section 1.B), which derive it properly from general relativity, then rewrite it in terms of the **density parameters** that observations measure. The 8.286 pages cover the geometry in much more detail; here it all fits in a few steps.

## The field equations

General relativity relates the geometry of space-time to its contents through Einstein's field equations:

<Formula label="Einstein's field equations with a cosmological constant" tex="G_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu} - \Lambda\,g_{\mu\nu}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (277), p. 65" />

- $G_{\mu\nu}$, the **Einstein tensor**, is built from first and second derivatives of the metric: it describes curvature;
- $T_{\mu\nu}$, the **stress–energy tensor**, describes the matter and energy;
- $g_{\mu\nu}$ is the **metric** itself.

The notes compare this to Poisson's equation of Newtonian gravity, $\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho$, with the potential $\Phi$ replaced by the curvature of space-time. [The notes add a term $\Lambda/3$ to Poisson's equation; the Newtonian limit of the field equations with $\Lambda$ gives $\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho - \Lambda c^2$.] To use the field equations we must first choose $g_{\mu\nu}$ and $T_{\mu\nu}$.

## Metrics

A metric gives the distance between nearby points, $ds^2 = g_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu$:

<Formula label="Space-time interval" tex="ds^2 = g_{\mu\nu}\,dx^\mu\,dx^\nu" cite="reading1" at="eq. (278), p. 66" />

The notes build up with examples. A flat plane in Cartesian and in polar coordinates:

<Formula label="Flat plane" tex="ds^2 = dx^2 + dy^2 = dr^2 + r^2\,d\theta^2" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (279)–(280), p. 66" />

The surface of a sphere of radius $R$, a curved 2D space:

<Formula label="Surface of a sphere" tex="ds^2 = R^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (281), p. 66" />

[The notes print $d\varphi$ without its square.] With the distance from the pole $\chi = R\theta$,

<Formula label="Sphere in terms of distance from the pole" tex="ds^2 = d\chi^2 + R^2\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\chi}{R}\right)d\varphi^2" cite="reading1" at="eq. (282), p. 66" />

and for an enormous sphere, $R \to \infty$, $\sin(\chi/R) \approx \chi/R$, which gives back the flat plane in polar coordinates:

<Formula label="The large-sphere limit" tex="\sin\!\left(\frac{\chi}{R}\right) \approx \frac{\chi}{R} \;\Rightarrow\; ds^2 = d\chi^2 + \chi^2\,d\varphi^2" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (283)–(284), p. 66" />

Flat space-time (special relativity) has

<Formula label="Minkowski space-time" tex="g_{\mu\nu} = \text{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1), \qquad ds^2 = -c^2dt^2 + dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (285)–(286), p. 66" />

## The Robertson–Walker metric

Homogeneity and isotropy fix the form of the metric up to one function of time, the scale factor, and one constant, the curvature $k$:

<Formula label="Robertson–Walker metric" tex="ds^2 = -c^2dt^2 + a^2(t)\left[d\chi^2 + f_k^2(\chi)\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\right)\right]" cite="reading1" at="eq. (287), p. 67" />

<Formula label="The three geometries" tex="f_k(\chi) = \begin{cases} k^{-1/2}\sin\!\left(k^{1/2}\chi\right), & \text{closed},\ k > 0 \\ \chi, & \text{flat},\ k = 0 \\ |k|^{-1/2}\sinh\!\left(|k|^{1/2}\chi\right), & \text{open},\ k < 0 \end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (288), p. 67" />

with $k$ in units of 1/length². [The notes write $a(t)$ for $a^2(t)$ and close the square bracket after $f_k^2(\chi)$, so that the angular factor would multiply $d\chi^2$ as well; the form above is the standard one.] A closed universe is the 3D version of the sphere above, with $\chi$ the distance from a chosen origin; an open one is its hyperbolic counterpart.

## The contents: a perfect fluid

The stress–energy tensor collects energy density ($T_{00}$), energy flux ($T_{0j}$), momentum density ($T_{j0}$) and the stresses ($T_{ik}$: pressure on the diagonal, shear off it). Homogeneity and isotropy allow no shear and only an isotropic pressure: a **perfect fluid**, which in the rest frame of a comoving observer is just

<Formula label="Perfect fluid in its rest frame" tex="T_{\mu\nu} = \text{diag}\left(\rho c^2,\ p,\ p,\ p\right)" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (289)–(292), p. 67" />

[The notes write the general form as $T_{\mu\nu} = (\rho c^2 + p)u_\mu u_\nu - (p/c^2)g_{\mu\nu}$ and the rest-frame entry as $-\rho c^2$, mixing the sign conventions of the two common metric signatures. For the $(-,+,+,+)$ metric used here the standard form is $(\rho + p/c^2)u_\mu u_\nu + p\,g_{\mu\nu}$, with energy density $+\rho c^2$ in the rest frame.]

## The Friedmann equations

Computing $G_{\mu\nu}$ from the Robertson–Walker metric and inserting it into the field equations leaves two independent equations, the **Friedmann equations**:

<Formula label="The Friedmann equations" tex="\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho - \frac{kc^2}{a^2} + \frac{\Lambda c^2}{3}, \qquad \frac{\ddot a}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left(\rho + \frac{3p}{c^2}\right) + \frac{\Lambda c^2}{3}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (293), p. 67" />

The first is the Newtonian energy equation of the previous page, with the curvature now written $-kc^2/a^2$ (so the Newtonian constant is $\tilde k = -kc^2$, and $\Lambda$ here has units of 1/length²). The second is new: **pressure also gravitates**, through $\rho + 3p/c^2$. For relativistic particles, bosons or fermions, $p = \rho c^2/3$; for non-relativistic matter $p = 0$.

## Critical density

The total density that makes the universe exactly flat, $k = 0$ (counting the cosmological constant as a density $\rho_\Lambda$ when there is one), is the **critical density**:

<Formula label="Critical density" tex="\rho_\text{crit} = \frac{3H^2(t)}{8\pi G}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (294), p. 67" />

Today, with $H_0 = 100h$ km/s/Mpc,

<Formula label="Critical density today" tex="\rho_\text{crit,0} = \frac{3H_0^2}{8\pi G} \approx 1.8\times10^{-29}\,h^2\ \text{g/cm}^3" cite="reading1" at="eq. (295), p. 68" />

In the Newtonian picture, a sphere filled with the critical density has kinetic energy equal to its gravitational binding energy:

<Formula label="Critical density as zero energy" tex="\frac{G\,\frac43\pi\rho_\text{crit}a^3}{a} = \frac{\dot a^2}{2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (296), p. 68" />

This is the borderline between an open universe and a closed one: a universe at exactly the critical density expands forever, but ever more slowly.

## Density parameters

Every density is measured against the critical density:

<Formula label="Density parameters" tex="\Omega_m(t) = \frac{\rho_m(t)}{\rho_\text{crit}(t)}, \quad \Omega_r(t) = \frac{\rho_r(t)}{\rho_\text{crit}(t)}, \quad \Omega_k(t) = -\frac{kc^2}{a^2H^2}, \quad \Omega_\Lambda(t) = \frac{\Lambda c^2}{3H^2} = \frac{\rho_\Lambda(t)}{\rho_\text{crit}(t)}, \quad \rho_\Lambda = \frac{\Lambda c^2}{8\pi G}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (297), p. 68" />

[The notes write $\Omega_k(t) = -kc^2/H^2$, which is only right today, when $a = 1$; in general $a^2$ belongs in the denominator.] The notes also define $\Omega(t) = \Omega_m + \Omega_r$. The values today carry a subscript 0 (eq. 298), and since matter, radiation, curvature and $\Lambda$ dilute as $a^{-3}$, $a^{-4}$, $a^{-2}$ and $a^0$,

<Formula label="Densities in terms of today's values" tex="\rho_m = \Omega_{m,0}\rho_\text{crit,0}a^{-3}, \quad \rho_r = \Omega_{r,0}\rho_\text{crit,0}a^{-4}, \quad \rho_k = \Omega_{k,0}\rho_\text{crit,0}a^{-2}, \quad \rho_\Lambda = \Omega_{\Lambda,0}\rho_\text{crit,0}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (298)–(299), p. 68" />

with $a = 1$ today. The matter is split into baryons and dark matter, $\Omega_m = \Omega_\text{dm} + \Omega_b$. The notes quote the Planck 2018 results:

<Formula label="Planck 2018 parameters, as quoted in the notes" tex="\Omega_{m,0} = 0.315 \pm 0.007, \quad \Omega_{\text{dm},0} = 0.264 \pm 0.003, \quad \Omega_{b,0} = 0.0493 \pm 0.0003, \quad \Omega_{k,0} = 0.0007 \pm 0.0019, \quad \Omega_{\Lambda,0} = 0.6847 \pm 0.0073" cite="reading1" at="eq. (300), p. 69" />

with $H_0 = 67.4 \pm 0.5$ km/s/Mpc. The radiation parameter follows from the measured CMB temperature together with the neutrinos: $\Omega_{r,0} \approx 10^{-4}$. Part III of the course shows how these numbers are measured (see the CMB pages). [The quoted $\Omega_{k,0}$ is the Planck result combined with baryon acoustic oscillations.]

<WorkedExample title="How empty is the universe?">
<Problem>
(a) Evaluate $\rho_\text{crit,0}$ for $h = 0.674$, in g/cm³ and in protons per cubic metre ($m_p = 1.67\times10^{-24}$ g). (b) How many of those are baryons?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The critical density">
$1.88\times10^{-29} \times 0.674^2 = 8.5\times10^{-30}$ g/cm³. Dividing by the proton mass, $5.1\times10^{-6}$ protons per cm³, which is about **5 protons per cubic metre**. (The more precise coefficient is $1.878\times10^{-29}$; the notes give 1.8.)
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Baryons">
Only $\Omega_{b,0} = 0.049$ of this is in baryons: $0.049 \times 5.1 \approx 0.25$ protons per cubic metre, one proton in every four cubic metres on average.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The Friedmann equation in its working form

Putting the densities into the first Friedmann equation, and using $\rho_\text{crit,0} = 3H_0^2/8\pi G$ and the definition of $\Omega_{k,0}$:

<Formula label="Rewriting the Friedmann equation" tex="H^2(t) = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\left(\rho_m + \rho_r + \rho_\Lambda\right) - \frac{kc^2}{a^2} = H_0^2\left[\Omega_{m,0}a^{-3} + \Omega_{r,0}a^{-4} + \Omega_{\Lambda,0} - \frac{kc^2}{a^2H_0^2}\right] = H_0^2\left[\Omega_{r,0}a^{-4} + \Omega_{m,0}a^{-3} + \Omega_{k,0}a^{-2} + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}\right]" cite="reading1" at="eq. (301), p. 69" />

where at $a = 1$ the equation itself requires $\Omega_{k,0} = 1 - \Omega_{r,0} - \Omega_{m,0} - \Omega_{\Lambda,0}$. The result is the form used everywhere in practice:

<Formula label="The Friedmann equation with density parameters" tex="H^2(a) = H_0^2\,E^2(a), \qquad E^2(a) = \Omega_{r,0}a^{-4} + \Omega_{m,0}a^{-3} + \Omega_{k,0}a^{-2} + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (302), p. 69" />

## Equality epochs

Reading the terms of $E^2(a)$ from the left: radiation dominates first, then matter, then the cosmological constant.

**Matter–Λ equality** happens when $\Omega_{\Lambda,0} = \Omega_{m,0}a^{-3}$:

<Formula label="Matter–Λ equality" tex="\Omega_{\Lambda,0} = \frac{\Omega_{m,0}}{a^3}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (303), p. 69" />

[The notes conclude $a \approx 1/2.3$ and $z \approx 1.3$. That is a slip: $2.17 \approx 2.3$ is the value of $a^{-3}$, not of $1/a$. Solving correctly, $a = (\Omega_{m,0}/\Omega_{\Lambda,0})^{1/3} = 0.77$ and $z \approx 0.3$, about 10 billion years after the Big Bang. Their remark that $z \approx 1$ corresponds to 6 to 7 billion years after the Big Bang is a little high (5.8 billion for these parameters).]

**Matter–radiation equality** happens when $\Omega_{r,0}a^{-4} = \Omega_{m,0}a^{-3}$:

<Formula label="Matter–radiation equality" tex="\Omega_{r,0}a^{-4} = \Omega_{m,0}a^{-3} \;\Rightarrow\; a = \frac{\Omega_{r,0}}{\Omega_{m,0}}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (304)–(305), p. 69" />

The notes give $a \approx 1/3700$, $z \approx 3700$. With the rounded $\Omega_{r,0} = 10^{-4}$ the formula gives $1/3150$; with the more precise $\Omega_{r,0} \approx 9.2\times10^{-5}$ (photons plus three neutrino species, for $h = 0.674$), $z \approx 3420$. Planck's full analysis (Planck 2018 VI) gives $z_\text{eq} \approx 3400$, so the notes' figure is a little high.

Finally, observations say that $\Omega_{k,0} \approx 0$: the universe is flat to good accuracy.

## Summary

- Einstein's field equations, $G_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4}T_{\mu\nu} - \Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$, need a metric and a stress–energy tensor.
- Homogeneity and isotropy give the **Robertson–Walker metric**, $ds^2 = -c^2dt^2 + a^2(t)[d\chi^2 + f_k^2(\chi)\,d\Omega^2]$, with closed, flat or open geometry, and a **perfect fluid** with density $\rho$ and pressure $p$.
- The field equations reduce to the two **Friedmann equations**; pressure gravitates through $\rho + 3p/c^2$, with $p = \rho c^2/3$ for radiation and $p = 0$ for matter.
- The **critical density** $\rho_\text{crit} = 3H^2/8\pi G \approx 1.9\times10^{-29}h^2$ g/cm³ (about 5 protons per cubic metre today) makes the universe flat. Density parameters measure everything against it.
- $H^2 = H_0^2E^2(a)$ with $E^2 = \Omega_{r,0}a^{-4} + \Omega_{m,0}a^{-3} + \Omega_{k,0}a^{-2} + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}$. Matter overtook radiation at $z \approx 3400$, and dark energy overtook matter at $z \approx 0.3$.
