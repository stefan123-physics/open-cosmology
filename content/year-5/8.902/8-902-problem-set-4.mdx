This set practises the lessons on [the Friedmann equations and the density parameters](/courses/8-902/friedmann-equations-density-parameters) and on [redshift and cosmic distances](/courses/8-902/observational-cosmology-distances). The three problems follow MIT's Problem Set 4 of Fall 2023, in our own words and in MIT's order. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

Several things are ours. MIT's sheet gives no points, so the points are ours. In Problem 1 we split MIT's parts into boxes and add the redshifts and $H(z_1)$ as numbers to check on the way. In Problem 2 the halo radius in part (b) is our addition. In Problem 3 MIT leaves several numbers open: the mass of a galaxy, the redshift of recombination, the radiation density and what "total mass" counts. We choose them, say so where they stand, and the boxes check our choices.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first; Problems 1 and 3 need a numerical integral, which you can do with any tool (a short program, a spreadsheet, a calculator with an integration key). Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it; type decimals, for example `0.25`, in the unit the box names. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `n*c/(H0*sqrt(Om))`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `H0` stands for $H_0$, `Om` for $\Omega_{m,0}$, `OL` for $\Omega_{\Lambda,0}$ and `sigma` for $\sigma$; `n`, `c` and `z` are typed as they are, and `sqrt` is the square root.

MIT's sheet gives no points. The points are ours: 35 for Problem 1, 25 for Problem 2 and 40 for Problem 3, 100 points in all; every split between parts is ours too. A part that asks you to show something has no box of its own: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## The model universe

As on MIT's sheet, every problem uses a flat universe with

<Formula label="MIT's parameters for the set" tex="\Omega_{m,0} = 0.3, \qquad \Omega_{\Lambda,0} = 0.7, \qquad H_0 = 71\ \text{km/s/Mpc}" cite="mit" at="Introduction, p. 1" />

unless a problem says otherwise. The notation is that of the lecture notes. The expansion rate at redshift $z$ is $H(z) = H_0E(z)$, with the Friedmann equation in the form

<Formula label="The Friedmann equation with density parameters" tex="E^2(a) = \Omega_{r,0}a^{-4} + \Omega_{m,0}a^{-3} + \Omega_{k,0}a^{-2} + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (302), p. 69" />

and $a = 1/(1+z)$ (reading 2, eq. (311), p. 70). In Problems 1 and 2 we use MIT's two parameters only: $\Omega_{r,0} = 0$ and $\Omega_{k,0} = 0$. Radiation would change the answers there by less than 0.1%. Problem 3 adds radiation (our choice), because one of its parts is about the time radiation gave way to matter.

Light travels at $c = 299\,792$ km/s, so $c/H_0 = 4222$ Mpc. A light ray covers the comoving distance

<Formula label="Comoving distance" tex="\chi = \int_{t_\text{em}}^{t_\text{obs}}\frac{c\,dt}{a(t)} = \frac{c}{H_0}\int_{a_\text{em}}^{a_\text{obs}}\frac{da}{a^2E(a)}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (314), p. 71" />

and in a flat universe an object of proper size $l$ at redshift $z$ is seen under the angle $l/D_A$, with the angular diameter distance $D_A = \chi/(1+z)$ (reading 2, eq. (318), p. 72, with $f_k(\chi) = \chi$).

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Relative redshifts and line-of-sight distances" points={35} mit="Problem 1">

A quasar's spectrum shows two absorption lines of doubly ionised silicon, Si⁺⁺, whose rest wavelength is 1206 Å. Each line is made by a galaxy that lies on the line of sight between us and the quasar. We observe the two lines at 4711 Å and 4836 Å. Call the nearer galaxy (the smaller redshift) galaxy 1, with redshift $z_1$, and the farther one galaxy 2, with redshift $z_2$. Both galaxies move with the Hubble flow: their redshifts are due to the expansion alone.

<Part label="a" points={12}>

Find the two redshifts (tolerance 0.5%), and the distance between the two galaxies in comoving megaparsecs, $\Delta\chi$ (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

The ratio of the observed to the rest wavelength is $1 + z$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Both galaxies lie on the same line of sight, so the comoving distance between them is the difference of their comoving distances from us: one integral of $1/E(z)$ from $z_1$ to $z_2$. The interval is short and $E(z)$ is smooth, so any integration rule with a few points will do.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-z1" label="$z_1 =$" value="2.906" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p1-z1" />

<Answer id="p1-z2" label="$z_2 =$" value="3.010" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p1-z2" />

<Answer id="p1-dchi" label="$\Delta\chi =$" value="99.6" unit="Mpc" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-dchi" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The redshifts follow from the wavelengths. The comoving distance from us to each galaxy is an integral over redshift; the distance between them is the part of that integral between $z_1$ and $z_2$.

<Step title="The redshifts">

$1 + z_1 = 4711/1206 = 3.9063$, so $z_1 = 2.906$; and $1 + z_2 = 4836/1206 = 4.0100$, so $z_2 = 3.010$.

</Step>

<Step title="The distance as an integral over redshift">

With $a = 1/(1+z)$, $da = -dz/(1+z)^2$, so $da/(a^2E) = -dz/E(z)$, as in the notes' flat case:

<Formula label="Comoving distance between the two galaxies" tex="\Delta\chi = \chi(z_2) - \chi(z_1) = \frac{c}{H_0}\int_{z_1}^{z_2}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}(1+z)^3 + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}}}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (334)–(335), p. 74, with Ω_r,0 = 0" />

</Step>

<Step title="The integral">

At the two ends, $E(z_1) = \sqrt{0.3 \times 3.9063^3 + 0.7} = 4.3107$ and $E(z_2) = \sqrt{0.3 \times 4.0100^3 + 0.7} = 4.4770$. A numerical integration (for example Simpson's rule) gives

$\displaystyle\int_{z_1}^{z_2}\frac{dz}{E(z)} = 0.023593$, so $\Delta\chi = 4222 \times 0.023593 = 99.6$ Mpc.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Even the trapezoid rule with only the two end points gives $0.10365 \times \frac12(1/4.3107 + 1/4.4770) \times 4222 = 99.64$ Mpc: the integrand changes by only 4% over the interval. For scale, the comoving distances from us are 6176 Mpc to galaxy 1 and 6276 Mpc to galaxy 2 (the same integral from 0).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={8}>

An observer in galaxy 1 receives the quasar light at the moment it passes through galaxy 1, which is when galaxy 1 absorbs it. What redshift $z_{21}$ does that observer measure for galaxy 2's absorption line? (Tolerance 1%.)

<Hint>

The light that galaxy 2 absorbed passes galaxy 1 on its way to us. Compare the wavelength of galaxy 2's line at galaxy 1 with its rest wavelength. How much does the universe stretch between galaxy 2 and galaxy 1?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-z21" label="$z_{21} =$" value="0.02653" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-z21" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Redshifts multiply, they do not add. The wavelength stretches by $a_1/a_2$ from galaxy 2 to galaxy 1, then by $1/a_1$ from galaxy 1 to us.

<Step title="Stretching in two stages">

The light absorbed by galaxy 2 had the rest wavelength $\lambda_\text{em}$ there. By the time it reaches galaxy 1 it is stretched by the ratio of scale factors (reading 2, eq. (311), p. 70), so

<Formula label="Redshift of galaxy 2 seen from galaxy 1" tex="1 + z_{21} = \frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{1 + z_2}{1 + z_1} = \frac{4836}{4711}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(B), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

$1 + z_{21} = 1.026534$, so $z_{21} = 0.02653$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The rest wavelength cancels: the observer in galaxy 1 needs only the two observed lines. Simply subtracting the redshifts, $z_2 - z_1 = 0.1036$, is four times too large: at galaxy 1 the universe was 3.9 times smaller than now, and the further stretching by that factor on the way to us multiplies every wavelength difference.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={15}>

Show that for a small redshift difference, $\Delta\chi$ of part (a) and $z_{21}$ of part (b) are related simply through the Hubble parameter $H(z)$ at the epoch of galaxy 1. Give $H(z)$ for this model as a formula in $H_0$, $\Omega_{m,0}$, $\Omega_{\Lambda,0}$ and $z$. Then find $H(z_1)$ in km/s/Mpc (tolerance 1%), and use the relation with $z_{21}$ from part (b) to estimate $\Delta\chi$ again (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

Over a short interval the integrand of part (a) hardly changes: take it out of the integral at $z_1$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write $z_2 - z_1$ in terms of $z_{21}$ and $z_1$, exactly, from part (b).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-hz" label="$H(z) =$" expr="H0*sqrt(Om*(1 + z)^3 + OL)"
  vars={{ H0: [50, 100], Om: [0.1, 0.5], OL: [0.5, 0.9], z: [0.5, 5] }}
  names={{ H0: "H_0", Om: "\\Omega_{m,0}", OL: "\\Omega_{\\Lambda,0}", z: "z" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "H0*(Om*(1 + z)^3 + OL)", hint: "That is H₀E², not H₀E: the Friedmann equation gives H², so take the square root." },
    { expr: "H0*(1 + z)", hint: "That is the expansion rate of an empty universe, whose scale factor grows in proportion to time. Here matter and the cosmological constant set the rate through E(z)." },
    { expr: "H0*sqrt(Om*(1 + z)^2 + OL)", hint: "The matter density grows as (1 + z)³, the number of particles in a shrinking volume. A term in (1 + z)² belongs to curvature, which is zero in this model." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-hz1" label="$H(z_1) =$" value="306.1" unit="km/s/Mpc" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-hz1" />

<Answer id="p1-dchi-approx" label="$\Delta\chi \approx$" value="101.5" unit="Mpc" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-dchi-approx" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Over a short stretch of the line of sight the expansion rate is nearly constant, and the two galaxies obey Hubble's law with the Hubble parameter of their own epoch.

<Step title="The Hubble parameter">

From the Friedmann equation with $\Omega_{r,0} = \Omega_{k,0} = 0$ (reading 1, eq. (302), p. 69):

<Formula label="Hubble parameter of the model" tex="H(z) = H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}(1+z)^3 + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (334), p. 74 (the integrand, with Ω_r,0 = 0)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The small-interval relation">

Take $1/H$ out of the integral of part (a) at $z_1$, and use $z_2 - z_1 = (1 + z_2) - (1 + z_1) = (1 + z_1)z_{21}$, which is exact:

<Formula label="Comoving separation for a small redshift difference" tex="\Delta\chi = \int_{z_1}^{z_2}\frac{c\,dz}{H(z)} \approx \frac{c\,(z_2 - z_1)}{H(z_1)} = (1 + z_1)\,\frac{c\,z_{21}}{H(z_1)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(C), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The proper separation at the epoch of galaxy 1 is $a_1\Delta\chi = \Delta\chi/(1 + z_1)$, so

<Formula label="Hubble's law at the epoch of galaxy 1" tex="c\,z_{21} \approx H(z_1)\,\frac{\Delta\chi}{1 + z_1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(C), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

This is Hubble's law, $v = Hd$, as the observer in galaxy 1 would write it: a small redshift $z_{21}$ means a recession speed $cz_{21}$, and $H(z_1)$ is the Hubble parameter of that time.

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

$E(z_1) = 4.3107$ (part (a)), so $H(z_1) = 71 \times 4.3107 = 306.1$ km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹. Then $cz_{21} = 299\,792 \times 0.026534 = 7955$ km/s, the proper separation is $7955/306.1 = 25.99$ Mpc, and $\Delta\chi \approx 3.9063 \times 25.99 = 101.5$ Mpc.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The estimate is 1.9% above the integral of part (a), 99.6 Mpc, because $H$ grows with $z$ across the interval and the estimate uses its smallest value. Taking $H$ at the midpoint redshift $(z_1 + z_2)/2$ instead gives 99.61 Mpc, within 0.01% of the integral: for small intervals the integral is that simple.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The size of extended baryonic halos from absorption statistics" points={25} mit="Problem 2">

Absorbing objects are scattered at random through the universe with a constant comoving number density $n$, and each one has the same constant cross-section $\sigma$ for absorbing the light of a distant quasar. By "constant" we take a fixed proper area (our reading): the objects are bound systems that do not expand with the universe.

<Part label="a" points={12}>

How many absorbers per unit redshift, $dN/dz$, does a typical line of sight cross? Give $dN/dz$ as a formula in $n$, $\sigma$, $c$, $H_0$, $\Omega_{m,0}$, $\Omega_{\Lambda,0}$ and $z$. Then say how it changes with $z$ in this model.

<Hint>

Think of the line of sight as a thin tube. Its comoving length per unit redshift follows from the comoving distance. What comoving area does one absorber of proper area $\sigma$ at redshift $z$ cover?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The notes convert between comoving and proper areas when they build the comoving volume element.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-dndz" label="$\dfrac{dN}{dz} =$" expr="n*sigma*c*(1 + z)^2/(H0*sqrt(Om*(1 + z)^3 + OL))"
  vars={{ n: [0.5, 2], sigma: [0.5, 2], c: [1, 3], H0: [0.5, 2], Om: [0.1, 0.5], OL: [0.5, 0.9], z: [0.5, 4] }}
  names={{ n: "n", sigma: "\\sigma", c: "c", H0: "H_0", Om: "\\Omega_{m,0}", OL: "\\Omega_{\\Lambda,0}", z: "z" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "n*sigma*c/(H0*sqrt(Om*(1 + z)^3 + OL))", hint: "That is nσ dχ/dz, which would be right if σ were a comoving area. A fixed proper area σ covers a comoving area σ(1 + z)², so a factor (1 + z)² is missing." },
    { expr: "n*sigma*c*(1 + z)^3/(H0*sqrt(Om*(1 + z)^3 + OL))", hint: "That puts the proper number density n(1 + z)³ together with the comoving path length c dz/H. The proper path length is smaller by 1 + z: c dz/[(1 + z)H]." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-trend" label="As $z$ grows, $dN/dz$ (for the model of this set):"
  choices={["decreases at every redshift", "increases at every redshift", "stays the same at every redshift", "rises to a peak near z = 1 and then falls"]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Count the absorbers in a thin tube along the line of sight. In comoving coordinates the absorbers keep their density $n$; the tube's comoving cross-section is the absorbing area converted to comoving units, and its comoving length per unit redshift is $c/H(z)$.

<Step title="Comoving length and comoving area">

From the comoving distance, $d\chi = c\,dz/[H_0E(z)]$ (reading 2, eq. (341), p. 75). An area at redshift $z$ is converted as

<Formula label="Comoving and proper areas" tex="A_\text{comoving} = \frac{1}{a^2}A_\text{proper} = (1 + z)^2A_\text{proper}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (339)–(340), p. 75" />

so one absorber covers the comoving area $\sigma(1 + z)^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The count">

The absorbers whose centres lie in a tube of that comoving cross-section and of comoving length $d\chi$ are hit, so

<Formula label="Absorbers per unit redshift" tex="\frac{dN}{dz} = n\,\sigma(1 + z)^2\,\frac{d\chi}{dz} = \frac{n\,\sigma\,c\,(1 + z)^2}{H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}(1 + z)^3 + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(A), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The same result follows in proper units: the proper density $n(1 + z)^3$ times $\sigma$ times the proper path $c\,|dt| = c\,dz/[(1 + z)H]$.

</Step>

<Step title="How it changes with z">

Write $dN/dz \propto (1 + z)^2/E(z)$. Its logarithmic slope is

$\dfrac{d\ln(dN/dz)}{d\ln(1 + z)} = 2 - \dfrac{3}{2}\,\dfrac{\Omega_{m,0}(1 + z)^3}{E^2(z)},$

and the fraction is never more than 1, so the slope is at least $\frac12$: $dN/dz$ **increases at every redshift**. From $z = 0$ to $z = 2$ it grows by a factor of 3.03. At large $z$, where matter dominates, it grows as $(1 + z)^{1/2}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The units: Mpc⁻³ × Mpc² × (km/s)/(km/s/Mpc) is a pure number, as a count must be. At $z \to 0$ the result is $n\sigma\,c/H_0$: the absorbers in a tube of area $\sigma$ and length one Hubble length, as in a static universe.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={13}>

Quasar absorption systems of heavy elements ("metal lines") at $z \sim 1.5$–2.5 come at $dN/dz \sim 2$. They are thought to belong to typical bright galaxies, of luminosity $L^\star$, whose comoving number density is $n \sim 0.001$ Mpc⁻³. Take $z = 2$ and find the cross-section $\sigma$ each galaxy needs, as a proper area in Mpc² (tolerance 2%). (Our addition:) if that area is a disc, what is its radius in kpc (tolerance 2%)?

<Hint>

Solve part (a) for $\sigma$. Work out $E(2)$ first.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-sigma" label="$\sigma =$" value="0.156" unit="Mpc²" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-sigma" />

<Answer id="p2-radius" label="$R_\text{halo} =$" value="223" unit="kpc" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-radius" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Turn part (a) around: the observed rate of absorbers and the known number of galaxies fix the area each galaxy must cover.

<Step title="The expansion rate at z = 2">

$E(2) = \sqrt{0.3 \times 27 + 0.7} = \sqrt{8.8} = 2.9665$, so $H(2) = 210.6$ km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ and $c/H(2) = 1423.4$ Mpc.

</Step>

<Step title="The cross-section">

<Formula label="Cross-section from the absorber count" tex="\sigma = \frac{dN/dz}{n\,(1 + z)^2}\,\frac{H(z)}{c}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(B), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

$\sigma = 2/(0.001 \times 9 \times 1423.4)$ Mpc² $= 2/12.810$ Mpc² $= 0.156$ Mpc².

</Step>

<Step title="The radius">

$R_\text{halo} = \sqrt{\sigma/\pi} = \sqrt{0.04970}$ Mpc $= 0.223$ Mpc $= 223$ kpc.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

That is more than ten times the radius of the bright stellar disc of a galaxy like the Milky Way (about 15 kpc, our value): the absorbing gas must fill a large, extended halo, which is the point of MIT's title. Without the factor $(1 + z)^2 = 9$ the area would be nine times larger, 1.41 Mpc², and the radius three times larger.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Baby pictures: the recombination surface and the horizon" points={40} mit="Problem 3">

The cosmic microwave background shows the universe at recombination, a "baby picture" of it. Compute the four quantities below.

Our values for this problem, since MIT's sheet leaves them open. Recombination happens at $z_\text{rec} = 1100$, the value in the notes (reading 3, eq. (396), p. 86). Radiation is included: photons at today's temperature $T = 2.725$ K plus three kinds of neutrinos give $\Omega_{r,0} = 8.24 \times 10^{-5}$ for $H_0 = 71$ km/s/Mpc. We keep space flat and ignore that $\Omega_{m,0} + \Omega_{\Lambda,0} + \Omega_{r,0}$ is then 1.00008. The critical density today is $\rho_\text{crit,0} = 3H_0^2/(8\pi G) = 1.399 \times 10^{11}\,M_\odot$ Mpc⁻³ (reading 1, eq. (295), p. 68).

<Part label="a" points={12}>

Find the comoving distance to the recombination surface, $\chi_\text{rec}$, in Mpc (tolerance 1%). Then find the angle that a sphere containing the mass of a galaxy subtends on the recombination surface, in arcminutes (tolerance 2%). Take the mass of a galaxy to be $M_\text{gal} = 10^{12}\,M_\odot$ (our value: a large galaxy with its dark-matter halo), and let the sphere contain the mean density of matter.

<Hint>

The comoving distance is an integral from 0 to 1100. If you integrate in $a$ instead of $z$, multiply the integrand top and bottom by $a^2$: it stays finite all the way to $a = 0$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The mass fixes the comoving radius of the sphere through today's mean matter density, since matter is conserved. In a flat universe a comoving size divided by the comoving distance gives the angle.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-chirec" label="$\chi_\text{rec} =$" value="13430" unit="Mpc" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-chirec" />

<Answer id="p3-theta-gal" label="$\theta =$" value="0.914" unit="arcmin" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-theta-gal" mistakes={[{ value: "0.457", tolerance: "2%", calc: "p3-theta-gal-radius", hint: "That is the angle of the sphere's radius. The angle a sphere subtends is that of its diameter, twice as large." }]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A region that holds a fixed mass of matter has a fixed comoving size. On the recombination surface it is seen under its comoving diameter divided by the comoving distance, since $l/D_A = [2R/(1 + z)]/[\chi/(1 + z)]$.

<Step title="The distance to recombination">

<Formula label="Comoving distance to recombination" tex="\chi_\text{rec} = \frac{c}{H_0}\int_0^{z_\text{rec}}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{\Omega_{r,0}(1 + z)^4 + \Omega_{m,0}(1 + z)^3 + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}}} = \frac{c}{H_0}\int_{a_\text{rec}}^{1}\frac{da}{\sqrt{\Omega_{r,0} + \Omega_{m,0}a + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}a^4}}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (314) and (334), pp. 71 and 74" />

Numerically the integral is 3.1802, so $\chi_\text{rec} = 4222 \times 3.1802 = 13\,430$ Mpc.

</Step>

<Step title="The size of the sphere">

Today's matter density is $\rho_{m,0} = 0.3 \times 1.399 \times 10^{11} = 4.197 \times 10^{10}\,M_\odot$ Mpc⁻³ (reading 1, p. 68), so

<Formula label="Comoving radius of a sphere holding a galaxy's mass" tex="R = \left(\frac{3M_\text{gal}}{4\pi\rho_{m,0}}\right)^{1/3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(A), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

$R = (3 \times 10^{12}/(4\pi \times 4.197 \times 10^{10}))^{1/3}$ Mpc $= 1.785$ Mpc, a diameter of 3.570 Mpc.

</Step>

<Step title="The angle">

$\theta = 3.570/13\,430 = 2.659 \times 10^{-4}$ rad $= 0.914$ arcmin, or 55 arcseconds.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Without radiation the distance would be 13\,491 Mpc, 0.5% more: radiation matters only in the last stretch near $z_\text{rec}$. Taking the radius instead of the diameter gives half the angle, 0.457 arcmin. The angle is much smaller than a degree: the seeds of single galaxies are far below the resolution of most CMB maps.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Find the angle, in degrees, that a region 200 comoving Mpc across subtends on the recombination surface (tolerance 2%). MIT notes that this is about the largest scale on which the universe today shows structure.

<Answer id="p3-theta-200" label="$\theta =$" value="0.853" unit="degrees" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-theta-200" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The size is already comoving, so divide it by $\chi_\text{rec}$.

<Step title="The angle">

$\theta = 200/13\,430 = 0.01489$ rad $= 0.853°$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The largest structures of today's universe thus span less than a degree on the CMB sky, about 1.7 times the Moon's apparent diameter (0.5°).

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="c" points={12}>

Find the total mass within the horizon at recombination. As MIT specifies, the horizon here is the proper distance $d_H$ that light has travelled from the beginning of the universe up to redshift $z$. Give $d_H$ in kpc (tolerance 2%) and the mass in units of $10^{18}\,M_\odot$ (tolerance 2%). By "total mass" we mean (our reading) all the mass-energy inside a sphere of radius $d_H$, matter and radiation, with $\rho_\text{tot} = \rho_\text{crit,0}E^2(z)$.

<Hint>

The horizon distance is the comoving distance from $a = 0$ to $a_\text{rec}$, multiplied by $a_\text{rec}$ to make it proper. The integral in $a$ of part (a) works here too.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Without the cosmological constant, which is negligible this early, the integral in $a$ can be done exactly. Try it as a check on your numerical value.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-dh-rec" label="$d_H =$" value="249.6" unit="kpc" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-dh-rec" />

<Answer id="p3-mass-rec" scale="1e18" label="$M_H =$" value="4.75" unit="10¹⁸ M☉" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-mass-rec" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The horizon is the comoving distance light has covered since the beginning, times the scale factor of the moment. The mass inside is the density of that moment times the volume of a sphere of that radius.

<Step title="The horizon">

<Formula label="Proper horizon distance" tex="d_H(z) = a\int_0^{t}\frac{c\,dt'}{a(t')} = \frac{1}{1 + z}\,\frac{c}{H_0}\int_0^{a}\frac{da'}{\sqrt{\Omega_{r,0} + \Omega_{m,0}a' + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}a'^4}}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (314), p. 71, with a_em = 0 (our own solution)" />

For $a_\text{rec} = 1/1101$ the integral is 0.06507, so the comoving horizon is $4222 \times 0.06507 = 274.8$ Mpc and $d_H = 274.8/1101$ Mpc $= 0.2496$ Mpc $= 249.6$ kpc.

</Step>

<Step title="The density and the mass">

$E^2(1100) = 0.3 \times 1101^3 + 8.24 \times 10^{-5} \times 1101^4 + 0.7 = 5.215 \times 10^8$, so $\rho_\text{tot} = 1.399 \times 10^{11} \times 5.215 \times 10^8 = 7.296 \times 10^{19}\,M_\odot$ Mpc⁻³. Then

<Formula label="Mass inside the horizon" tex="M_H = \frac{4\pi}{3}\,d_H^3\,\rho_\text{crit,0}\,E^2(z)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(C), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

$M_H = \frac{4\pi}{3} \times 0.2496^3 \times 7.296 \times 10^{19}\,M_\odot = 4.75 \times 10^{18}\,M_\odot$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

With $\Omega_{\Lambda,0}$ left out, the integral is $\frac{2}{\Omega_{m,0}}\left(\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}a + \Omega_{r,0}} - \sqrt{\Omega_{r,0}}\right) = 0.06507$, the same to all digits shown. Matter alone makes up 77% of the mass, $3.65 \times 10^{18}\,M_\odot$. If radiation were left out altogether, the horizon would come out 1.7 times larger and the mass about four times larger, $1.8 \times 10^{19}\,M_\odot$: at $z = 1100$ the universe left the radiation era only recently. The horizon mass is a few million galaxies' worth, and its radius subtends $274.8/13\,430$ rad $= 1.2°$ on the sky today (the whole horizon sphere about $2.3°$).

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="d" points={10}>

Find the redshift $z_\text{eq}$ at which the universe first became matter dominated (tolerance 1%), and the total mass within the horizon then, in units of $10^{17}\,M_\odot$ (tolerance 2%), with the same meaning of horizon and total mass as in part (c).

<Hint>

Matter takes over when its density equals that of radiation. The notes give the condition in terms of the density parameters.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-zeq" label="$z_\text{eq} =$" value="3640" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-zeq" />

<Answer id="p3-mass-eq" scale="1e17" label="$M_H =$" value="4.17" unit="10¹⁷ M☉" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-mass-eq" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Equality fixes the redshift. Then repeat part (c) at that redshift; at equality the total density is twice the matter density.

<Step title="The redshift of equality">

<Formula label="Matter–radiation equality" tex="\Omega_{r,0}a^{-4} = \Omega_{m,0}a^{-3} \;\Rightarrow\; a_\text{eq} = \frac{\Omega_{r,0}}{\Omega_{m,0}}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (304)–(305), p. 69" />

$1 + z_\text{eq} = 0.3/(8.24 \times 10^{-5}) = 3641$, so $z_\text{eq} = 3640$.

</Step>

<Step title="The horizon">

At $a_\text{eq}$ the cosmological constant is negligible, and the exact integral of part (c) becomes $\frac{2}{\Omega_{m,0}}\sqrt{\Omega_{r,0}}\left(\sqrt2 - 1\right) = 0.02507$. The comoving horizon is $4222 \times 0.02507 = 105.8$ Mpc, and $d_H = 105.8/3641$ Mpc $= 29.07$ kpc.

</Step>

<Step title="The mass">

The total density is $2\rho_{m,0}(1 + z_\text{eq})^3 = 2 \times 4.197 \times 10^{10} \times 3641^3 = 4.05 \times 10^{21}\,M_\odot$ Mpc⁻³, so $M_H = \frac{4\pi}{3} \times 0.02907^3 \times 4.05 \times 10^{21}\,M_\odot = 4.17 \times 10^{17}\,M_\odot$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Half of it, $2.1 \times 10^{17}\,M_\odot$, is matter: the mass of a large galaxy cluster. The notes quote equality at $z \approx 3700$ (reading 1, p. 69), with their own parameters; ours is 1.6% lower. The horizon at equality matters for structure formation: perturbations smaller than it entered the horizon while radiation still dominated, and the notes explain that a perturbation grows differently once it is inside the horizon (reading 3, p. 86).

</Step>

</Solution>
