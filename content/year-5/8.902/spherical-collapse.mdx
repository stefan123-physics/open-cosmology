The power spectrum describes the ripples while they are small. Linear theory assumes $\delta \ll 1$; once $\delta$ approaches 1, **nonlinear** evolution begins: matter falls together and forms gravitationally bound clumps of dark matter called **halos**, inside which galaxies later form. The full problem needs computer simulations, but one case can be solved exactly: a single, uniform, spherical overdensity. This page follows section 2.E of the 8.902 notes, which starts at the end of the Lecture 15 notes and continues in Lecture 16. [The Lecture 16 notes open by repeating p. 92 of the Lecture 15 notes; eqs. (423)–(425) are cited to Lecture 15.]

## The picture

Think of the dark matter as a collection of roughly spherical overdense clouds. Take one of them, a uniform sphere, sitting in an **Einstein–de Sitter** background (flat, matter-only, $\Omega = 1$). At first the sphere expands with the universe. But it contains more mass than average, so its own gravity decelerates it more strongly: it lags behind the background, reaches a maximum radius (**turnaround**) and then falls back in, eventually settling into a **virialised** halo.

This is the same physics as the closed universe in 8.286: by Birkhoff's theorem (Newtonian version: only the enclosed mass matters), an overdense sphere behaves like a small closed universe embedded in a flat one.

## Equations of motion

Measure everything relative to turnaround (subscript "ta"). The background obeys the Einstein–de Sitter Friedmann equation $H = H_0a^{-3/2}$. Define

<Formula label="Dimensionless variables" tex="x = \frac{a}{a_\text{ta}}, \qquad y = \frac{R}{R_\text{ta}}, \qquad \tau = H_\text{ta}\,t \quad (H_\text{ta} = H_0a_\text{ta}^{-3/2})" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (423)–(424), p. 92" />

so $x$ is the background scale factor and $y$ the sphere's radius, both equal to 1 at turnaround. Differentiating $x$ with respect to $\tau$, and using $H/H_\text{ta} = x^{-3/2}$:

<Formula label="Background expansion" tex="x' = \frac{dx}{d\tau} = \frac{1}{H_\text{ta}}\frac{\dot a}{a_\text{ta}} = \frac{H}{H_\text{ta}}\,x = x^{-1/2}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (424)–(425), p. 92" />

The sphere's radius obeys Newton's law, with the enclosed mass fixed:

<Formula label="Newtonian equation of motion for the radius" tex="\ddot R = -\frac{GM}{R^2} = -\frac{4\pi}{3}\rho_\text{ta}R_\text{ta}^3\,\frac{G}{R^2}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (426), p. 93" />

Write the sphere's density at turnaround in units of the background density then, which is the critical density $3H_\text{ta}^2/8\pi G$:

<Formula label="Overdensity parameter" tex="\rho_\text{ta} = \frac{3H_\text{ta}^2}{8\pi G}\,\xi" cite="reading2" at="eq. (427), p. 93" />

where $\xi > 1$ for an overdensity. (This $\xi$ has nothing to do with the correlation function of the previous page.) In the dimensionless variables the equation of motion becomes

<Formula label="Equation of motion and boundary conditions" tex="y'' = -\frac{\xi}{2y^2}, \qquad y'\big|_{x=1} = 0, \qquad y\big|_{x=0} = 0" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (428)–(429), p. 93" />

The conditions say that the sphere is momentarily at rest at turnaround and starts from zero size at the Big Bang. [In eq. (430) the notes repeat the equation with $3$ in place of $\xi$; $\xi$ is meant.]

## Solving it

The background is easy: $x' = x^{-1/2}$ integrates to

<Formula label="Background solution" tex="\tau = \frac{2}{3}x^{3/2} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x = \left(\frac{3\tau}{2}\right)^{2/3}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (431)–(432), p. 93" />

the familiar $a \propto t^{2/3}$. [Eq. (432) prints $x = (3/2)\,\tau^{2/3}$; the exponent applies to $3/2$ as well, as the next line of the notes shows; that line also writes $dx/dt$ for $dx/d\tau$.] For the sphere, multiply $y''$ by $y'$ and integrate once (energy conservation). Since $y' = 0$ at $y = 1$:

<Formula label="First integral" tex="y' = \pm\sqrt\xi\,\sqrt{\frac{1}{y} - 1}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (433), p. 93" />

with $+$ before turnaround (expanding) and $-$ after (collapsing). The notes check by differentiating again that this reproduces eq. (428) (eq. 434). Separating variables and integrating from $y = 0$ gives an implicit solution:

<Formula label="Solution before turnaround" tex="\tau = \frac{1}{\sqrt\xi}\left(\frac12\arcsin(2y - 1) - \sqrt{y - y^2} + \frac{\pi}{4}\right)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (435), p. 94" />

**Turnaround** is at $x = y = 1$, $\tau = 2/3$. Then $\arcsin(1) = \pi/2$ and

<Formula label="Overdensity at turnaround" tex="\frac{2}{3} = \frac{1}{\sqrt\xi}\,\frac{\pi}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \xi = \left(\frac{3\pi}{4}\right)^2 \approx 5.55" cite="reading2" at="eq. (436), p. 94" />

At turnaround the sphere is about 5.6 times denser than the background. **Collapse**: the motion is symmetric in time about turnaround, so the sphere returns to $y = 0$ at $\tau = 4/3$, when the background has

<Formula label="Scale factor at collapse" tex="x_c = \left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{2/3}\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)^{2/3} = 4^{1/3}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (437), p. 94" />

Substituting $y = \tfrac12(1 - \cos s)$ turns eq. (435) into the cycloid $\tau = \frac{2}{3\pi}(s - \sin s)$, the same curve as the closed universe in 8.286. (This parametric form is this page's own step; it is used for the plot below.)

## The linear theory's verdict: δ_c ≈ 1.69

What would **linear** theory have said about this sphere? At early times $y \ll 1$, and expanding eq. (435):

<Formula label="Early-time expansion" tex="\tau \approx \frac{8}{9\pi}\,y^{3/2}\left(1 + \frac{3y}{10}\right)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (438), p. 94" />

The density inside the sphere relative to the background is the turnaround value $\xi$ scaled by how much each has expanded since: the background density goes as $x^{-3}$, the sphere's as $y^{-3}$:

<Formula label="Overdensity relative to the background" tex="\Delta = \left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^3\xi" cite="reading2" at="eq. (439), p. 94" />

[The notes print $(x/y)\,\xi$ in eq. (439) but use the cube in eq. (440).] Equating the two expressions for $\tau$ at early times, $(x/y)^3 \approx \xi^{-1}(1 + 3y/5)$, so

<Formula label="Early-time density contrast" tex="\Delta = 1 + \frac{3y}{5}, \qquad \delta = \Delta - 1 = \frac{3y}{5}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (440)–(441), p. 95" />

Linear perturbations grow like the scale factor, so extrapolating this early $\delta$ to turnaround multiplies it by $a_\text{ta}/a = 1/x$, and to lowest order $1/x \approx (3\pi/4)^{2/3}/y$:

<Formula label="Linear density contrast at turnaround" tex="\delta_\text{ta} = \frac{\delta}{x} = \frac{3y}{5x} = \frac35\left(\frac{3\pi}{4}\right)^{2/3} \approx 1.06" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (442)–(444), p. 95" />

and at collapse, a further factor $x_c = 4^{1/3}$:

<Formula label="Critical linear density contrast" tex="\delta_c = \frac{a_c}{a_\text{ta}}\,\delta_\text{ta} = 4^{1/3}\delta_\text{ta} = \frac35\left(\frac{3\pi}{2}\right)^{2/3} \approx 1.69" cite="reading2" at="eq. (445), p. 95" />

[The notes write $\delta_\text{ra}$ for $\delta_\text{ta}$ in the middle step.] This is the key result. The real sphere has collapsed, with formally infinite density, at the moment the **linear** theory says $\delta = 1.69$. So we can use linear theory as a bookkeeping device: evolve the density field linearly, and wherever it exceeds $\delta_c \approx 1.69$, a halo has formed. The notes illustrate this with a one-dimensional sketch: as the linear field grows, more and more of its peaks poke above the $\delta_c$ line.

## Virialisation: 18π² ≈ 178

A real halo does not collapse to a point: the particles' random motions build up and the halo settles into **virial equilibrium**, $2T + V = 0$. Energy is conserved, and at turnaround there is no kinetic energy:

<Formula label="Energy at turnaround and after virialisation" tex="E = V_\text{ta}, \qquad E = T_c + V_c = \tfrac12 V_c \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V_c = 2V_\text{ta}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (446), p. 96" />

[The notes write $V_\text{ra}$ for $V_\text{ta}$.] The potential energy goes as $1/R$, so the halo's radius is **half** its turnaround radius, $y = 1/2$. Taking virialisation to be complete at the collapse time, when $x = x_c$:

<Formula label="Virial overdensity" tex="\Delta_V = \left(\frac{x_c}{y}\right)^3\xi = \left(\frac{4^{1/3}}{1/2}\right)^3\xi = 32\,\xi = 32\left(\frac{3\pi}{4}\right)^2 = 18\pi^2 \approx 178" cite="reading2" at="eq. (447), p. 96" />

A virialised halo is about 178 times denser than the mean background. That is why halo masses and radii are usually quoted as $M_{200}$ and $R_{200}$: the mass inside the sphere whose mean density is 200 times the mean (or the critical) density of the universe.

<WorkedExample title="A cluster that collapses today">
<Problem>
In an Einstein–de Sitter universe with $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc, a halo of mass $10^{15}\,M_\odot$, a rich galaxy cluster, finishes collapsing today. When did it turn around, and what are its turnaround and virial radii? (Take $\rho_c = 3H_0^2/8\pi G = 9.2\times10^{-27}$ kg/m³ and $M_\odot = 1.99\times10^{30}$ kg.)
</Problem>
<Step title="Turnaround time">
Collapse happens at twice the turnaround time, so $t_\text{ta} = t_0/2$. With $a \propto t^{2/3}$, $1 + z_\text{ta} = 2^{2/3} = 1.59$: turnaround was at $z \approx 0.59$.
</Step>
<Step title="Virial radius">
The halo's mean density is $178\rho_c$ (the background today is $\rho_c$ for Ω = 1). So $R_\text{vir} = \left(3M/4\pi\cdot178\rho_c\right)^{1/3} = 6.6\times10^{22}$ m $\approx 2.1$ Mpc.
</Step>
<Step title="Turnaround radius: a check">
$R_\text{ta} = 2R_\text{vir} \approx 4.3$ Mpc. Independently: at turnaround the sphere had density $\xi\rho_c(1+z_\text{ta})^3$, and $\left(3M/4\pi\xi\rho_c(1+z_\text{ta})^3\right)^{1/3}$ also gives 4.3 Mpc; the factor $32 = 4 \times 8$ in $\Delta_V$ is exactly the background dilution $x_c^3 = 4$ times the halo compression $2^3 = 8$.
</Step>
<Step title="Linear density today">
Its linear density contrast today is $\delta_c = 1.69$; at $z = 1$ ($a$ half as large) linear theory gave it 0.84. The true sphere was then still expanding (it turns around at $z \approx 0.59$), but already $\Delta \approx 3.3$ times denser than the background, i.e. $\delta \approx 2.3$; linear theory had long stopped being accurate. (From the parametric solution: $\tau = 0.471$, $s = 2.67$, $y = 0.946$.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="note" title="Beyond the simple model">
This note goes beyond the 8.902 notes. Real halos are not spherical or uniform, and the numbers change in a universe with a cosmological constant: $\delta_c$ depends only weakly on $\Omega_m$ (about 1.68 today), while the virial overdensity relative to the mean density rises to roughly 340 for $\Omega_m \approx 0.3$ (from the fitting formula of Bryan & Norman, ApJ 495, 80, 1998, eq. 6). Simulations also find that halos keep growing by accretion and mergers. Still, the spherical model's two numbers, 1.69 and ~200, remain the standard working definitions of "collapsed" and "halo".
</Callout>

## Summary

- Once $\delta \sim 1$ linear theory fails and halos form. The **spherical collapse model** follows one uniform overdense sphere in an Einstein–de Sitter background: it obeys $y'' = -\xi/(2y^2)$, expands, turns around and collapses.
- At turnaround the sphere is $\xi = (3\pi/4)^2 \approx 5.55$ times denser than the background; collapse happens at twice the turnaround time, when the background has grown by $4^{1/3}$.
- Linear theory would give $\delta_\text{ta} \approx 1.06$ at turnaround and $\delta_c = \frac35(3\pi/2)^{2/3} \approx 1.69$ at collapse: a region has collapsed when its **linear** δ reaches 1.69.
- The virial theorem halves the radius, giving a halo $\Delta_V = 18\pi^2 \approx 178$ times denser than the background, hence the common $M_{200}$ halo definitions.
