Lecture 2 of 8.902 asks what holds a galaxy together and how its stars move. The notes start from a definition: a **galaxy** is stars and dark matter, which behave as a collisionless fluid, orbiting together with gas, which does collide, in a gravitational potential they create together. The first tool for understanding such a system is the **virial theorem**, which this page derives and applies. (The notes then begin section 2.B on relaxation times, which the next page covers.)

## The virial

Take a system of particles (stars) with masses $m_i$, positions $\vec r_i$ and velocities $\vec v_i$. Define the **virial**:

<Formula label="The virial" tex="G = \sum_i \vec p_i\cdot\vec r_i = \sum_i\left(m_i\frac{d\vec r_i}{dt}\right)\cdot\vec r_i" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (28)–(29), p. 12" />

(The notes call it $G$; it has nothing to do with the gravitational constant.) Because $\frac{d}{dt}(\vec r\cdot\vec r) = 2\dot{\vec r}\cdot\vec r$,

<Formula label="The virial and the moment of inertia" tex="G = \frac12\sum_i m_i\frac{d}{dt}(\vec r_i\cdot\vec r_i) = \frac12\frac{d}{dt}\sum_i m_ir_i^2 = \frac12\frac{dI}{dt}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (30)–(32), p. 12" />

where $I = \sum_i m_ir_i^2$ is the moment of inertia about the origin. Its time derivative contains the forces $\vec F_i = \dot{\vec p}_i$ and the kinetic energy $T = \frac12\sum_i m_iv_i^2$:

<Formula label="Rate of change of the virial" tex="\frac{dG}{dt} = \sum_i\dot{\vec p}_i\cdot\vec r_i + \sum_i\vec p_i\cdot\dot{\vec r}_i = \sum_i\vec F_i\cdot\vec r_i + 2T" cite="reading1" at="eq. (33), p. 12" />

## Pair forces

Gravity acts between pairs. The force on particle $k$ is the sum of the forces $\vec F_{jk}$ from every other particle $j$:

<Formula label="Total force as a sum of pair forces" tex="\vec F_k = \sum_{j=1}^N\vec F_{jk}, \qquad \vec F_{kk} = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (34), p. 13" />

[The notes write the sum over i, and the vanishing self-force with a repeated index i; the index of the sum should be j, and the self-force is the term with j = k.] Arrange the $\vec F_{jk}\cdot\vec r_k$ in a matrix and split the double sum into the parts below and above the diagonal (eqs. 35–37). Since the forces are equal and opposite, $\vec F_{kj} = -\vec F_{jk}$, the part above the diagonal can be relabelled to look like the part below it (eqs. 38–39), and the two combine:

<Formula label="Combining the two halves" tex="\sum_{k=1}^N\vec F_k\cdot\vec r_k = \sum_{k=2}^N\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\vec F_{jk}\cdot\left(\vec r_k - \vec r_j\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (40), p. 13" />

Only the **separations** of the pairs matter. Now suppose each pair force comes from a potential $V$ that depends only on the distance $r_{jk} = |\vec r_k - \vec r_j|$:

<Formula label="Force from a pair potential" tex="\vec F_{jk} = -\nabla_kV(r_{jk}) = -\frac{dV}{dr}\left(\frac{\vec r_k - \vec r_j}{r_{jk}}\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (41), p. 13" />

so that

<Formula label="The force term in terms of the potential" tex="\sum_{k=1}^N\vec F_k\cdot\vec r_k = -\sum_{k=2}^N\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{dV}{dr}\,\frac{|\vec r_k - \vec r_j|^2}{r_{jk}} = -\sum_{k=2}^N\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{dV}{dr}\,r_{jk}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (42), p. 14" />

## Power-law potentials

For a power law, $V(r_{jk}) = \alpha r_{jk}^n$, the derivative is simple:

<Formula label="Power-law potential" tex="\frac{dV}{dr} = n\alpha r_{jk}^{n-1} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{dV}{dr}\,r_{jk} = nV" cite="reading1" at="eq. (43), p. 14" />

and the double sum becomes $n$ times the total potential energy, counting each pair once:

<Formula label="Force term for a power law" tex="\sum_{k=1}^N\vec F_k\cdot\vec r_k = -n\sum_{k=2}^N\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}V(r_{jk}) = -nV_\text{tot}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (44), p. 14" />

so (eq. 45, where the notes misprint the force as a letter I)

<Formula label="The virial equation" tex="\frac{dG}{dt} = 2T - nV_\text{tot}, \qquad \text{and for gravity}\ (n = -1,\ U = V_\text{tot}):\quad \frac12\frac{d^2I}{dt^2} = 2T + U" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (45)–(46), p. 14" />

## The time average

Average over a long time span $\tau$, written with angle brackets (the notes use a calligraphic T for this time span; it is renamed here to avoid confusion with the kinetic energy):

<Formula label="Time average of the virial equation" tex="\left\langle\frac{dG}{dt}\right\rangle = \frac{1}{\tau}\int_0^\tau\frac{dG}{dt}\,dt = \frac{G(\tau) - G(0)}{\tau} = 2\langle T\rangle - n\langle V_\text{tot}\rangle" cite="reading1" at="eq. (47), p. 14" />

For a system in a **steady state**, $G$ stays bounded, so the middle term vanishes for a long enough average:

<Formula label="The virial theorem" tex="0 = 2\langle T\rangle - n\langle V_\text{tot}\rangle, \qquad 0 = 2\langle T\rangle + \langle U\rangle\quad\text{for } n = -1" cite="reading1" at="eq. (48), p. 14" />

This is the **virial theorem**, usually written simply as $2T + U = 0$. Three assumptions went in:

- the force acts between **pairs** of particles;
- the pair potential is a **power law**, $V \propto r^n$;
- the system is in a **steady state**: the time-averaged $d^2I/dt^2$ is zero.

A consequence: the total energy $E = T + U = U/2 = -T$ is negative, as it must be for a bound system.

## How fast do stars move in a galaxy?

Apply $2T + U = 0$ to a galaxy of $N$ stars of equal mass $m$, total mass $M = Nm$, with a typical speed $v$:

<Formula label="Kinetic energy of the galaxy" tex="T = \frac12\sum_i m_iv_i^2 \approx \frac12M\bar v^2 = \frac12Mv^2" cite="reading1" at="eq. (50), p. 15" />

For a galaxy of size $R$, dimensional analysis gives the potential energy up to a factor of order one:

<Formula label="Potential energy, roughly" tex="U = -\frac{GM^2}{R}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (51), p. 15" />

so the virial theorem predicts

<Formula label="Virial speed" tex="Mv^2 - \frac{GM^2}{R} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R}}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (52), p. 15" />

In convenient units, $G = 0.0043\,M_\odot^{-1}\,\text{pc}\,(\text{km/s})^2$. With $M \approx 10^{11}M_\odot$ and $R \approx 10$ kpc:

<Formula label="Typical numbers" tex="v = \sqrt{\frac{0.0043\,M_\odot^{-1}\,\text{pc}\,(\text{km/s})^2 \times 10^{11}M_\odot}{10\,000\ \text{pc}}} \approx \sqrt{4\times10^4}\ \text{km/s} \approx 200\ \text{km/s}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (53), p. 15" />

in good agreement with the rotation speeds of the previous page.

<WorkedExample title="Weighing a galaxy cluster">
<Problem>
Galaxies in a large cluster move with typical speeds of about 1000 km/s, within a radius of about 1 Mpc. Use $v = \sqrt{GM/R}$ to estimate the cluster's mass.
</Problem>
<Step title="Rearranging">
$M = v^2R/G = (1000)^2 \times 10^6/0.0043 = 2.3\times10^{14}M_\odot$.
</Step>
<Step title="What it means">
The stars in all the cluster's galaxies add up to only a few percent of this. This kind of estimate is how Zwicky found the "missing mass" in the Coma cluster in the 1930s (see the [Ferreras page on galaxy clusters](/courses/fundamentals-of-dark-matter/galaxy-clusters)), one of the oldest pieces of evidence for dark matter. (Not in the Lecture 2 notes; it applies the same formula.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The ideal gas law from the virial theorem

The theorem also works for gas in a container, where the forces on the particles come from the walls. An ideal gas of $N$ particles at temperature $T$ has kinetic energy

<Formula label="Kinetic energy of an ideal gas" tex="K = \frac32NkT" cite="reading1" at="eq. (54), p. 15" />

(written $K$ here to avoid confusion with the temperature; $k$ is Boltzmann's constant). A wall element $d\vec A$ pushes on the gas with force $d\vec F = -P\,d\vec A$ (eq. 55). Summing $\vec F\cdot\vec r$ over the whole wall and using the divergence theorem,

<Formula label="Force term from the pressure" tex="-\frac12\left\langle\sum_i\vec F_i\cdot\vec r\right\rangle = \frac P2\oint\vec r\cdot d\vec A, \qquad \oint\vec r\cdot d\vec A = \int\nabla\cdot\vec r\,dV = 3\int dV = 3V" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (56)–(57), p. 16" />

so the wall term is $-3PV$. The time-averaged virial equation, $2\langle K\rangle + \langle\sum\vec F\cdot\vec r\rangle = 0$, then gives $2 \cdot \frac32NkT = 3PV$:

<Formula label="Ideal gas law" tex="NkT = PV" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (58)–(59), p. 16" />

[The notes reach this result with some unusual bookkeeping, writing the wall term as a potential with $n = 2$; the form above, with the wall forces put directly into eq. (33), is the standard one.]

## Summary

- A galaxy is a collisionless fluid of stars and dark matter, plus collisional gas, in its own gravitational potential.
- The virial $G = \sum\vec p_i\cdot\vec r_i = \frac12dI/dt$ obeys $dG/dt = \sum\vec F_i\cdot\vec r_i + 2T$. For pair forces from a power-law potential $V \propto r^n$, $\sum\vec F_i\cdot\vec r_i = -nV_\text{tot}$.
- For a steady state the time average of $dG/dt$ vanishes, giving the **virial theorem** $2\langle T\rangle = n\langle V_\text{tot}\rangle$; for gravity ($n = -1$), $2T + U = 0$.
- With $T \approx \frac12Mv^2$ and $U \approx -GM^2/R$, a galaxy has $v \approx \sqrt{GM/R} \approx 200$ km/s for $10^{11}M_\odot$ within 10 kpc. The same estimate for clusters reveals dark matter.
- Applied to a gas pushed by container walls, the virial theorem gives the ideal gas law, $NkT = PV$.
