8.286's dynamics assumed a smooth universe: it explained where the ripples come from, not how they grow. The CMB shows ripples of about one part in $10^5$, and today the universe is full of galaxies and clusters. This page starts a short sequence based on the lecture notes of **MIT 8.902 Astrophysics II** (Prof. Mark Vogelsberger, Fall 2023), which add small perturbations to the smooth cosmology and follow their growth. Unlike 8.286, the source here is written notes with numbered equations, which the citations below refer to.

## The ingredients

The Lecture 13 notes open mid-way through an overview of cosmic history, with a public-domain NASA/WMAP timeline: the earliest stages are opaque to photons, the later ones transparent and potentially observable. At early times the universe holds **matter** (dark matter and baryons) and **radiation**; the cosmological constant and curvature don't matter yet.

Non-relativistic matter obeys the equations of a self-gravitating fluid:

<Formula label="Fluid equations for non-relativistic matter" tex="\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\vec v) = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial\vec v}{\partial t} + (\vec v\cdot\nabla)\vec v = -\frac{\nabla p}{\rho} - \nabla\phi, \qquad \nabla^2\phi = 4\pi G\rho" cite="reading1" at="eq. (376), p. 82" />

These are the continuity, momentum (Euler) and Poisson equations. [The notes write $+\nabla\phi$ in the momentum equation, here and in eq. (377); with $\nabla^2\phi = 4\pi G\rho$ the gravitational force per unit mass is $-\nabla\phi$, as here.] For radiation the pressure joins the density: $\rho$ becomes $\rho + p/c^2$ in the continuity and momentum equations and $\rho + 3p/c^2$ in Poisson's equation, with $p = \rho c^2/3$ for pure radiation (eq. 377).

Some cautions from the notes. Dark matter is **collisionless**: it really obeys the collisionless Boltzmann equation, and the fluid equations hold only for its averages (the Jeans equations); there is no single velocity at each point, since streams of particles can cross. Still, the fluid description gives the correct growth on large scales, where pressure (or velocity dispersion) can be ignored.

## The perturbation equation

Write the density as a smooth background $\bar\rho$ plus a small **density contrast**

<Formula label="Density contrast" tex="\delta = \frac{\Delta\rho}{\bar\rho} = \frac{\rho - \bar\rho}{\bar\rho}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (378), p. 83" />

Linearising the fluid equations in an expanding background, with comoving coordinates $\vec x$ (physical $\vec r = a\vec x$), gives

<Formula label="Perturbation equations" tex="\ddot\delta + 2H\dot\delta = 4\pi G\bar\rho\,\delta + \frac{v_s^2\nabla^2\delta}{a^2} \quad\text{(non-relativistic)}, \qquad \ddot\delta + 2H\dot\delta = \frac{32\pi}{3}G\bar\rho\,\delta + \frac{v_s^2\nabla^2\delta}{a^2} \quad\text{(relativistic)}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (379), p. 83" />

where $v_s$ is the sound speed $c_s$ for baryons, the velocity dispersion $\sigma$ for dark matter, and $c/\sqrt3$ for radiation (eq. 380). Each term has a meaning: **gravity** ($4\pi G\bar\rho\,\delta$) makes overdensities grow; **pressure** ($v_s^2\nabla^2\delta$) resists; and **Hubble drag** ($2H\dot\delta$), the expansion, slows everything down.

Splitting $\delta$ into Fourier modes $\hat\delta(\vec k, t)$ of comoving wavenumber $k$ (eq. 381) turns $\nabla^2$ into $-k^2$:

<Formula label="Equation for one Fourier mode" tex="\ddot{\hat\delta} + 2H\dot{\hat\delta} = \hat\delta\left(4\pi G\bar\rho - \frac{v_s^2k^2}{a^2}\right) \quad\text{(non-relativistic)}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (382), p. 84" />

with $\frac{32\pi}{3}G\bar\rho$ in place of $4\pi G\bar\rho$ for radiation. [Eq. (381) of the notes writes $\hat\delta(\vec k, \delta)$; $\hat\delta(\vec k, t)$ is meant.]

## The Jeans length

In a static background ($H = 0$) the equation is $\ddot{\hat\delta} + \omega_0^2\hat\delta = 0$ with $\omega_0^2 = v_s^2k^2/a^2 - 4\pi G\bar\rho$ (eqs. 383–384). If $\omega_0^2 > 0$ the mode **oscillates** (a sound wave); if $\omega_0^2 < 0$ it **grows**. With the physical wavenumber $\tilde k = k/a$, the dividing line is

<Formula label="Jeans length" tex="\tilde k_J = \frac{2\sqrt{\pi G\bar\rho}}{v_s}, \qquad \lambda_J = \frac{2\pi}{\tilde k_J} = \frac{\pi\,v_s}{\sqrt{\pi G\bar\rho}} \propto \frac{v_s}{\sqrt{\pi G\bar\rho}}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (385)–(386), p. 84" />

(for radiation, $\sqrt{32/3}\,\sqrt{\pi G\bar\rho}/v_s$). Perturbations **larger** than the Jeans length $\lambda_J$ collapse under gravity; smaller ones are held up by pressure and oscillate. (The explicit form $\pi v_s/\sqrt{\pi G\bar\rho}$ is this page's own step; the notes give the proportionality.) The corresponding mass is the **Jeans mass**, $M_J = \frac{4}{3}\pi\bar\rho\lambda_J^3$ (notes, p. 85).

## Growth in an expanding universe

On scales well above $\lambda_J$ the pressure term can be dropped. For a flat universe, $\bar\rho$ is the critical density $3H^2/8\pi G$ (eq. 388), so

<Formula label="Growth equations for Ω = 1" tex="\ddot{\hat\delta} + 2H\dot{\hat\delta} = \frac{3}{2}H^2\hat\delta \quad\text{(matter dominated)}, \qquad \ddot{\hat\delta} + 2H\dot{\hat\delta} = 4H^2\hat\delta \quad\text{(radiation dominated)}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (389), p. 85" />

with $H = 2/(3t)$ in the matter era and $H = 1/(2t)$ in the radiation era (eq. 390). Try $\hat\delta \propto t^n$:

<Formula label="Power-law solutions" tex="n^2 + \frac{n}{3} - \frac{2}{3} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; n = -1,\ \tfrac{2}{3} \quad\text{(matter)}, \qquad n^2 - 1 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; n = -1,\ +1 \quad\text{(radiation)}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (391)–(392), p. 85" />

The $n = -1$ solutions are **decaying modes** that die away and don't matter. The **growing modes** give

<Formula label="Growing modes" tex="\hat\delta \propto \begin{cases} a & \text{matter dominated} \\ a^2 & \text{radiation dominated} \end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (393), p. 85" />

since $a \propto t^{2/3}$ and $a \propto t^{1/2}$ respectively. In general one writes $\delta = D\,\delta_0$ with the **growth factor** $D$, normalised to $D = 1$ today. Note the striking contrast with a static universe: there, gravitational instability grows **exponentially**; in an expanding one, Hubble drag tames it to a **power law**.

<WorkedExample title="Exponential versus power-law growth">
<Problem>
In a static medium with $\omega_0^2 = -4\pi G\bar\rho$ (no pressure), a perturbation grows as $e^{t/\tau}$ with $\tau = 1/\sqrt{4\pi G\bar\rho}$. In a flat matter-dominated universe it grows as $t^{2/3}$. Compare the growth over the time from $t_1$ to $8t_1$, taking $\bar\rho$ at $t_1$ for the static case. (Use $\bar\rho = 1/(6\pi Gt^2)$ for the matter era.)
</Problem>
<Step title="The static time scale">
At $t_1$: $4\pi G\bar\rho = 4\pi G/(6\pi Gt_1^2) = 2/(3t_1^2)$, so $\tau = t_1\sqrt{3/2} = 1.22\,t_1$.
</Step>
<Step title="Static growth">
Over $7t_1$: $e^{7/1.22} = e^{5.72} \approx 300$.
</Step>
<Step title="Expanding universe">
$(8t_1/t_1)^{2/3} = 8^{2/3} = 4$. The scale factor grows by the same factor 4, and so does δ.
</Step>
<Step title="Lesson">
The expansion cuts the growth from a factor of ~300 to 4. This slow, power-law growth is why the ripples need a head start, the subject of the next page.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A perturbation is described by its density contrast $\delta = (\rho - \bar\rho)/\bar\rho$; linearising the fluid equations gives $\ddot\delta + 2H\dot\delta = 4\pi G\bar\rho\,\delta + v_s^2\nabla^2\delta/a^2$ (with $\frac{32\pi}{3}G\bar\rho$ for radiation).
- Gravity drives growth, pressure resists, and Hubble drag slows it. Modes larger than the Jeans length $\lambda_J \propto v_s/\sqrt{\pi G\bar\rho}$ grow; smaller ones oscillate.
- Ignoring pressure, the growing mode is $\delta \propto a$ in the matter era and $\delta \propto a^2$ in the radiation era: power laws, not exponentials. One writes $\delta = D\delta_0$ with the growth factor $D$.
