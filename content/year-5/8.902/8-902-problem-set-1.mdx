This set practises the first two lectures: [magnitudes, surface brightness, profile laws and redshifts](/courses/8-902/galaxy-observations) (Lecture 1) and [the virial theorem](/courses/8-902/virial-theorem) (Lecture 2). It follows MIT's Problem Set 1 in our own words and in MIT's order: the light profiles of two galaxies (Problem 1), their redshifts, distances, sizes and luminosities (Problem 2), and the width of a spectral line and the mass it implies (Problem 3). MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

**Our own galaxies.** MIT's sheet works with the images and spectra of two real galaxies from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey. OCW marks the galaxy images as copyright of the Sloan Digital Sky Survey, with every right kept by it, and excludes them from its Creative Commons licence. Neither the images nor the spectra are used here, in any form. In their place this page gives the data of two galaxies that we made ourselves with Python (our choice): an image of each, built from a profile formula plus a flat sky and random noise, and a spectrum of each, built from a smooth continuum, one spectral line at a redshift we chose, and random noise. You do not need the images themselves: the tables below give everything each part uses, already measured from our images in the way MIT's sheet describes. How each galaxy was made is told in the solutions, after you have measured it. Every number in the tables is ours; only the steps, their order and a few constants of the sheet are MIT's.

A few things to know about the source. The sheet has printed page numbers; the problems are on pp. 1 to 3, cited below as "p. 1" and so on. The notes are cited by their printed page and equation number. The sheet asks you to read the data files with your own software (it suggests ds9 or Astropy) and to plot the profiles; here the data are tables, and you may plot them with any tool. Two steps of the sheet's Problem 1(A) are left out: the plot in bins one pixel wide (our table starts from bins 2 arcseconds wide, the sheet's part B), and refining the centre of each galaxy (our galaxies sit exactly on the centre of the bins; our choice). Two plots of the sheet have no box here: re-plotting the profiles in magnitudes per square arcsecond in Problem 1(C), and drawing each fitted model over the points in Problem 1(D); make them with any tool. The sheet writes the de Vaucouleurs radius as $r_\text{eff}$; this page writes $r_e$, as the notes do. For the calcium line the sheet gives a rest wavelength of 8545 Å; the strongest line of the Ca II triplet, the one meant, lies at 8542 Å, which this page uses.

Some choices are ours, each marked where it appears:

- every number in the tables, the redshifts, the calibrations of the images and the strength of the lines;
- the exact rules for the sky level, the fits and the line centres, so that everyone who follows them gets the same numbers;
- the Sun's absolute magnitude in the band of the images, which the luminosity of Problem 2(d) needs;
- the width of the lines of nearby stars in Problem 3(b): 90 km/s (MIT's sheet has 95 km/s for its own spectrograph);
- the split of the points between the problems and their parts (the sheet gives no points).

Some boxes are our additions, each marked where it appears: the number of pixels in a bin in Problem 1(b), the apparent magnitudes in Problem 2(d), and the ratio of mass to light in Problem 3(c).

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper or in your own code first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit named in the question. Most parts follow an exact rule given in the statement, applied to the numbers in the tables, so a correct calculation lands on our value; the tolerances leave room for rounding. Where a step is only rough (reading a peak from a coarse table), the tolerance is wider and says so.

Give a number answer to 3 significant figures and keep 4 in intermediate steps; an answer with only 2 significant figures may or may not be accepted. Where a box needs more digits, its label says so.

The points are ours: the sheet gives none. Problem 1 has 40, Problem 2 has 35 and Problem 3 has 25. Some parts ask you to compare or explain: their boxes are choices, and the solution gives the reasons. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The tools come from the notes. Magnitudes are logarithms of flux, brighter meaning smaller:

<Formula label="Magnitude difference" tex="m_1 - m_2 = -2.5\log_{10}\frac{F_1}{F_2}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.A, eq. (6), p. 5" />

The absolute magnitude $M_G$ is the magnitude the object would have at 10 pc, and the difference is the distance modulus:

<Formula label="Distance modulus" tex="m - M_G = 5\log_{10}\frac{d}{10\ \text{pc}}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.A, eqs. (14) and (15), pp. 7 and 8" />

Surface brightness $I$ is light per unit area of the sky, quoted in magnitudes per square arcsecond, $\mu = -2.5\log_{10} I + \text{constant}$ (notes, eq. (17), p. 8). The redshift of a line is $z = (\lambda_\text{obs} - \lambda_0)/\lambda_0$, and for $z \ll 1$ the recession velocity is $v \approx cz$ (notes, eqs. (24) and (26), p. 11). The subscript $G$ on $M_G$ names the band of the images, MIT's "G band".

## Problem 1

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Before the counts can be read as the light of the galaxy, the sky must come off. Estimate the sky level $S$ of each image as the mean of the counts in its three outermost rings (60 to 62, 70 to 72 and 78 to 80 arcseconds; our rule), in DN per pixel. Then plot $\log_{10}$ of the counts minus $S$ against $r$ for each galaxy (no box for the plot).

<Hint>

Far from the centre the counts stop falling: what is left there is the sky. Average the three outermost values of each column.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1a-s1" label="Sky level of Galaxy 1, $S$ (DN per pixel, 2 decimal places):" value="120.17" unit="DN" tolerance="0.02" calc="p1a-s1" />

<Answer id="p1a-s2" label="Sky level of Galaxy 2, $S$ (DN per pixel, 2 decimal places):" value="120.10" unit="DN" tolerance="0.02" calc="p1a-s2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The sky adds the same number of counts to every pixel, so it shows up as the level the profile flattens to far from the galaxy.

<Step title="The sky levels">

For Galaxy 1, $(120.2 + 120.3 + 120.0)/3 = 120.17$ DN per pixel. For Galaxy 2, $(120.0 + 120.1 + 120.2)/3 = 120.10$ DN per pixel.

</Step>

<Step title="Why it matters">

Near the centre the sky is a small part of the counts (120 of 729 DN in the first bin of Galaxy 1), but by 30 arcseconds the galaxy adds only about 3 DN to a sky of 120 DN. An error of 1 DN in $S$ there changes the light of the galaxy by a third, and it bends the outer profile on a logarithmic plot. That is why the sheet insists that the sky be taken off before anything is fitted.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The rings at 40 to 42 and 50 to 52 arcseconds are still a little above $S$ (121.2 and 120.4 DN for Galaxy 1): the galaxy is not quite gone there, which is why the rule uses only the three outermost rings. Even those may hold a trace of galaxy light; Problem 1(e) comes back to this.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

The table is already binned in rings 2 arcseconds wide, as the sheet's part B asks. About how many pixels does a ring from 10 to 12 arcseconds hold? Work it out from the area of the ring and the pixel scale, and compare with the table (our addition). Then say, in the second box, why each bin gives the median of its pixels rather than their mean.

<Hint>

A ring from $r_1$ to $r_2$ has area $\pi(r_2^2 - r_1^2)$ in square arcseconds; one pixel covers $0.394^2$ square arcseconds.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Think of what a single bright pixel in the ring does to each average: a foreground star, or a cosmic ray that hit the camera.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1b-npix" label="Pixels in the ring from 10 to 12 arcseconds:" value="890" tolerance="2%" calc="p1b-npix" />

<Answer id="p1b-median" label="The median is used because:" choices={["The median of many pixels has no noise at all, which the mean of the same pixels always keeps", "A few very bright pixels, from a star or a cosmic ray, pull the mean up but barely move the median", "The median always equals the light at the exact centre of the bin, where the radius is known", "The median takes off the sky by itself, so the sky level S need not be subtracted afterwards"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The number of pixels is an area divided by the area of one pixel; the median is chosen because it ignores outliers.

<Step title="Pixels in the ring">

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): pixels in a ring" tex="N_\text{pix} = \frac{\pi(r_2^2 - r_1^2)}{(0.394'')^2} = \frac{\pi(144 - 100)}{0.1552} \approx 890" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(B), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The table has 892: a ring drawn on a grid of square pixels holds a whole number of them, close to the area.

</Step>

<Step title="Why the median">

Binning many pixels together shrinks the noise, which is the point of the sheet's part B. A median does that too, and in addition a handful of very bright pixels (a foreground star in the ring, a cosmic ray) cannot pull it up, because it only asks which value sits in the middle. The mean would be dragged up by them. The median does not remove the noise (it shrinks it, about as $1/\sqrt{N_\text{pix}}$), it does not remove the sky, and it is the value of the middle pixel in brightness, not the light at the centre of the bin.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The outer rings hold thousands of pixels, so their medians scatter by only a few tenths of a DN even though a single pixel scatters by about 11 DN (the square root of the 120 counts of sky). That is why the three outermost rings of each image, used in (a), agree to within 0.3 DN.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

Turn the profiles into the usual unit of surface brightness, magnitudes per square arcsecond. Our calibration (our choice of numbers, the sheet's form): in the image of Galaxy 1 a star of magnitude 20 gives $N_{20} = 2400$ counts above the sky in all, summed over its whole image; in the image of Galaxy 2 it gives $N_{20} = 2550$ counts. Give the surface brightness $\mu$ of each galaxy in the bin from 10 to 12 arcseconds, using the sky levels of (a).

<Hint>

A source that gives $N$ counts in all has magnitude $m = 20 - 2.5\log_{10}(N/N_{20})$. Apply that to the counts that fall on one square arcsecond.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Counts per pixel divided by the area of a pixel in square arcseconds give counts per square arcsecond.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1c-mu1" label="Galaxy 1, $\mu$ at 10 to 12 arcsec (mag/arcsec²):" value="22.89" unit="mag/arcsec²" tolerance="0.02" calc="p1c-mu1" />

<Answer id="p1c-mu2" label="Galaxy 2, $\mu$ at 10 to 12 arcsec (mag/arcsec²):" value="21.91" unit="mag/arcsec²" tolerance="0.02" calc="p1c-mu2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The magnitude scale of eq. (6) compares any flux with the flux of the calibration star; surface brightness applies it to the light of one square arcsecond.

<Step title="The formula">

With $I - S$ the counts per pixel of the galaxy and $(0.394'')^2$ the area of a pixel,

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): surface brightness from counts" tex="\mu = 20 - 2.5\log_{10}\frac{I - S}{(0.394)^2 N_{20}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(C), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

in magnitudes per square arcsecond.

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

Galaxy 1: $I - S = 146.2 - 120.17 = 26.03$ DN, and $26.03/(0.1552 \times 2400) = 0.0699$, so $\mu = 20 + 2.89 = 22.89$ mag/arcsec². Galaxy 2: $188.0 - 120.10 = 67.9$ DN, and $67.9/(0.1552 \times 2550) = 0.172$, so $\mu = 20 + 1.91 = 21.91$ mag/arcsec².

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A fainter patch of sky has a larger $\mu$: Galaxy 1 is fainter than Galaxy 2 at this radius (26 against 68 DN), and its $\mu$ is larger by $2.5\log_{10}(67.9 \times 2400/(26.03 \times 2550)) = 0.98$ magnitudes. On the re-plotted profiles $\mu$ grows outwards, so astronomers often draw that axis upside down.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={14}>

The sheet compares each profile with the two standard laws of the notes:

<Formula label="Exponential profile" tex="I(r) = I_0\,e^{-r/r_s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(D), eq. (1), p. 2" />

<Formula label="de Vaucouleurs profile" tex="I(r) = I_0\,e^{-7.67\,(r/r_e)^{1/4}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(D), eq. (2), p. 2" />

Fit both laws to both galaxies by this rule (our choice): take $y = \log_{10}(I - S)$ for the fifteen points of the profile, and fit a straight line to $y$ by ordinary least squares, against $r$ for the exponential law and against $r^{1/4}$ for the de Vaucouleurs law, with $r$ in arcseconds. From the slopes, give the scale length $r_s$ and the effective radius $r_e$ for each galaxy, in arcseconds. Then say, in the last box, which law describes each galaxy better.

<Hint>

Take $\log_{10}$ of each law. Each becomes a straight line: in $r$ for the first, in $r^{1/4}$ for the second. What does the slope of each line contain?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The exponential law gives a slope $-1/(r_s \ln 10)$ in a plot of $\log_{10} I$ against $r$. The de Vaucouleurs law gives $-7.67/(r_e^{1/4}\ln 10)$ against $r^{1/4}$. To judge which law fits better, look at how far the points scatter about each line.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1d-rs1" label="Galaxy 1, $r_s$ (arcsec):" value="5.80" unit="arcsec" tolerance="2%" calc="p1d-rs1" />

<Answer id="p1d-re1" label="Galaxy 1, $r_e$ (arcsec):" value="11.5" unit="arcsec" tolerance="2%" calc="p1d-re1" />

<Answer id="p1d-rs2" label="Galaxy 2, $r_s$ (arcsec):" value="8.28" unit="arcsec" tolerance="2%" calc="p1d-rs2" />

<Answer id="p1d-re2" label="Galaxy 2, $r_e$ (arcsec):" value="58.2" unit="arcsec" tolerance="2%" calc="p1d-re2" />

<Answer id="p1d-which" label="The better description is:" choices={["Galaxy 1 exponential and Galaxy 2 de Vaucouleurs", "Both galaxies de Vaucouleurs", "Galaxy 1 de Vaucouleurs and Galaxy 2 exponential", "Both galaxies exponential"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each law is a straight line in the right variables, so a straight-line fit to the logarithm of the counts gives its two numbers, and the scatter about the line says how well it fits.

<Step title="The laws as straight lines">

Taking $\log_{10}$ of eqs. (1) and (2):

<Formula label="Problem 1(d): the two laws as straight lines" tex="\log_{10} I = \log_{10} I_0 - \frac{r}{r_s\ln 10}, \qquad \log_{10} I = \log_{10} I_0 - \frac{7.67}{\ln 10}\left(\frac{r}{r_e}\right)^{1/4}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(D), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

So a fitted slope $\beta$ gives $r_s = -1/(\beta\ln 10)$ for the first law and $r_e = \left(-7.67/(\beta\ln 10)\right)^4$ for the second.

</Step>

<Step title="Galaxy 1">

Against $r$ the slope is $\beta = -0.0748$ per arcsecond, so $r_s = 1/(0.0748 \times 2.3026) = 5.80$ arcseconds. Against $r^{1/4}$ the slope is $\beta = -1.808$, so $r_e = (7.67/(1.808 \times 2.3026))^4 = 11.5$ arcseconds. The points scatter about the exponential line by 0.17 in $\log_{10}$ (root mean square), a factor of about 1.5, with a clear bend: too high at both ends and too low in the middle. About the de Vaucouleurs line they scatter by only 0.036. Galaxy 1 follows the de Vaucouleurs law.

</Step>

<Step title="Galaxy 2">

Against $r$ the slope is $\beta = -0.0524$ per arcsecond, so $r_s = 8.28$ arcseconds; against $r^{1/4}$ it is $\beta = -1.206$, so $r_e = 58.2$ arcseconds. Now the exponential line fits better (scatter 0.047 against 0.093), and the de Vaucouleurs fit gives an "effective radius" far beyond the light we measured, a sign that the law does not describe this galaxy. Galaxy 2 follows the exponential law: it looks like a disc galaxy, and Galaxy 1 like an elliptical (notes, Section 1.B, p. 9).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At the centre of the profile, $r = 15$ arcseconds, the de Vaucouleurs fit of Galaxy 1 predicts $10^{4.678 - 1.808 \times 15^{1/4}} = 13.2$ DN; the table has $133.3 - 120.17 = 13.1$ DN. The exponential fit of Galaxy 2 predicts $10^{2.451 - 0.0524 \times 15} = 46.2$ DN at $r = 15$; the table has $163.8 - 120.10 = 43.7$ DN, a little lower, a first hint of what (e) is about.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Answer id="p1e-break" label="The single law fails:" choices={["For Galaxy 1, whose outer bins lie a factor of 1.4 above its line, because its outer disc is warped and flared", "For both galaxies at all radii, because neither law fits real galaxies", "Only far out, where the sky dominates, so the sky level was wrong for both", "For Galaxy 2, whose innermost bin lies a factor of 1.4 above its line, because of a central bulge"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A single law leaves a pattern in the residuals where a second component adds light. Disc galaxies have a bulge at their centre, with a profile close to de Vaucouleurs (notes, Section 1.B, p. 9).

<Step title="Galaxy 2">

The innermost bin of Galaxy 2 lies $0.149$ above the exponential line in $\log_{10}$, a factor of $10^{0.149} = 1.41$, while the other bins scatter by a few hundredths. The line is also pulled up by that bin, so most of the bins out to 15 arcseconds lie a little below it (by up to 0.04). The galaxy has more light at its centre than its disc provides: a **bulge**, a small, dense, round component of mostly old stars. A fit with two components, an exponential disc plus a de Vaucouleurs bulge, would describe the whole profile; fitting the exponential law to the outer bins only would come closer to the disc alone.

</Step>

<Step title="Galaxy 1">

The de Vaucouleurs line fits Galaxy 1 to within 0.09 in $\log_{10}$ in every bin, so one law is adequate. Its innermost bin lies 0.085 below the line: the law is very steep at the centre, and the median of a 2-arcsecond bin cannot follow the peak. The last bins lie up to 0.06 below the line: the sky level of (a) still holds a little light of the galaxy's faint, far-reaching outskirts, so subtracting it takes off slightly too much where the galaxy is faintest. With real images the blurring of the centre by the atmosphere (seeing) adds to the first effect.

</Step>

<Step title="How we made the galaxies (our choice)">

Galaxy 1 is a pure de Vaucouleurs law with $r_e = 10.0$ arcseconds and 31.0 DN per pixel at $r_e$. Galaxy 2 is an exponential disc with $I_0 = 230$ DN per pixel and $r_s = 9.0$ arcseconds, plus a de Vaucouleurs bulge with $r_e = 2.0$ arcseconds and 80 DN per pixel at its $r_e$. Both sit on a sky of 120.0 DN per pixel, and each pixel got random noise of about the square root of its counts. Your fits came out at 11.5 and 8.28 arcseconds rather than 10.0 and 9.0: the small error of the sky level, the medians of wide bins and, for Galaxy 2, the bulge all move a fit. Real measurements have the same problems.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
