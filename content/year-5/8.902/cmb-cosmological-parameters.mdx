The previous page explained why the CMB power spectrum has a plateau, a series of acoustic peaks and a damping tail. Their exact positions and heights depend on what the universe is made of, which turns the CMB into a measuring instrument. This page follows section 1.C of the 8.902 notes, which the Lecture 17 notes begin and the Lecture 18 notes repeat and complete. (Citations below use the Lecture 18 PDF.)

## Three parameters from two peaks

The notes' sketch reduces the idea to three arrows on the first two peaks:

- the **position** of the first peak (a horizontal shift) measures the curvature, $\Omega_k$;
- the **height** of the first peak measures the matter density, $\Omega_m$;
- the **difference in height** between the first and second peaks measures the baryon density, $\Omega_b$.

Each is explained in turn below. The full analysis of real data fits all the peaks at once with a numerical model, but these three effects are the heart of it.

## The curvature from the position of the first peak

The first peak is set by the sound horizon at recombination, a length fixed by early physics: how far sound could travel by then. Its true size does not depend much on $\Omega_k$. What does depend on curvature is how large that length **looks** from here, $13.8$ billion years later: the angular diameter distance to the last scattering surface.

- In an **open** (negatively curved) universe the ruler appears **smaller** on the sky, and the first peak moves to **larger** $\ell$.
- In a **closed** universe it appears **larger**, and the first peak moves to **smaller** $\ell$.

[This is the same geometry as on the 8.286 page on open, closed and flat universes: the angles of a triangle add up to less than 180° in an open universe and more in a closed one. In the usual convention $\Omega_k > 0$ for an open universe and $\Omega_k < 0$ for a closed one.] Since $\theta \sim 180^\circ/\ell$, the multipole of the first peak scales inversely with the apparent size of the ruler. The observed first peak sits where a flat universe puts it. (Not in the notes: this is the measurement, by balloon experiments around 2000 and then WMAP, that established that the universe is close to flat.)

<WorkedExample title="Moving the first peak">
<Problem>
The sound horizon at recombination appears at an angle $\theta_1$ on the sky, and the first peak sits at $\ell_1 \approx 180^\circ/\theta_1$. Suppose a model with the same early physics has a curvature that makes the last scattering surface look 10% closer (its angular diameter distance is 0.9 times as large). Where does the first peak move, if it was at $\ell_1 = 220$ before? Is this model open or closed?
</Problem>
<Step title="The angle">
The angle is the ruler's size divided by the angular diameter distance, so it grows by $1/0.9 = 1.11$.
</Step>
<Step title="The multipole">
$\ell_1$ scales as $1/\theta_1$, so it shrinks by a factor 0.9: from $220$ to $0.9 \times 220 = 198$. The peak moves to **smaller** $\ell$ because the ruler looks bigger, which is what a **closed** universe does.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The matter density from the height of the first peak

A first guess would be that more matter means more gravity, deeper wells and so **bigger** peaks. The notes stress that this is wrong: a timing effect matters more.

Compare two universes with matter densities $\Omega_1 > \Omega_2$. For the same Hubble constant today, the universe with more matter decelerates more strongly, so it reaches any given redshift, and in particular the redshift of recombination, **sooner** after the Big Bang. The notes draw the two curves $a(t)$ meeting at the same scale factor at different ages, $t_1 < t_2$. With less time to form structures, the universe with more matter has a **smaller** first peak.

[The notes give only this timing argument. The standard detailed treatments describe the same trend through matter–radiation equality: with more matter, equality comes earlier, and the boost that the photons get from decaying potentials while radiation still dominates is weaker.]

Once the first peak has given both $\Omega_m$ and $\Omega_k$, the vacuum energy follows too, since the density parameters add up to one: $\Omega_\Lambda = 1 - \Omega_m - \Omega_k$ (ignoring the tiny radiation term). For a flat universe, $\Omega_k = 0$ and $\Omega_\Lambda = 1 - \Omega_m$. [The notes put this as "assuming flat universe"; the sum rule itself holds for any curvature.]

<WorkedExample title="How much younger?">
<Problem>
In a matter-dominated universe the age at redshift $z$ is $t = 2/\big(3H_0\sqrt{\Omega_m}\,(1+z)^{3/2}\big)$ (for $z$ large enough that curvature and vacuum energy no longer matter). By how much does recombination come earlier if $\Omega_m$ is doubled, with $H_0$ the same?
</Problem>
<Step title="The scaling">
At a fixed redshift, $t \propto \Omega_m^{-1/2}$. Doubling $\Omega_m$ divides $t_\text{rec}$ by $\sqrt2 = 1.41$, so recombination comes about 29% earlier (the factor $1/\sqrt2 = 0.71$).
</Step>
<Step title="Why this matters">
This is the notes' picture in numbers: with more matter there is less time before recombination, and according to the notes, a smaller first peak. (This simple formula ignores radiation, which matters near recombination; the full calculation is numerical.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The baryon density from the second peak

Changing $\Omega_b$ changes the first peak in a way that can be mimicked by changing $\Omega_m$: the two are **degenerate**. The second peak breaks the tie. ($\Omega_b$ can also be measured independently from Big Bang nucleosynthesis, the subject of Lecture 20.)

The notes use a mechanical picture. The photon–baryon fluid is like a **mass on a spring**: gravity of the dark matter well pulls the mass down, and photon pressure is the spring that pushes back. Adding baryons is like hanging a heavier mass on the same spring (**baryon loading**):

- the heavier mass falls **deeper** into the well;
- but it rebounds only to the **same** position as before.

The odd-numbered peaks come from compression, from how deep the fluid falls into the wells, so more baryons **enhance** them. The even-numbered peaks come from rarefaction, which is not enhanced. So with more baryons the second peak is lower **relative to** the first, and the ratio of the two peaks measures $\Omega_b$.

A simple model, not in the notes but often used to explain this, writes the oscillation of the fluid's temperature as a cosine about a shifted zero point, $(1+R)\cos(\pi\ell/\ell_1) - R$, where $R$ measures the baryon loading (in the full theory, $R = 3\rho_b/4\rho_\gamma$). At the compression peaks ($\ell = \ell_1, 3\ell_1, \ldots$) the cosine is $-1$ and the square of the amplitude is $(1+2R)^2$; at the rarefaction peaks ($2\ell_1, 4\ell_1, \ldots$) it is $+1$ and the square is just $1$. Without baryons ($R = 0$) all peaks would be equally high; the more baryons, the larger the difference.

<Callout kind="note" title="What Planck measured">
The Lecture 18 notes reproduce the Planck satellite's 2018 temperature power spectrum (Planck 2018 V, Fig. 57). In it the first peak, near $\ell \approx 220$, rises to almost 6000 μK², while the second and third peaks reach only about 2500 μK², a ratio of about 2.3. (The toy model above would need $R \approx 0.26$ to match it; the real baryon loading at recombination is about 0.6, because the toy leaves out the driving and damping that also shape the peaks.) Fitting the temperature, polarization and lensing data together (Planck 2018 VI) gives about 5% of the critical density in baryons and about 31% in all matter, and a universe close to flat; combined with baryon acoustic oscillations in the galaxy distribution, the curvature is below a few tenths of a percent.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The CMB peaks act as a measuring instrument: the notes read three parameters from the first two peaks.
- **Curvature, $\Omega_k$:** the sound horizon is a ruler of known size. An open universe makes it look smaller (first peak at larger $\ell$), a closed one larger (smaller $\ell$). The observed position says the universe is close to flat.
- **Matter, $\Omega_m$:** more matter means a younger universe at recombination ($t \propto \Omega_m^{-1/2}$ when matter dominates), less time to grow structure and, according to the notes, a lower first peak. With $\Omega_k$ and $\Omega_m$ known, $\Omega_\Lambda = 1 - \Omega_m - \Omega_k$.
- **Baryons, $\Omega_b$:** baryon loading is like a heavier mass on a spring. It deepens the compressions, so the odd peaks grow relative to the even ones, and the ratio of the first to the second peak measures $\Omega_b$, independently checked by Big Bang nucleosynthesis.
