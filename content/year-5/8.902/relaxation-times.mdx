The virial theorem gave a first picture of how a galaxy holds together. Lecture 3 of 8.902 (section 2.B of the notes, with the end of 2.A, which the previous page covered) asks a sharper question: **do stars in a galaxy ever collide**, or deflect each other noticeably? If they hardly ever do, a galaxy can be described as a smooth density field moving in a smooth gravitational potential, which is what the rest of the course assumes. The answer is a resounding no: they almost never do. The page ends with the processes by which galaxies relax anyway (section 2.C).

## One encounter

Follow a "subject" star of mass $m$ moving at speed $v$ past a "field" star of the same mass, with **impact parameter** $b$ (the distance of closest approach on a straight path). Assume the deflection is small, $|\delta\vec v|/|\vec v| \ll 1$, and the field star stays put. The pulls along the path cancel between approach and departure, so only the perpendicular force matters. With $x = vt$ along the path:

<Formula label="Perpendicular force during the encounter" tex="F_\perp = \frac{Gm^2}{b^2 + x^2}\cos\theta = \frac{Gm^2b}{(b^2 + x^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{Gm^2}{b^2}\left[1 + \left(\frac{vt}{b}\right)^2\right]^{-3/2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (60), p. 16" />

Integrating Newton's law over the whole encounter, with $s = vt/b$:

<Formula label="Velocity kick" tex="\delta v = \frac1m\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dt\,F_\perp = \frac{Gm}{b^2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{dt}{\left[1 + (vt/b)^2\right]^{3/2}} = \frac{Gm}{bv}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{ds}{(1 + s^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{2Gm}{bv}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (61), p. 17" />

(the last integral equals 2). This is roughly the acceleration at closest approach, $Gm/b^2$, times the time it acts, $2b/v$.

## Strong encounters are almost impossible

An encounter is **strong** when the kick is as large as the speed itself, $\delta v \sim v$, which turns the star's path by about 90° (and breaks the small-deflection approximation). That happens inside an impact parameter

<Formula label="Strong-encounter radius" tex="\delta v \sim v \;\Leftrightarrow\; b \lesssim b_{90} \equiv b_\text{strong}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (62), p. 17" />

From $2Gm/(bv) = v$, $b_\text{strong} \approx 2Gm/v^2$. [The notes write $GM/v^2$; the mass that deflects the star is that of a single star, $m$, and the factor 2 is needed for the result $2R/N$ below.] The cross-section is

<Formula label="Strong-encounter cross-section" tex="\sigma_\text{strong} = \pi b_\text{strong}^2" cite="reading1" at="eq. (63), p. 17" />

The virial theorem relates $v$ to the whole galaxy, $v^2 \sim GM/R = GNm/R$, so

<Formula label="Strong encounters in terms of the galaxy" tex="v^2 \sim \frac{GNm}{R} \;\Rightarrow\; b_\text{strong} \approx \frac{2R}{N_*}, \qquad \sigma_\text{strong} \approx \frac{4\pi}{N_*^2}R^2" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (64)–(65), p. 17" />

tiny, since $N \sim 10^{11}$. The probability of a strong encounter while crossing the galaxy once, through a mean density $n$ of stars, is

<Formula label="Probability of a strong encounter per crossing" tex="p = n\,\sigma_\text{strong}\,R = \frac{N}{\frac43\pi R^3}\,\frac{4\pi R^2}{N^2}\,R = \frac3N \sim 10^{-11}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (66), p. 17" />

Practically never. In dense **globular clusters**, with $N \sim 10^4$ stars, strong encounters are far more frequent, though still rare for any one star.

## Many weak encounters

Weak encounters happen constantly, though. Each nudges the star's velocity a little, in a random direction, so the star performs a **random walk** in velocity. The squares of independent kicks add:

<Formula label="Kicks add in quadrature" tex="\delta v_\text{tot}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^N(\delta v_i)^2" cite="reading1" at="eq. (67), p. 18" />

In a time $\Delta t$ the star sweeps out a cylinder of length $v\Delta t$. The number of field stars at impact parameters between $b$ and $b + db$ is the volume of a thin shell of that cylinder times the density:

<Formula label="Number of encounters at impact parameter b (area times length times density)" tex="N = (2\pi b\,db)\,(v\Delta t)\,n" cite="reading1" at="eq. (68), p. 18" />

Adding up the squared kicks from all impact parameters:

<Formula label="Total squared kick" tex="\sum_i(\delta v_i)^2 = \int_{b_\text{min}}^{b_\text{max}}\left(2\pi v\,\Delta t\,n\,b\,db\right)\left(\frac{2Gm}{bv}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G^2m^2n}{v}\,\Delta t\int_{b_\text{min}}^{b_\text{max}}\frac{db}{b}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (69), p. 18" />

Every factor of ten in $b$ contributes equally. The largest impact parameter is the size of the galaxy, $b_\text{max} \approx R \approx 10$ kpc; below $b_\text{min} \approx b_\text{strong}$ the encounters are strong, not weak:

<Formula label="The Coulomb logarithm" tex="b_\text{max} \approx R \approx 10\ \text{kpc}, \quad b_\text{min} \approx b_\text{strong} = \frac{2R}{N_*} \approx 10^{-10}\ \text{kpc}, \qquad \int_{b_\text{min}}^{b_\text{max}}\frac{db}{b} = \ln\left(\frac{b_\text{max}}{b_\text{min}}\right) \equiv \ln\Lambda = \ln\left(10^{11}\right) \approx 25" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (70)–(71), p. 19" />

the **Coulomb logarithm** (the same integral appears for charged particles in a plasma). [With $2R/N = 2\times10^{-10}$ kpc, $b_\text{min}$ is about 0.04 AU rather than the notes' 0.01 AU; $\ln\Lambda$ hardly changes, 24.6 instead of 25.3. The notes also print $b_\text{min}$ as 2R divided by a speed, and the acceleration at closest approach with the galaxy's mass $M$ instead of the star's mass $m$; both are slips.]

## The relaxation time

The **relaxation time** is how long it takes for the accumulated kicks to be as large as the velocity itself, $\sum(\delta v_i)^2 \approx v^2$: after that the star has "forgotten" its original orbit.

<Formula label="Relaxation time" tex="\sum_i(\delta v_i)^2 \approx v^2 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{8\pi G^2m^2n}{v}\ln\Lambda\,t_\text{relax} = v^2 \;\Rightarrow\; t_\text{relax} = \frac{v^3}{8\pi G^2m^2n\ln\Lambda}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (72), p. 19" />

Compare it with the time to cross the galaxy, $t_\text{orbit} \approx R/v$:

<Formula label="Relaxation time in crossing times" tex="\frac{t_\text{relax}}{t_\text{orbit}} = \frac{v^4}{8\pi G^2m^2nR\ln\Lambda}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (73), p. 19" />

With the virial theorem, $v^2 = GM/R$, and $n = (M/m)/\left(\frac43\pi R^3\right)$, all the constants cancel:

<Formula label="Relaxation in terms of the number of stars" tex="\frac{t_\text{relax}}{t_\text{orbit}} = \frac{(GM/R)^2}{8\pi G^2m^2\left(3M/4\pi mR^3\right)R\ln\Lambda} = \frac{N}{6\ln\Lambda} = \frac{N_*}{6\ln\left(R/(2R/N_*)\right)} \sim \frac{N_*}{6\ln N_*}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (74), p. 19" />

For a galaxy that is an enormous number. The stars orbit in the smooth, **collective** potential of the whole galaxy, practically unaffected by individual neighbours: galaxies are **collisionless**.

## How collisionless systems relax

Gas relaxes to a Maxwellian distribution through collisions. The stars and dark matter in galaxies cannot, yet galaxies and clusters do reach relaxed states within the age of the universe. A system is called **relaxed** when its **coarse-grained phase-space distribution** (the density of stars in position and velocity, smoothed over small cells) no longer changes. Two collisionless processes do the job.

**Phase mixing.** Stars on different orbits have different periods. A group that starts as a compact blob in phase space gets sheared out along the orbits into a thin spiral that wraps around and around. The fine-grained distribution keeps changing, but once the spiral is wound up tightly, the coarse-grained distribution is smooth and constant.

**Violent relaxation.** The energy per unit mass of a star is

<Formula label="Specific energy of a star" tex="E = \frac12v^2 + \phi" cite="reading1" at="eq. (75), p. 20" />

Its rate of change, using $d\vec v/dt = -\nabla\phi$ and $d\phi/dt = \partial\phi/\partial t + \vec v\cdot\nabla\phi$, is

<Formula label="Energy changes only through a changing potential" tex="\frac{dE}{dt} = -\vec v\cdot\nabla\phi + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + \vec v\cdot\nabla\phi = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (76), p. 20" />

So without collisions, a star's energy can change **only if the potential changes in time**. Picture a ball rolling through a dip. If the dip stays the same, the ball climbs out with the energy it came in with. If the dip deepens while the ball is inside, the ball loses energy and may be trapped; if the dip becomes shallower, it gains energy. When a galaxy or cluster forms, mass falls in and collapses into a halo, and the potential changes strongly. Averaged over all particles the timescale is

<Formula label="Violent relaxation time" tex="t_\text{vr} = \left\langle\frac{(dE/dt)^2}{E^2}\right\rangle^{-1/2} = \left\langle\frac{(\partial\phi/\partial t)^2}{E^2}\right\rangle^{-1/2} \sim \left\langle\frac{\dot\phi^2}{\phi^2}\right\rangle^{-1/2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (77), p. 21" />

where the last step uses the time-dependent virial theorem (Lynden-Bell 1967). [The notes print $\dot\phi^2/\phi$; the square in the denominator is needed for a rate.] This is about the free-fall time, the time on which the potential changes during collapse: very fast, hence "violent" relaxation.

## Summary

- A single encounter at impact parameter $b$ gives a sideways kick $\delta v = 2Gm/(bv)$.
- Strong encounters need $b \lesssim 2Gm/v^2 \approx 2R/N$; the chance of one per crossing is about $3/N \sim 10^{-11}$ in a galaxy.
- Weak encounters add in quadrature, every decade of $b$ contributing equally (the Coulomb logarithm, $\ln\Lambda \approx 25$). The relaxation time is $t_\text{relax} = v^3/(8\pi G^2m^2n\ln\Lambda)$, about $N/(6\ln N)$ crossing times.
- For galaxies this is far longer than the age of the universe: stars move in a smooth collective potential. Globular clusters, with far fewer stars, do relax by encounters.
- Collisionless systems relax by **phase mixing** and by **violent relaxation**: since $dE/dt = \partial\phi/\partial t$, a rapidly changing potential during collapse redistributes energies on about the free-fall time.
