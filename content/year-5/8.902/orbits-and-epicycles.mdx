The previous page built potentials for galaxies. Lecture 5 of 8.902 (section 3.B of the notes) puts stars into them and follows their orbits: what is conserved, how orbits close or precess, and how nearly circular orbits in a disc wobble around a "guiding centre". It ends with relations between luminosity and velocity that let astronomers measure distances. (The notes then begin section 3.C on the distribution function, which the next page covers.)

## Integrals of motion

An **orbit** is the path of a star through six-dimensional phase space, $(\vec x(t), \vec v(t))$. Every conserved quantity (integral of motion) removes one dimension. In a time-independent potential the energy per unit mass is

<Formula label="Orbital energy" tex="E = \frac12v^2 + \phi(r)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (102), p. 26" />

and it is conserved, $dE/dt = 0$ (eq. 103), since the change in kinetic energy is exactly the work done by $-\nabla\phi$. In a central potential the angular momentum per unit mass is conserved too:

<Formula label="Angular momentum is conserved in a central force" tex="\vec L = \vec r\times\dot{\vec r}, \qquad \frac{d\vec L}{dt} = \dot{\vec r}\times\dot{\vec r} + \vec r\times\ddot{\vec r} = \vec r\times\left(F(r)\hat e_r\right) = 0" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (104)–(105), p. 27" />

so the motion is confined to a plane, and effectively a four-dimensional phase space remains.

## The orbit equation

In the orbital plane use polar coordinates $(r, \psi)$ with unit vectors $\hat e_r = (\cos\psi, \sin\psi)$ and $\hat e_\psi = (-\sin\psi, \cos\psi)$. [The notes print $\hat e_\psi$ with the opposite sign, but then use $d\hat e_r/d\psi = \hat e_\psi$, which needs the form given here.] Differentiating $\vec r = r\hat e_r$ twice (eqs. 107–108) and setting the acceleration equal to the central force per unit mass $F(r)\hat e_r$ gives a radial and a tangential equation:

<Formula label="Radial and tangential equations" tex="\ddot r - r\dot\psi^2 = F(r), \qquad 2\dot r\dot\psi + r\ddot\psi = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (110), p. 28 (central force from eq. 109)" />

The tangential equation says that $L = r^2\dot\psi$ is constant. To simplify the radial one, substitute $u = 1/r$ and use $\psi$ instead of time, with $d/dt = Lu^2\,d/d\psi$:

<Formula label="Changing variables" tex="L = r^2\frac{d\psi}{dt}, \qquad \frac{d}{dt} = \frac{L}{r^2}\frac{d}{d\psi} = Lu^2\frac{d}{d\psi}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (112)–(114), p. 28" />

Working through the derivatives (eq. 115) gives the **orbit equation**, which describes the shape of the orbit without any reference to time:

<Formula label="The orbit equation" tex="\frac{d^2u}{d\psi^2} + u = -\frac{F(u)}{L^2u^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (116), p. 29" />

## Kepler orbits close; relativistic orbits precess

For a point mass, $F = -GMu^2$, and the orbit equation becomes a harmonic oscillator with a constant driving term:

<Formula label="Kepler orbit equation" tex="\frac{d^2u}{d\psi^2} + u = \frac{GM}{L^2}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (117)–(118), p. 29" />

<Formula label="Kepler solution" tex="u(\psi) = C\cos(\psi - \psi_0) + \frac{GM}{L^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (119), p. 29" />

The "frequency" in $\psi$ is exactly 1, so $u(0) = u(2\pi)$: the orbit **closes**, an ellipse. Now add a post-Newtonian correction to the potential:

<Formula label="Corrected potential and force" tex="\phi(r) = -\frac{GM}{r}\left(1 + \frac{2GM}{rc^2}\right), \qquad F(r) = -\frac{GM}{r^2} - \frac{4G^2M^2}{r^3c^2} = -GMu^2 - \frac{4G^2M^2}{c^2}u^3" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (120)–(121), p. 29" />

The extra $u^3$ term moves to the left-hand side and changes the coefficient of $u$:

<Formula label="Precessing orbit equation" tex="\frac{d^2u}{d\psi^2} + \left(1 - \frac{4G^2M^2}{L^2c^2}\right)u = \frac{GM}{L^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (122), p. 30" />

The frequency is now slightly less than 1, so after $\psi = 2\pi$ the star has not quite returned to its closest point: the orbit **precesses**, like the orbit of Mercury. [For a harmonic oscillator $m\ddot u + ku = $ const the solution is $\cos(\omega t - \phi)$ with $\omega^2 = k/m$ (eqs. 123–124); the notes print $k\dot u$. Note also that this simple correction is not the full general-relativistic one: for Mercury it gives about 29 arcseconds per century instead of the observed anomalous 43 (the part of Mercury's precession not caused by the other planets), two thirds of the right value.]

## Axisymmetric potentials and the effective potential

A disc galaxy is not spherical but **axisymmetric**: $\phi = \phi(R, |z|)$ in cylindrical coordinates $(R, \psi, z)$. Repeating the derivation with the extra $z$ direction (eqs. 125–128) gives three equations:

<Formula label="Equations of motion in an axisymmetric potential" tex="\ddot r - r\dot\psi^2 = -\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r}, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left(r^2\dot\psi\right) = 0, \qquad \ddot z = -\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (129), p. 31" />

The middle one conserves the angular momentum about the symmetry axis, $L_z = r^2\dot\psi$. The centrifugal term can then be absorbed into an **effective potential**,

<Formula label="Effective potential" tex="\phi_\text{eff} = \phi + \frac{L_z^2}{2r^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (130), p. 31" />

whose second term is the **centrifugal barrier**. The motion in the meridional plane $(r, z)$ is then just motion in $\phi_\text{eff}$:

<Formula label="Motion in the meridional plane" tex="\ddot r = -\frac{\partial\phi_\text{eff}}{\partial r}, \qquad \ddot z = -\frac{\partial\phi_\text{eff}}{\partial z}, \qquad E = \frac12\left(\dot r^2 + \dot z^2\right) + \phi_\text{eff}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (132)–(135), pp. 31–32" />

with integrals $E$ and $L_z$. The star circles the axis at the rate $\dot\psi = L_z/r^2$ while oscillating in $r$ and $z$. [Eq. (133) in the notes has two slips in its intermediate lines, "=" for "−" in the first line and ψ_eff for φ_eff in the third; the result is right.]

## Guiding centre and epicycles

For a given $L_z$, $\phi_\text{eff}$ in the plane $z = 0$ has a minimum at a radius $R_g$, where both derivatives vanish (eq. 136; by symmetry there is no vertical force at $z = 0$):

<Formula label="The guiding centre" tex="0 = \left.\frac{\partial\phi_\text{eff}}{\partial r}\right|_{R_g, 0} \;\Rightarrow\; \left.\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r}\right|_{R_g, 0} = \frac{L_z^2}{R_g^3} = R_g\dot\psi^2, \qquad \Omega^2 = \dot\psi^2 = \frac{L_z^2}{R_g^4}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (137)–(139), pp. 32–33" />

[In eq. (137) the notes write the derivative of the effective potential where that of the potential $\phi$ is meant, as the line before it shows.] A star at $R_g$ with $\dot r = \dot z = 0$ moves on a **circular orbit** with angular speed $\Omega$: the **guiding centre**. A star pushed slightly off it feels a restoring force and oscillates around it. To see how, write $x = r - R_g$, $y = z$ (eq. 142) and expand $\phi_\text{eff}$ to second order. The first derivatives vanish at the minimum and the mixed one by symmetry (eqs. 143–144), so with

<Formula label="Epicycle and vertical frequencies" tex="\kappa^2 \equiv \left.\frac{\partial^2\phi_\text{eff}}{\partial r^2}\right|_{R_g, 0}, \qquad \nu^2 \equiv \left.\frac{\partial^2\phi_\text{eff}}{\partial z^2}\right|_{R_g, 0}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (145), p. 33" />

<Formula label="Effective potential near the guiding centre" tex="\tilde\phi_\text{eff}(x, y) \approx \tilde\phi_\text{eff}(0, 0) + \frac12\kappa^2x^2 + \frac12\nu^2y^2" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (146)–(147), pp. 33–34" />

the motion separates into two harmonic oscillators:

<Formula label="Epicyclic equations of motion" tex="\ddot x = -\kappa^2x, \qquad \ddot y = -\nu^2y" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (148)–(149), p. 34" />

$\kappa$ is the **epicycle frequency** (radial) and $\nu$ the **vertical frequency**, alongside the circular frequency

<Formula label="Circular frequency" tex="\Omega(r) = \frac{L_z}{r^2} = \frac{v_c}{r} = \sqrt{\frac1r\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r}}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (150), p. 34" />

using $v_c^2/r = \partial\phi/\partial r$ on a circular orbit. The epicycle frequency can be written entirely in terms of $\Omega$:

<Formula label="Epicycle frequency from the rotation curve" tex="\kappa^2 = r\frac{d\Omega^2}{dr} + 4\Omega^2" cite="reading1" at="eq. (151), p. 35" />

(The notes show the algebra: substitute $\Omega^2 = \frac1r\partial\phi/\partial r$ and recognize $\partial^2\phi_\text{eff}/\partial r^2$.) So $\kappa$ follows from the rotation curve. For a point mass $\Omega \propto r^{-3/2}$ and $\kappa = \Omega$: the radial oscillation has the orbital period, and the orbit closes. For a flat rotation curve $\Omega \propto 1/r$ and $\kappa = \sqrt2\,\Omega$.

## What an epicycle looks like

For $x, z \ll R_g$ the three motions are

<Formula label="Motion in the epicycle approximation" tex="r(t) = r_0\cos(\kappa t + \alpha) + R_g, \qquad z(t) = z_0\cos(\nu t + \beta), \qquad \dot\psi = \frac{L_z}{r^2} = \Omega(R_g)\left(1 + \frac{x}{R_g}\right)^{-2} \approx \Omega_g\left(1 - \frac{2x}{R_g}\right)" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (152)–(153), p. 35" />

and integrating the last one,

<Formula label="Azimuthal motion" tex="\psi(t) = \Omega_gt + \psi_0 - \frac{2\Omega_gr_0}{\kappa R_g}\sin(\kappa t + \alpha)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (154), p. 36" />

Seen from the guiding centre, which moves round on its circle, the star traces a small closed **ellipse**, traversed in the opposite (retrograde) sense, while also bobbing up and down with frequency $\nu$. The ellipse has radial half-axis $r_0$ and tangential half-axis $2\Omega r_0/\kappa$.

## Oort constants: measuring κ near the Sun

Section 3.B ends by measuring these frequencies in the Milky Way (the notes refer to a problem set for the derivation). Positions on the sky are given in **galactic coordinates**: longitude $l$, measured in the plane from the direction of the Galactic centre, and latitude $b$ above the plane. The Sun is at $R \sim 8$ kpc from the centre. For nearby stars at distance $d$, differential rotation produces a pattern in their motions:

<Formula label="Motions of nearby stars" tex="\text{proper motion:}\ \mu \approx d\left(A\cos 2l + B\right), \qquad \text{line of sight:}\ v_\parallel \approx dA\sin 2l" cite="reading1" at="eq. (155), p. 36" />

[As printed, the first is the transverse velocity; the proper motion itself, an angle per time, is $A\cos 2l + B$ without the factor $d$.] $A$ and $B$ are the **Oort constants**:

<Formula label="Oort constants" tex="A = -\frac12R\frac{d\Omega}{dR}, \qquad B = -\left(\Omega + \frac12R\frac{d\Omega}{dR}\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (156), p. 36" />

[The notes omit the factor $R$ in $A$; it is needed for the units, and they include it in $B$.] They give the local angular speed, $\Omega = A - B$, and the epicycle frequency:

<Formula label="Epicycle frequency from the Oort constants" tex="\kappa^2 = -4B(A - B)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (157), p. 36" />

<WorkedExample title="Epicycles at the Sun">
<Problem>
Measurements with the Gaia satellite give $A = 15.3$ km/s/kpc and $B = -11.9$ km/s/kpc (Bovy 2017). Find $\Omega$, $\kappa$ and the orbital and epicyclic periods (1 kpc/(km/s) is 977.8 million years).
</Problem>
<Step title="Frequencies">
$\Omega = A - B = 27.2$ km/s/kpc, and $\kappa = \sqrt{-4 \times (-11.9) \times 27.2} = \sqrt{1295} = 36.0$ km/s/kpc, so $\kappa/\Omega = 1.32$, close to the $\sqrt2 = 1.41$ of a flat rotation curve.
</Step>
<Step title="Periods">
Orbit: $2\pi/\Omega = 0.231$ kpc/(km/s) $= 226$ million years. Epicycle: $2\pi/\kappa = 0.175$ kpc/(km/s) $= 171$ million years. The Sun makes about 1.3 radial oscillations per orbit, so its path is a rosette, not a closed ellipse.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Luminosity and velocity

The section closes with a scaling argument. For a galaxy of size $R$ at distance $d$:

<Formula label="Observables of a galaxy" tex="\theta = \frac Rd, \qquad F = \frac{L}{4\pi d^2}, \qquad v^2 = \frac{GM}{R}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (158), p. 37" />

The surface brightness, flux per solid angle, does not depend on $d$:

<Formula label="Surface brightness" tex="\Sigma = \frac{F}{\theta^2} = \frac{L}{4\pi d^2}\cdot\frac{d^2}{R^2} = \frac{L}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{v^4}{G^2M^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (159), p. 37" />

using $R = GM/v^2$. Solving for $L$ with the mass-to-light ratio $M/L$:

<Formula label="Luminosity from velocity" tex="L = \frac{v^4}{\Sigma\,4\pi G^2(M/L)^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (160), p. 37" />

If galaxies of a given class share the same surface brightness and mass-to-light ratio, then

<Formula label="Luminosity scales as velocity to the fourth" tex="L \propto v^4" cite="reading1" at="eq. (161), p. 37" />

For **spirals** this is the **Tully–Fisher relation** with the maximum rotation speed, and for **ellipticals** the **Faber–Jackson relation** with the velocity dispersion:

<Formula label="Tully–Fisher and Faber–Jackson relations" tex="L \propto v_\text{max}^4, \qquad L \propto \sigma_v^4" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (162)–(163), p. 37" />

The constant of proportionality is roughly $L_*/(220\ \text{km/s})^4$. Measuring a galaxy's internal velocities therefore gives its luminosity, and comparing with its flux gives its distance. (The notes say the rotation speed is measured from "HII spectra"; in practice the Tully–Fisher relation is usually based on the width of the 21 cm line of neutral hydrogen, HI, or on optical emission lines.)

## Summary

- In a static potential the energy is conserved; in a central one the angular momentum too. With $u = 1/r$ the shape of the orbit obeys $d^2u/d\psi^2 + u = -F/(L^2u^2)$.
- Kepler orbits close; a relativistic correction to the potential lowers the frequency below 1, and the orbit precesses.
- In an axisymmetric disc, $L_z$ is conserved and the effective potential $\phi + L_z^2/2r^2$ has a minimum at the guiding-centre radius. Nearly circular orbits oscillate about it with the epicycle frequency $\kappa$ ($\kappa^2 = r\,d\Omega^2/dr + 4\Omega^2$) and vertical frequency $\nu$, tracing retrograde ellipses around the guiding centre.
- The Oort constants $A$ and $B$ give $\Omega = A - B$ and $\kappa^2 = -4B(A - B)$; at the Sun $\kappa \approx 36$ km/s/kpc.
- For fixed surface brightness and mass-to-light ratio, $L \propto v^4$: the Tully–Fisher relation for spirals and the Faber–Jackson relation for ellipticals.
