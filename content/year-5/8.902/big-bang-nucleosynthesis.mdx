The previous page followed the dark matter through the hot early universe until it froze out. The same reasoning, applied to protons and neutrons, explains where the lightest elements came from. This page follows the 8.902 Lecture 20 notes. (They open by finishing the timeline of the hot universe, which the dark matter page already covers.)

Big Bang nucleosynthesis (BBN) happened between roughly 10 MeV and 0.1 MeV, seconds to minutes after the Big Bang. Once protons and neutrons exist, they can fuse into deuterium (D), helium-4, helium-3 and a trace of lithium-7. The result is a detailed prediction for the composition of the gas from which the first stars formed, and a test the hot Big Bang has passed.

## Neutrons and protons in equilibrium

Protons and neutrons appear when the quark–gluon plasma condenses (the notes write "gluon-gluon plasma"). At first they are continually converted into each other by **weak interactions** with neutrinos, electrons and positrons:

<Formula label="Weak interactions between neutrons and protons" tex="n + \nu_e \;\rightleftharpoons\; p + e^-, \qquad n + e^+ \;\rightleftharpoons\; p + \bar\nu_e" cite="reading1" at="eq. (477), p. 109" />

While these reactions are fast, both species are in thermal equilibrium, with the non-relativistic number density of the dark matter page:

<Formula label="Equilibrium number density" tex="n_\text{eq} = g\left(\frac{mkT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2} e^{-mc^2/kT}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (478), p. 109" />

[The notes print $2\pi\hbar$ without the square; the dark matter page's eq. (466) has it.] Protons and neutrons both have two spin states, $g_n = g_p = 2$, so in the ratio almost everything cancels:

<Formula label="Neutron-to-proton ratio in equilibrium" tex="\frac{n_n}{n_p} = \left(\frac{m_n}{m_p}\right)^{3/2} e^{-(m_n - m_p)c^2/kT} \approx e^{-1.29\,\text{MeV}/kT}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (479)–(480), pp. 109–110" />

The neutron is heavier by $1.29$ MeV$/c^2$, and the mass prefactor $(m_n/m_p)^{3/2} \approx 1.002$ hardly matters. [The notes' eq. (479) has a misprinted subscript on the neutron mass in the exponent.] At high temperature the ratio is close to one; as the universe cools, neutrons become rarer.

## Freeze-out at one neutron per five protons

The weak reactions need neutrinos, and the neutrinos **decouple** at about $kT \approx 0.8$ MeV, one second after the Big Bang. From then on the conversions stop and the ratio is frozen at

<Formula label="Frozen neutron-to-proton ratio" tex="\frac{n_n}{n_p} \approx e^{-1.29/0.8} \approx 0.2" cite="reading1" at="eq. (481), p. 110" />

a ratio of one neutron to five protons. Now nucleosynthesis can begin, in principle.

## The deuterium bottleneck

Every heavier nucleus has to be built through **deuterium**, made by the strong reaction

<Formula label="Deuteron formation" tex="p + n \;\rightleftharpoons\; D + \gamma" cite="reading1" at="eq. (482), p. 110" />

The deuteron is held together by $2.22$ MeV. At neutrino decoupling the temperature is already below that, yet deuterium cannot survive. The reason is the enormous number of photons: there are about a billion of them for every baryon, so even when the typical photon is far too weak, the **high-energy tail** of the black-body spectrum still contains enough photons above 2.22 MeV to break up every deuteron that forms. Deuterium only builds up once $kT \ll 2.22$ MeV.

Once deuterium is available, the further steps to helium-4 go quickly, so the waiting time at this first step is called the **deuterium bottleneck**. The wait matters, because free neutrons are unstable. They $\beta$-decay ($n \to p + e^- + \bar\nu_e$), and without fusion into deuterium all of them would eventually be gone.

### A first estimate: instant fusion

Suppose deuterium formed at once, and every neutron ended up in helium-4. Take 12 nucleons in the 5-to-1 ratio: 10 protons and 2 neutrons. The 2 neutrons and 2 of the protons make one helium-4 nucleus, leaving 8 free protons. The **helium mass fraction** is then

<Formula label="Helium fraction with instant fusion" tex="Y = \frac{4}{4 + 8} = \frac{4}{12} \approx 0.33" cite="reading1" at="eq. (483), p. 110" />

Observations give less, about 0.24 in the notes. The difference is the neutrons that decayed during the bottleneck.

## When does deuterium form?

The reaction $p + n \rightleftharpoons D + \gamma$ never freezes out: it simply stops when the neutrons run out. While it runs, the three abundances follow the **Saha equation**, the equilibrium law for a reaction:

<Formula label="Saha equation for deuterium" tex="\frac{n_D}{n_p n_n} = \frac{g_D}{g_p g_n}\left(\frac{m_D}{m_p m_n}\right)^{3/2}\left(\frac{kT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{-3/2} e^{2.22\,\text{MeV}/kT}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (484), p. 110" />

The deuteron has spin 1 and so three spin states, $g_D = 3$, while $g_p = g_n = 2$. With $m_p \approx m_n \approx m_D/2$ the constants combine into

<Formula label="Saha equation, simplified" tex="\frac{n_D}{n_p n_n} = 6\left(\frac{m_n kT}{\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{-3/2} e^{2.22\,\text{MeV}/kT}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (485), p. 110" />

(the factor is $\tfrac34 \cdot 2^{3/2} \cdot 2^{3/2} = 6$). The notes define the moment of deuterium formation as the time when **half** of the neutrons are in deuterons, $n_D = n_n$:

<Formula label="Condition for deuterium formation" tex="\frac{n_D}{n_n} = 1 = 6\,n_p\left(\frac{m_n kT}{\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{-3/2} e^{2.22\,\text{MeV}/kT}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (486), p. 111" />

To solve it we need $n_p$ as a function of temperature. With $n_n = n_p/5$ the baryon density is $n_b = n_p + n_n = \tfrac65 n_p$, and the baryons are a fixed fraction of the photons, the **baryon-to-photon ratio** $\eta = n_b/n_\gamma$. A black body has $n_\gamma = 0.24\,(kT/\hbar c)^3$, so

<Formula label="Proton density from the photon density" tex="n_b = n_p + n_n = \frac65 n_p, \qquad n_p = \frac56\,\eta\,n_\gamma = \frac56\,\eta\left(0.24\left(\frac{kT}{\hbar c}\right)^3\right)" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (487)–(488), p. 111" />

Putting this in, the powers of $\hbar$ cancel and everything collapses to one dimensionless equation:

<Formula label="Deuterium formation condition, numerically" tex="1 \approx 6.9\,\eta\left(\frac{kT}{m_n c^2}\right)^{3/2} e^{2.22\,\text{MeV}/kT} \approx 3.4\times10^{-9}\left(\frac{kT}{m_n c^2}\right)^{3/2} e^{2.22\,\text{MeV}/kT}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (489), p. 111" />

using the fiducial value $\eta \sim 5\times10^{-10}$. [The prefactor is $0.24 \times 5 \times \pi^{3/2} = 1.2 \times 5.57 = 6.7$; the notes write 1.25 for 1.2 and get 6.9. The difference changes the temperature below by less than a percent.] The tiny factor $3.4\times10^{-9}$ must be balanced by a large exponential, so $kT$ must be far below 2.22 MeV. Solving gives

<Formula label="Time of deuterium formation" tex="T \approx 8\times10^8\ \text{K}, \qquad t \approx 200\ \text{s}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (490), p. 111" />

## Neutron decay and the helium fraction

During those 200 seconds neutrons decay. The notes use $890$ s for the neutron, which is its **mean lifetime** $\tau$ (they call it a half-life; the half-life is $\tau\ln2 \approx 620$ s, and using it would not give their number). Starting from one neutron in six nucleons, after 200 s the ratio has dropped to

<Formula label="Neutron-to-proton ratio at deuterium formation" tex="\frac{n_n}{n_p} \approx 0.15 < 0.2" cite="reading1" at="eq. (491), p. 111" />

Practically all of these neutrons now end up in helium-4. Each helium nucleus holds two neutrons, so $n_\text{He} = n_n/2$, and the protons left as hydrogen number $n_\text{H} = n_p - n_n$. The helium mass fraction is

<Formula label="Helium mass fraction" tex="Y = \frac{4n_\text{He}}{4n_\text{He} + n_\text{H}} = \frac{2n_n}{2n_n + (n_p - n_n)} = \frac{2n_n}{n_p + n_n}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (492), p. 111" />

<Formula label="Helium mass fraction from the neutron ratio" tex="Y = \frac{2(n_n/n_p)}{1 + (n_n/n_p)} \approx 0.25" cite="reading1" at="eq. (493), p. 112" />

in good agreement with the measured value of about a quarter. A neat form of the same result: $Y$ is just **twice the fraction of nucleons that are neutrons**, since $2n_n/(n_p + n_n) = 2X_n$.

<WorkedExample title="Neutron bookkeeping">
<Problem>
(a) Check the ratio $n_n/n_p \approx 0.2$ at $kT = 0.8$ MeV. (b) Starting from one neutron per six nucleons, how many neutrons are left after 200 s with $\tau = 890$ s, and what is $Y$? (c) If the baryon density were lower, so that deuterium formed only at 300 s, what would $Y$ be?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Freeze-out">
$e^{-1.29/0.8} = e^{-1.61} = 0.199$: about one neutron for every five protons, so one nucleon in six is a neutron.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) After 200 s">
The neutron fraction falls to $X_n = \tfrac16 e^{-200/890} = 0.1667 \times 0.799 = 0.133$. The ratio is $n_n/n_p = 0.133/0.867 = 0.154$, close to the notes' 0.15, and $Y = 2X_n = 0.266$. With the rounded ratio 0.15, eq. (493) gives $2(0.15)/1.15 = 0.26$; the notes round to about 0.25.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A later start">
$X_n = \tfrac16 e^{-300/890} = 0.119$, so $Y = 0.238$. Fewer baryons mean a later start, more decay and less helium. (In the Saha estimate above, a delay this long would need a far smaller $\eta$; the example only shows the direction of the effect.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## What BBN measures

The notes end with two consequences:

- **$Y$ grows with $\Omega_b$.** More baryons means a larger $\eta$, so deuterium forms earlier (the crossing in the first graph moves to higher temperature), fewer neutrons have decayed, $n_n/n_p$ is larger and so is $Y$. The dependence is gentle.
- **Deuterium is the sensitive probe.** Measuring the abundances of helium-4 and deuterium determines $\eta$ and hence $\Omega_b$. The deuterium left over is especially sensitive to $\Omega_b$ (with more baryons, more of it is burned on to helium). It can be measured with the **Lyman-α forest** technique, where gas clouds in front of distant quasars absorb at the slightly shifted lines of hydrogen and deuterium, and the relative strength of the lines gives D/H. (In practice the precise measurements use the thickest absorbers, Lyman-limit and damped systems, rather than the thin forest clouds.)

This is the second, independent measurement of $\Omega_b$ promised on the CMB page, from physics in the first few minutes rather than at 380,000 years.

<Callout kind="note" title="Later measurements">
The deuterium measurement from quasar absorbers and the CMB's measurement of $\Omega_b$ agree closely (about 5% of the critical density in baryons), one of the strongest checks of the hot Big Bang. The one light element that does not fit is lithium-7: BBN predicts about three times more than is seen in old stars, the unresolved "lithium problem".
</Callout>

## Summary

- Weak interactions keep neutrons and protons in equilibrium, $n_n/n_p \approx e^{-1.29\,\text{MeV}/kT}$, until neutrinos decouple at $kT \approx 0.8$ MeV ($t \approx 1$ s), freezing the ratio at about 1 to 5.
- Deuterium ($p + n \rightleftharpoons D + \gamma$, binding 2.22 MeV) is destroyed by the vast number of photons until $kT \approx 0.07$ MeV: the Saha equation with $\eta \sim 5\times10^{-10}$ gives $T \approx 8\times10^8$ K, $t \approx 200$ s. This is the deuterium bottleneck.
- Meanwhile neutrons decay, lowering $n_n/n_p$ to about 0.15. Almost all survivors end up in helium-4: $Y = 2(n_n/n_p)/(1 + n_n/n_p) \approx 0.25$, compared with 0.33 for instant fusion.
- A larger baryon density gives slightly more helium and much less leftover deuterium, so the D/H ratio, measured in the Lyman-α forest, determines $\Omega_b$.
