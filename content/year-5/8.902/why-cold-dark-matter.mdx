The previous page showed that perturbations larger than the Jeans length grow, but only slowly, in proportion to the scale factor during the matter era. Following the 8.902 notes, this page asks whether **ordinary matter** (baryons) could have grown into the structure we see, finds that it could not, and shows why **cold dark matter** solves the problem.

## Baryons: stuck until recombination

Perturbations grow only above the Jeans length, or above the Jeans mass $M_J = \frac{4}{3}\pi\bar\rho\lambda_J^3$. For baryons the sound speed changes dramatically at recombination:

- **Before recombination** the electrons are tightly coupled to the photons, so the baryons and photons form a single fluid whose pressure is that of the radiation, $p = \rho c^2/3$:

<Formula label="Sound speed before recombination" tex="c_s^2 = \frac{\partial p}{\partial\rho}, \quad p = \frac{1}{3}\rho c^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c_s = \frac{c}{\sqrt3}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (394), p. 85" />

- **After recombination** the baryons are an ordinary ideal gas, with $p = \rho kT/m$ and $T = 2.7\ \text{K}\,(1+z)$ [the notes print $p = \rho/(mkT)$, a typo for $\rho kT/m$]:

<Formula label="Sound speed after recombination" tex="c_s = \sqrt{\frac{kT}{m}} \approx 5\ \text{km/s}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (395), p. 85" />

The Jeans mass therefore drops enormously when the photon pressure support is removed:

<Formula label="Baryonic Jeans mass" tex="z \gg 1100:\ M_J \gtrsim 10^{16}\,M_\odot, \qquad z < 1100:\ M_J \lesssim 10^{5}\,M_\odot" cite="reading1" at="eq. (396), p. 86" />

Before recombination, anything smaller than a supercluster just oscillates. Baryonic structure can only start forming after $z \sim 1000$.

## Not enough time

From $z \sim 1000$ to today the scale factor grows by about $10^3$, and in the matter era $\delta \propto a$. The CMB shows $\delta \sim 10^{-5}$ at recombination, so baryons alone would reach only $\delta_\text{today} \sim 10^{-5} \times 10^3 = 10^{-2}$, far less than the clumping we see in the low-redshift universe, where galaxies and clusters have $\delta \gg 1$. Growth of ordinary matter alone **cannot** produce today's structure. The way out: dark matter must have clumped **earlier**, and after recombination the baryons fell into the dark matter's gravitational wells.

## Cold dark matter

**Cold dark matter** (CDM) has two properties that make this work:

- It is very cold, with a tiny velocity dispersion $\sigma$, so its Jeans mass is tiny: collapse is possible on essentially all scales.
- It does not interact with radiation, so photon pressure doesn't hold it up: it can grow **before** recombination.

In the notes' words, cold dark matter is "needed to make structure formation work".

## Growth on different scales: the horizon

Perturbations come on all length scales, and their growth also depends on whether they are larger or smaller than the **horizon**. A full general-relativistic treatment shows that modes **outside** the horizon always grow; there is no causal contact to resist them:

<Formula label="Super-horizon growth" tex="\delta \propto \begin{cases} a \propto t^{2/3} & \text{matter dominated} \\ a^2 \propto t & \text{radiation dominated} \end{cases}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (397), p. 86" />

Once a mode enters the horizon, its growth changes. The physical horizon grows, for Ω = 1, as

<Formula label="Physical horizon" tex="\ell_\text{horizon} = a\int_0^t\frac{c\,dt'}{a(t')} = \begin{cases} 2ct = \dfrac{c}{\sqrt{G\bar\rho}}\sqrt{\dfrac{3}{8\pi}} & \text{radiation dominated} \\[1.5ex] 3ct = \dfrac{c}{\sqrt{G\bar\rho}}\sqrt{\dfrac{3}{2\pi}} & \text{matter dominated} \end{cases}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (399), p. 86" />

(the same horizon distances as in 8.286). For baryons before recombination, the Jeans length $\lambda_J = \frac{c}{\sqrt{G\bar\rho}}\sqrt{\pi/3}$ (eq. 398, with $c_s = c/\sqrt3$) is **larger** than the horizon. So as soon as a baryonic mode enters the horizon, it starts to oscillate. The Jeans mass reaches about $10^{16}\,M_\odot$ by $z \sim 1000$, so every baryonic mode smaller than that entered the horizon before recombination, and stopped growing.

### Silk damping

Worse, those modes are erased. Before decoupling photons can't stream freely (they keep Thomson-scattering off free electrons), but their mean free path grows towards recombination. Below about $10^{16}\,M_\odot$ perturbations oscillate under photon pressure, and photons can **diffuse** out of the potential wells, dragging the baryons with them: electrons through Thomson scattering, protons through their Coulomb attraction to the electrons. The net effect: baryonic perturbations below about $10^{12}\,M_\odot$, the **Silk mass**, are damped away.

### Dark matter: frozen, not erased

Cold dark matter has essentially zero Jeans mass, so all its modes can grow. But a CDM mode that enters the horizon during the **radiation era** stalls: $\delta$ stays roughly constant. The reason is that the expansion is then faster than the collapse:

<Formula label="Expansion versus collapse in the radiation era" tex="\tau_\text{Hubble} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{G\rho_r}}, \qquad \tau_\text{Jeans} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{G\rho_m}}, \qquad \tau_\text{Hubble} \ll \tau_\text{Jeans}\ \text{if}\ \rho_r \gg \rho_m" cite="reading2" at="eq. (401), p. 88" />

So modes entering the horizon during radiation domination are **frozen** (a fuller treatment gives a slow, logarithmic growth), but, unlike the baryons, not damped. A mode entering after matter–radiation equality grows all the time; one entering earlier stagnates until matter domination and then grows. After recombination the baryons fall into the CDM wells and catch up. So the notes make cold dark matter the main engine of structure formation: only it has time to build perturbations large enough, and without it there would be no structure to speak of.

<WorkedExample title="The sound speed and Jeans mass after recombination">
<Problem>
Take $T = 2.7255\ \text{K} \times 1101 \approx 3000$ K at recombination, a gas of hydrogen atoms ($m = m_p$), and a total matter density $\bar\rho = 0.31\rho_c(1+z)^3$ with $\rho_c = 8.6 \times 10^{-27}$ kg/m³. Estimate $c_s$, $\lambda_J = c_s\sqrt{\pi/(G\bar\rho)}$ and $M_J = \frac{4}{3}\pi\bar\rho\lambda_J^3$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Sound speed">
$c_s = \sqrt{kT/m_p} = \sqrt{1.38\times10^{-23} \times 3000 / 1.67\times10^{-27}}$ m/s $= 5.0$ km/s, the notes' "≈ 5 km/s".
</Step>
<Step title="Density">
$\bar\rho = 0.31 \times 8.6\times10^{-27} \times 1101^3 = 3.6 \times 10^{-18}$ kg/m³.
</Step>
<Step title="Jeans length and mass">
$\lambda_J = 5.0\times10^3\sqrt{\pi/(6.67\times10^{-11} \times 3.6\times10^{-18})}$ m $\approx 5.7 \times 10^{17}$ m $\approx 19$ pc, and $M_J \approx 1.4 \times 10^6\,M_\odot$.
</Step>
<Step title="Comparison">
That is the mass of a large star cluster, tiny compared with a galaxy. The notes quote $M_J \lesssim 10^5\,M_\odot$; factors of order ten depend on conventions (which density, $\gamma$, mean molecular weight, the exact definition of $\lambda_J$). Either way, after recombination the pressure barrier essentially disappears for galaxy-sized masses.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="note" title="Names and later developments">
The stagnation of dark matter perturbations during the radiation era is usually called the **Mészáros effect** (after Péter Mészáros, 1974); Silk damping is named after Joseph Silk (1968). Despite decades of searches (underground detectors, colliders, telescopes), dark matter particles have not been detected directly; cold dark matter remains the standard ingredient of the ΛCDM model.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Before recombination baryons and photons form one fluid with $c_s = c/\sqrt3$ and Jeans mass ≳ $10^{16}\,M_\odot$; afterwards $c_s \approx 5$ km/s and $M_J$ drops to ~$10^5$–$10^6\,M_\odot$.
- Baryons alone could grow only from $z \sim 1000$, by ~$10^3$: from $10^{-5}$ to ~$10^{-2}$, far too little.
- Before decoupling, baryonic modes inside the horizon oscillate, and Silk damping erases those below ~$10^{12}\,M_\odot$.
- Cold dark matter has almost no Jeans mass and ignores radiation; its sub-horizon modes are frozen (not erased) in the radiation era and grow from matter–radiation equality. Baryons then fall into its wells: cold dark matter is needed for structure formation.
