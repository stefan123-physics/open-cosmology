This set practises the lessons on [the cosmological principle and the expanding universe](/courses/8-902/cosmological-principle-expansion), [the Friedmann equations and the density parameters](/courses/8-902/friedmann-equations-density-parameters) and [redshift and cosmic distances](/courses/8-902/observational-cosmology-distances), with the flat rotation curves of [the lesson on observing galaxies](/courses/8-902/galaxy-observations). The cycloid of Problem 1 is the closed universe of [8.286](/courses/8-286/closed-universe-cycloid). The two problems follow MIT's Problem Set 5, in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

One thing differs from MIT's sheet in substance. For Problem 2, MIT hands out a data file: the light-curve fits of the Supernova Legacy Survey (SNLS) three-year sample, from Guy et al. (2010). That catalogue is not used here. **In its place, Problem 2 gives a table of 20 supernovae that we generated ourselves** from a stated model with random scatter (our choice, in place of MIT's data file). The supernovae are not real, but they behave like real ones, and every question of MIT's Problem 2 can be answered with them. Our assumptions and values are marked where they stand.

As on MIT's sheet, assume $\Omega_M = 0.3$, $\Omega_\Lambda = 0.7$, $\Omega_k = \Omega_r = 0$ and $H_0 = 71$ km/s/Mpc unless a problem says otherwise. $\Omega_M$ and $\Omega_\Lambda$ are the notes' $\Omega_{m,0}$ and $\Omega_{\Lambda,0}$, the density parameters of matter and of the cosmological constant today, and $\Omega_k = 1 - \Omega_M - \Omega_\Lambda$ is the curvature term.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The mass of the Local Group: the timing argument" points={30} mit="Problem 1">

The Local Group has two large galaxies, of about the luminosity $L^*$ of the Schechter function: the Milky Way and the Andromeda galaxy, M31. M31 is the one large galaxy that is coming towards us: its light is blueshifted. The idea of the problem is that the two galaxies flew apart with the expansion after the big bang, until their own gravity stopped them and turned them back.

MIT's numbers: once the Sun's motion around the Milky Way is taken out, M31 approaches us at 121 km/s, so the relative velocity is $v = -121$ km/s. Cepheid variables put M31 at a distance of about 700 kpc. By Birkhoff's theorem the pair can be treated as a small closed universe, whose separation $r$ and time $t$ since the big bang follow a cycloid:

<Formula label="The separation as a cycloid" tex="r = A\,(1 - \cos\eta), \qquad t = B\,(\eta - \sin\eta)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

Here $A$ and $B$ are constants (not the Oort constants of the Milky Way's rotation) and the parameter $\eta$ runs from 0 to $2\pi$, from the big bang to the moment the two galaxies meet.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Show that the relative velocity is

<Formula label="Relative velocity on the cycloid" tex="v = \frac{r}{t}\,\frac{\sin\eta\,(\eta - \sin\eta)}{(1 - \cos\eta)^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(A), p. 1" />

<Hint>

Both $r$ and $t$ depend on $\eta$: $v = dr/dt = (dr/d\eta)/(dt/d\eta)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

At the end, remove $A$ and $B$ with the two cycloid equations themselves, so that only $r$, $t$ and $\eta$ are left.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Differentiate along the curve, then use the curve to replace the constants by the observed $r$ and $t$.

<Step title="The derivative">

$dr/d\eta = A\sin\eta$ and $dt/d\eta = B(1 - \cos\eta)$, so $v = \dfrac{A}{B}\,\dfrac{\sin\eta}{1 - \cos\eta}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Removing A and B">

From the cycloid, $A = r/(1 - \cos\eta)$ and $B = t/(\eta - \sin\eta)$. Then $A/B = \dfrac{r}{t}\,\dfrac{\eta - \sin\eta}{1 - \cos\eta}$, and multiplying by $\sin\eta/(1 - \cos\eta)$ gives MIT's expression.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The sign follows $\sin\eta$: positive for $0 < \eta < \pi$ (moving apart), zero at $\eta = \pi$ (greatest separation), negative for $\pi < \eta < 2\pi$ (falling together). Near the start, $\sin\eta \approx \eta$, $1 - \cos\eta \approx \eta^2/2$ and $\eta - \sin\eta \approx \eta^3/6$, so $v \approx \frac{r}{t}\cdot\frac{\eta^4/6}{\eta^4/4} = \frac{2}{3}\frac{r}{t}$: the expansion rate $H = 2/(3t)$ of a matter-dominated universe, as it should be for a pair that at first expands with the universe.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Starting from Newtonian mechanics, show that the total mass $M_T$ of the pair satisfies

<Formula label="Mass of the pair" tex="GM_T = \frac{A^3}{B^2} = \frac{r^3}{t^2}\,\frac{(\eta - \sin\eta)^2}{(1 - \cos\eta)^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(B), p. 1" />

<Hint>

Treat the separation as the radius of a sphere containing the mass $M_T$, as the notes do for the expanding universe: then $\ddot r = -GM_T/r^2$, and $\tfrac12\dot r^2 - GM_T/r$ is a constant. Use the speed of part (a) in its form with $A/B$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write $\dot r^2$ with $\sin^2\eta = (1 - \cos\eta)(1 + \cos\eta)$. The energy must be the same at every $\eta$: compare the terms with $\cos\eta$ and the terms without it.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The cycloid solves Newton's equation for one value of $GM_T$ only. Find it by asking the energy to stay constant along the curve.

<Step title="Newton's law and its energy">

For two point masses moving straight apart, Newton's law for their separation is $\ddot r = -GM_T/r^2$, with $M_T$ the sum of the two masses: the same law as for the surface of the notes' uniform sphere. Multiplying by $\dot r$ and integrating gives the notes' energy equation in our letters:

<Formula label="Energy equation of the notes" tex="\frac12\dot a^2 = \frac{4\pi G\rho_0 a_0^3}{3}\,\frac1a + \tilde\kappa" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (273)–(274), p. 63" />

with $a \to r$ and $\frac43\pi\rho_0a_0^3 \to M_T$: $\tfrac12\dot r^2 - GM_T/r = E$, a constant.

</Step>

<Step title="The energy on the cycloid">

From part (a), $\dot r^2 = \dfrac{A^2}{B^2}\,\dfrac{\sin^2\eta}{(1 - \cos\eta)^2} = \dfrac{A^2}{B^2}\,\dfrac{1 + \cos\eta}{1 - \cos\eta}$, and $GM_T/r = GM_T/[A(1 - \cos\eta)]$. So

$E\,(1 - \cos\eta) = \dfrac{A^2}{2B^2}(1 + \cos\eta) - \dfrac{GM_T}{A}$.

For this to hold at every $\eta$, the terms with $\cos\eta$ give $-E = A^2/(2B^2)$, and the others give $E = A^2/(2B^2) - GM_T/A$. Together, $GM_T/A = A^2/B^2$, so $GM_T = A^3/B^2$. Putting in $A$ and $B$ from part (a) gives the second form.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The energy is $E = -A^2/(2B^2) < 0$: the pair is bound, as a closed universe is. Units: $A^3/B^2$ is m³/s², the units of $GM$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Take the age of the universe, $t = 13.7 \times 10^9$ years (MIT's value), as the time since the pair started to move apart. With the result of part (a) and the observed $r$ and $v$, find the present value of $\eta$ (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

Form the number $vt/r$ from the observations: it must equal $\sin\eta(\eta - \sin\eta)/(1 - \cos\eta)^2$. Watch the units: $r$ in km and $t$ in s.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$v$ is negative, so $\sin\eta < 0$: the root lies between $\pi$ and $2\pi$. Solve numerically (bisection is enough).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-eta" label="$\eta$ today:" value="4.297" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-eta" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** One equation, one unknown: the observed ratio $vt/r$ fixes where on the cycloid we are.

<Step title="The observed ratio">

$t = 13.7 \times 3.156 \times 10^{16}$ s $= 4.32 \times 10^{17}$ s and $r = 700 \times 3.086 \times 10^{16}$ km $= 2.16 \times 10^{19}$ km, so $vt/r = -121 \times 4.32 \times 10^{17}/2.16 \times 10^{19} = -2.422$.

</Step>

<Step title="The root">

The function $\sin\eta(\eta - \sin\eta)/(1 - \cos\eta)^2$ is 0 at $\eta = \pi$ and falls without limit as $\eta \to 2\pi$, so it takes the value $-2.422$ once in between: at $\eta = 4.297$ (246°).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Putting $\eta = 4.297$ back: $\sin\eta = -0.9151$, $\eta - \sin\eta = 5.212$, $1 - \cos\eta = 1.403$, and $-0.9151 \times 5.212/1.403^2 = -2.42$. Since $\eta > \pi$, the pair has already turned around: it is falling together, as the blueshift says.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

With the $\eta$ of part (c), find the total mass of the pair, in units of $10^{12}\,M_\odot$ (tolerance 2%). The total luminosity of the two galaxies is about $3 \times 10^{10}\,L_\odot$ (MIT's value). Find the mass-to-light ratio, in solar units (tolerance 3%).

<Hint>

Use the second form of part (b), all in SI units, and divide by $G M_\odot$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-mass" label="$M_T$ in $10^{12}\,M_\odot$:" value="3.99" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-mass" />

<Answer id="p1-ml" label="$M_T/L$ in $M_\odot/L_\odot$:" value="133" tolerance="3%" calc="p1-ml" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Once $\eta$ is known, the cycloid's constants follow from $r$ and $t$, and so does the mass.

<Step title="The constants">

$A = r/(1 - \cos\eta) = 700/1.403 = 498.8$ kpc and $B = t/(\eta - \sin\eta) = 13.7/5.212 = 2.628$ Gyr.

</Step>

<Step title="The mass">

$GM_T = A^3/B^2 = (1.539 \times 10^{22}\ \text{m})^3/(8.293 \times 10^{16}\ \text{s})^2 = 5.30 \times 10^{32}$ m³/s². Dividing by $GM_\odot = 1.327 \times 10^{20}$ m³/s² gives $M_T = 3.99 \times 10^{12}\,M_\odot$.

</Step>

<Step title="Mass to light">

$M_T/L = 3.99 \times 10^{12}/3 \times 10^{10} = 133\,M_\odot/L_\odot$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The stars of a galaxy give a few solar masses per solar luminosity. A ratio over a hundred means that most of the mass of the Local Group is dark: this is one of the classic arguments for dark matter halos far larger than the visible galaxies.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={5}>

MIT's sheet recalls that the Milky Way has a flat rotation curve, so that its mass grows in proportion to the radius if it sits in a spherical dark-matter halo. Take a circular speed $v_c = 220$ km/s (our value) and give the Milky Way half the mass of part (d) (MIT allows equal masses; in reality M31 is somewhat heavier). Out to what radius must its dark matter reach to hold that mass? Give it in kpc (tolerance 3%). Are the halos of the two galaxies separate, or do they overlap?

<Hint>

For a circular orbit in a spherical mass distribution, the notes balance the centripetal acceleration against gravity: $v_c^2/R = GM(<R)/R^2$. (MIT's sheet writes $M(<R) = v_c^2R/(2G)$; see the solution for why we drop the 2.)

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-halo" label="$R$ in kpc:" value="177" tolerance="3%" calc="p1-halo" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A flat rotation curve means $M(<R) \propto R$, so a mass fixes a radius.

<Step title="The radius">

The notes' circular orbit,

<Formula label="Circular orbit" tex="\frac{mv_c^2}{r} = \frac{GMm}{r^2}" cite="reading3" at="eq. (22), p. 10" />

gives $M(<R) = v_c^2R/G$. With $M = 1.997 \times 10^{12}\,M_\odot$ and $G = 4.301 \times 10^{-6}$ kpc (km/s)² $/M_\odot$: $R = GM/v_c^2 = 4.301 \times 10^{-6} \times 1.997 \times 10^{12}/220^2 = 177$ kpc.

</Step>

<Step title="Separate or overlapping">

Two halos of 177 kpc each reach 355 kpc together, about half of the 700 kpc between the galaxies: on this estimate the halos are separate, though not by much on the scale of the Local Group (and a heavier M31 has a larger halo).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

MIT's sheet writes $M(<R) = v_c^2R/(2G)$. For a spherical halo the factor 2 does not belong there: the notes' eq. (22) gives $v_c^2 = GM(<R)/R$ exactly. (A factor 2 appears in $M(<R) = 2\sigma^2R/G$, written with the velocity dispersion $\sigma = v_c/\sqrt2$ of an isothermal halo; that may be its origin.) With the sheet's formula the radius doubles, to 355 kpc, and the two halos would just meet (710 kpc against 700). Either way the answer is that the halos are a few hundred kpc across, comparable with the distance between the galaxies.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Cosmology from Type Ia supernovae" points={60} mit="Problem 2">

The 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics went to members of the two teams that found, with distant supernovae, that the expansion is speeding up. Type Ia supernovae are good standard candles: once corrected, they all have nearly the same peak luminosity, so their apparent brightness measures their luminosity distance, and the luminosity distance against redshift depends on the cosmological parameters.

MIT's problem goes through this the way a real survey does, in two stages. The full analysis fits every parameter at once with a Markov chain Monte Carlo; here, first, a few "nuisance" parameters that describe the supernovae are fitted in a flat universe, where only one cosmological parameter is free ($\Omega_M$, with $\Omega_\Lambda = 1 - \Omega_M$). Then those nuisance parameters are held fixed while $\Omega_M$ and $\Omega_\Lambda$ are both free.

<Part label="a" points={12}>

Plot the luminosity distance $D_L$ against $z$ up to $z = 5$, and again up to $z = 1$ (the range of the supernova data), for five universes:

- $(\Omega_M, \Omega_\Lambda) = (1, 0)$: flat, matter only;
- $(0, 1)$: flat, $\Lambda$ only;
- $(0.3, 0.7)$: flat, the present model;
- $(1.2, 0)$: closed, matter only;
- $(0.3, 0)$: open, matter only.

Use the right form of $D_L$ for flat, open and closed universes. As a check of your code, give $D_L$ at $z = 1$ in Mpc for three of them (tolerance 1% each).

<Hint>

The notes give $D_L = (1 + z)\,f_k(\chi)$, with the comoving distance $\chi = (c/H_0)\int_0^z dz'/E(z')$ and $E^2(z) = \Omega_M(1 + z)^3 + \Omega_k(1 + z)^2 + \Omega_\Lambda$. What is the curvature $k$ in terms of $\Omega_k$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

At $a = 1$ the notes' $\Omega_k = -kc^2/(a^2H^2)$ gives $k = -\Omega_kH_0^2/c^2$. So for $\Omega_k > 0$ (open) $f_k$ is $(c/H_0)\,\sinh(\sqrt{\Omega_k}\,I)/\sqrt{\Omega_k}$, where $I = \int_0^z dz'/E(z')$, and for $\Omega_k < 0$ (closed) the sinh becomes a sin with $\sqrt{|\Omega_k|}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-dl-flat" label="$D_L(z = 1)$ for $(0.3, 0.7)$, in Mpc:" value="6515" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-dl-flat" />

<Answer id="p2-dl-closed" label="$D_L(z = 1)$ for $(1.2, 0)$, in Mpc:" value="4770" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-dl-closed" />

<Answer id="p2-dl-open" label="$D_L(z = 1)$ for $(0.3, 0)$, in Mpc:" value="5790" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-dl-open" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** One integral, $I(z)$, and one curvature function cover all five cases.

<Step title="The formulas">

The notes' comoving distance and luminosity distance:

<Formula label="Comoving distance" tex="\chi = \frac{c}{H_0}\int_{a_\text{em}}^{a_\text{obs}}\frac{da}{a^2E(a)}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (314), p. 71" />

<Formula label="Luminosity distance" tex="D_L = (1 + z)\,f_k(\chi)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (322), p. 73" />

with $f_k$ of eq. (315) (reading 2, p. 72). With $a = 1/(1 + z)$ the integral becomes $(c/H_0)\,I(z)$, $I(z) = \int_0^z dz'/E(z')$, and with $k = -\Omega_kH_0^2/c^2$ (reading 1, eq. (297), p. 68, at $a = 1$):

- flat: $D_L = (1 + z)(c/H_0)\,I$;
- open: $D_L = (1 + z)(c/H_0)\,\sinh(\sqrt{\Omega_k}\,I)/\sqrt{\Omega_k}$;
- closed: $D_L = (1 + z)(c/H_0)\,\sin(\sqrt{|\Omega_k|}\,I)/\sqrt{|\Omega_k|}$.

Here $c/H_0 = 299\,792/71 = 4222$ Mpc.

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers at z = 1">

Flat $(0.3, 0.7)$: $I(1) = 0.7714$, so $D_L = 2 \times 0.7714 \times 4222 = 6515$ Mpc.

Closed $(1.2, 0)$, $\Omega_k = -0.2$: $I(1) = 0.5711$ and $\sin(\sqrt{0.2} \times 0.5711)/\sqrt{0.2} = 0.5649$, so $D_L = 2 \times 0.5649 \times 4222 = 4770$ Mpc.

Open $(0.3, 0)$, $\Omega_k = 0.7$: $I(1) = 0.6527$ and $\sinh(\sqrt{0.7} \times 0.6527)/\sqrt{0.7} = 0.6856$, so $D_L = 2 \times 0.6856 \times 4222 = 5790$ Mpc.

</Step>

(324), p. 73).

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="b1" points={6}>

**The data (our choice, in place of MIT's data file).** A light-curve fitter such as SALT2 or SiFTO (MIT's set uses SiFTO) turns each supernova's light curve into three numbers: the peak apparent magnitude $m^*$, a shape (stretch) parameter $s$ and a colour parameter $c$. Brighter supernovae fade more slowly (larger stretch), and redder ones look fainter, so the distance modulus is formed with corrections for both:

<Formula label="Distance modulus of a supernova" tex="\mu_i(\alpha, \beta, M) = m_i^* - M + \alpha s_i - \beta c_i" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(B), p. 3" />

When the acceleration was first announced, many doubted the cosmological constant, and one worry was dust between the galaxies. How would intergalactic dust change the comparison of part (a), and why was it a concern?

<Answer id="p2-dust" choices={[
  "Dust reddens distant supernovae, which makes them look brighter than they are, so their distances come out too small and real acceleration is hidden.",
  "Dust dims all supernovae by the same amount, near and far: that only shifts the fitted M and cannot change the shape of μ against z.",
  "Dust dims distant supernovae more, through their longer path, so their distances come out too large: just what a cosmological constant does.",
  "Dust scatters light and so raises the redshift of distant supernovae: at a given brightness they seem further away in z, which imitates deceleration."
]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A cosmological constant shows itself as supernovae that are fainter than a matter-only universe predicts. Anything else that makes them fainter can fake it.

<Step title="The effect">

Dust absorbs light, so it adds a positive amount to every $m^*$, and the more dust lies along the path, the more it adds. More distant supernovae shine through more intergalactic space, so the extra dimming grows with $z$. The derived $\mu_i$ and the inferred $D_L$ then come out too large at high $z$: the points rise above the matter-only curves of part (a), towards the curves with $\Lambda$.

</Step>

<Step title="Why it is a concern, and the way out">

Without a test, a universe with matter only plus some grey dust could look like one with $\Lambda$. Ordinary dust also reddens light, and the colour term $-\beta c_i$ corrects for that, but very large ("grey") grains would dim without reddening and escape the correction. The worry faded because dust can only make the supernovae fainter and fainter as $z$ grows, while a cosmological constant predicts that beyond $z$ of about 1 the supernovae come back towards the curve of an open, matter-only universe (the expansion was still decelerating then); supernovae at $z > 1$ showed that turn.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The second choice would be right for dust in the supernova's own galaxy that is the same on average at every $z$; intergalactic dust grows with the path, so it does change the shape. Scattering by dust does not change redshift.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Supernovae below $z = 1$ measure essentially one combination of $\Omega_M$ and $\Omega_\Lambda$: roughly how much the expansion has accelerated. Matter decelerates, $\Lambda$ accelerates, so more of one can be traded for more of the other.

<Step title="The grid">

On a grid with steps of 0.01 (with $\Omega_M \ge 0$, leaving out models without a big bang), the minimum lies at $(\Omega_M, \Omega_\Lambda) = (0.41, 0.86)$, slightly closed, with $\chi^2 = 22.27$. <Step title="Flat or not">

A flat universe fits as well as the best curved one, but so do many curved ones along the ellipse: the supernovae alone do not decide the curvature. Other measurements break the degeneracy: the position of the first acoustic peak of the CMB fixes $\Omega_M + \Omega_\Lambda$ close to 1, along a line that crosses the supernova ellipse.

</Step>

<Step title="Why the ellipse leans">

At low redshift, $D_L \approx (c/H_0)[z + \frac12(1 - q_0)z^2]$ with the deceleration parameter $q_0 = \Omega_M/2 - \Omega_\Lambda$ (a standard expansion, stated by us). To lowest order the data fix $q_0$, that is, $\Omega_\Lambda - \Omega_M/2$: a line of slope $\frac12$. At $z$ near 0.5 to 1, matter matters more (its density grows as $(1 + z)^3$), and the line of constant $D_L$ steepens. Near the minimum of our grid, $\chi^2$ changes least along the direction in which $\Omega_\Lambda$ rises by 1.36 for each unit of $\Omega_M$ (from the second derivatives of $\chi^2$ there). So the long axis runs from small $\Omega_M$ and $\Omega_\Lambda$ to large ones, nearly at right angles to the flat line.

</Step>
