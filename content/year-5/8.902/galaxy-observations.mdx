8.902 begins with galaxies: what astronomers measure and what the measurements show. This page follows the Lecture 1 notes, from the basic quantities of radiative transfer through magnitudes to the main properties of the galaxy population, the hint of dark matter in rotating galaxies, and a first look at how spectra are interpreted. (The notes' photographs of galaxies are credited to other photographers and are not reproduced here.)

## Flux and specific intensity

The **flux** at frequency $\nu$ is the energy arriving per area, per time, per frequency interval:

<Formula label="Flux" tex="F_\nu = \frac{dE_\nu}{dA\,dt\,d\nu}, \qquad [F_\nu] = \text{erg s}^{-1}\,\text{cm}^{-2}\,\text{Hz}^{-1}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (1), p. 4" />

The **specific intensity** counts only the radiation arriving from a small solid angle $d\Omega$: it is the flux per solid angle.

<Formula label="Specific intensity" tex="I_\nu = \frac{dE_\nu}{dA\,dt\,d\nu\,d\Omega}, \qquad [I_\nu] = \text{erg s}^{-1}\,\text{cm}^{-2}\,\text{Hz}^{-1}\,\text{sr}^{-1}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (2), p. 4" />

The two behave differently with distance. The energy crossing any sphere around a source is the same, so

<Formula label="Flux falls as one over distance squared" tex="F_\nu(r_1)\cdot 4\pi r_1^2 = F_\nu(r_2)\cdot 4\pi r_2^2 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{F_\nu(r_1)}{F_\nu(r_2)} = \left(\frac{r_2}{r_1}\right)^2 \;\Rightarrow\; F_\nu \propto \frac{1}{r^2}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (3)–(4), p. 4" />

[The notes' eq. (3) squares $F_\nu(r_2)$ by mistake.] But for an extended source, both the flux from a patch and the solid angle that patch covers fall as $1/r^2$, so

<Formula label="Specific intensity is constant" tex="I_\nu = \frac{F_\nu}{d\Omega}, \quad F_\nu \propto \frac{1}{r^2}, \quad d\Omega \propto \frac{1}{r^2} \;\Rightarrow\; I_\nu = \text{constant}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (5), p. 4" />

## Magnitudes

Astronomers measure brightness on a logarithmic scale. The **apparent magnitude** difference of two objects is

<Formula label="Apparent magnitude" tex="m_1 - m_2 = -2.5\log\left(\frac{(F_\nu)_1}{(F_\nu)_2}\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (6), p. 5" />

(log to base 10). A brighter object has a **smaller** magnitude; 5 magnitudes is a factor of 100 in flux. The zero point needs a reference, and two systems are in use:

- the **Vega system**, calibrated on the bright star Vega (spectral type A0 V), whose spectrum varies with frequency;
- the **AB system**, calibrated on a hypothetical source with the same flux density at every frequency, which in CGS units (erg s⁻¹ cm⁻² Hz⁻¹) is

<Formula label="AB reference flux (erg per second per square centimetre per hertz)" tex="(F_\nu)_\text{AB} = 3.63\times10^{-20}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7), p. 5" />

The **monochromatic magnitude** at one frequency is then

<Formula label="Monochromatic magnitudes" tex="\text{Vega:}\ m_\nu = -2.5\log\left(\frac{F_\nu}{(F_\nu)_\text{Vega}}\right), \qquad \text{AB:}\ m_\nu = -2.5\log\left(\frac{F_\nu}{(F_\nu)_\text{AB}}\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8), p. 5" />

In practice light is collected through a filter with transmission $T_X(\nu)$ (such as the U, B, V, R, I filters, or the u, g, r, i, z filters of the Sloan Digital Sky Survey), and the **band magnitude** compares integrated fluxes:

<Formula label="Band magnitudes" tex="\text{Vega:}\ m_X = -2.5\log\left(\frac{\int F_\nu T_X(\nu)\,d\nu}{\int (F_\nu)_\text{Vega}T_X(\nu)\,d\nu}\right), \qquad \text{AB:}\ m_X = -2.5\log\left(\frac{\int F_\nu T_X(\nu)\,d\nu}{\int (F_\nu)_\text{AB}T_X(\nu)\,d\nu}\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9), p. 5" />

Since $(F_\nu)_\text{AB}$ is constant and $\int T_X\,d\nu = 1$ (with that normalization), the AB denominator is just $(F_\nu)_\text{AB}$, and with $-2.5\log(3.63\times10^{-20}) = 48.6$:

<Formula label="AB magnitudes in practice" tex="m_X = -2.5\log\left(\int F_\nu T_X(\nu)\,d\nu\right) - 48.6, \qquad m_\nu = -2.5\log(F_\nu) - 48.6" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (10)–(11), pp. 5, 7" />

with $F_\nu$ in CGS units. The AB constant was chosen so that both systems agree in the V band; other bands need a conversion, which follows from eq. (9):

<Formula label="Converting between the systems" tex="m_X(\text{AB}) - m_X(\text{Vega}) = -2.5\log\left(\frac{\int (F_\nu)_\text{Vega}T_X(\nu)\,d\nu}{\int (F_\nu)_\text{AB}T_X(\nu)\,d\nu}\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (12), p. 7" />

[The notes print the ratio upside down, with the AB flux on top, and drop the integral in the denominator. Checking with Vega itself: its Vega magnitude is 0 by definition, so its AB magnitude must be $-2.5\log$ of its own flux over the AB reference, as above.]

The notes give $U_\text{AB} = U_\text{Vega} - 0.8$, $B_\text{AB} = B_\text{Vega} - 0.11$, $V_\text{AB} = V_\text{Vega}$ (eq. 13). [For U the sign is reversed in standard tables: Blanton and Roweis (2007) give $U_\text{AB} - U_\text{Vega} \approx +0.79$ and $B_\text{AB} - B_\text{Vega} \approx -0.09$, because Vega is faint in the ultraviolet compared with a flat spectrum.] The lesson of the notes holds either way: always check which system a magnitude is in.

The notes also show how different filters reveal different physics: the Orion Nebula looks like diffuse glowing gas in visible light but like a scattering of point-like stars in X-rays, and the James Webb Space Telescope's filters reach somewhat longer, infrared wavelengths than the mainly visible range of Hubble and SDSS.

## Absolute magnitude and the distance modulus

The **absolute magnitude** $M$ is the apparent magnitude an object would have at 10 parsecs. It measures intrinsic brightness, while $m$ also depends on distance. Since the flux is $L/4\pi D^2$,

<Formula label="Distance modulus" tex="m_X - M_X = 5\log\left(\frac{D}{10\ \text{pc}}\right) \equiv \mu" cite="reading1" at="eq. (14), p. 7" />

<Formula label="Distance modulus derived" tex="m_X = -2.5\log\left(\frac{L}{4\pi D^2}\right) + \text{const}, \quad M_X = -2.5\log\left(\frac{L}{4\pi(10\ \text{pc})^2}\right) + \text{const} \;\Rightarrow\; m_X - M_X = 5\log(D/\text{pc}) - 5" cite="reading1" at="eq. (15), p. 8" />

The **distance modulus** $\mu$ is therefore a measure of distance.

## Colours and surface brightness

With magnitudes in two filters $X$ and $Y$ one defines a **colour**, which does not depend on distance:

<Formula label="Colour" tex="(X - Y) = m_X - m_Y = M_X - M_Y" cite="reading1" at="eq. (16), p. 8" />

A common one is $g - r$. Because a larger magnitude means fainter, a **larger** $g - r$ means relatively less light in the bluer g band: a **redder** object. Images through several filters can also be combined into colour pictures, as the notes show for the X-ray emission of the supernova remnant Cassiopeia A.

The **surface brightness** $I$ (also written $\Sigma$) is the luminosity per area, usually quoted in magnitudes per square arcsecond, $\propto -2.5\log I$ (eq. 17). Spirals and ellipticals have characteristically different profiles:

<Formula label="Exponential and de Vaucouleurs profiles" tex="\text{exponential (spirals):}\ I(r) = I_0e^{-r/r_s}, \qquad \text{de Vaucouleurs (ellipticals):}\ I(r) = I_0e^{-7.67(r/r_e)^{1/4}}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (18), p. 8" />

Both are special cases of the **Sérsic profile** with index $n$:

<Formula label="Sérsic profile" tex="I(r) = I_0\,e^{-(r/r_0)^{1/n}}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (19), p. 9" />

Observed ellipticals have $n \approx 4$ and spirals $n \approx 1$. As the notes say, theory needs to explain this.

## Types of galaxies

Images show three main types:

- **Spirals (Sp):** most common in the field, away from clusters. Discs of gas and stars, young stellar populations, supported by **rotation**, blue colours, exponential surface brightness profiles. Andromeda (M31) is one.
- **Ellipticals (E):** common in clusters. Spheroidal, old stellar populations, supported by **pressure** (random motions of the stars), red colours, de Vaucouleurs profiles. M87 is one.
- **Lenticulars (S0):** a stellar disc but no gas disc, a link between spirals and ellipticals (for example NGC 2787).

## How many galaxies of each luminosity?

The luminosity of an object is its total energy output per time, $L = dE/dt = \int I_\nu\,dA\,d\Omega\,d\nu$ (eq. 20). The number density of galaxies per luminosity interval is well described by the **Schechter function**:

<Formula label="Schechter luminosity function" tex="\phi(L)\,dL = \phi_*\left(\frac{L}{L_*}\right)^\alpha e^{-L/L_*}\,\frac{dL}{L_*}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (21), p. 10" />

with the normalization $\phi_* \approx 0.02\,h^3$ Mpc⁻³, the faint-end slope $\alpha \approx -1.09$, and the characteristic luminosity $L_* \approx 10^{10}\,h^{-2}L_\odot$, where the power law turns into an exponential cutoff. Faint galaxies are far more numerous than bright ones, but galaxies brighter than a few $L_*$ are rare.

## Rotation curves and the first hint of dark matter

Spectra show that spiral galaxies rotate in ordered, circular orbits at about $V_c \sim 200 \pm 50$ km/s. The circular speed can be traced through the motions of stars and, further out, of gas. Far beyond the visible disc it stays **constant**. That is not what the visible mass predicts. For a circular orbit around a central mass,

<Formula label="Circular orbit" tex="\frac{mv_c^2}{r} = \frac{GMm}{r^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (22), p. 10" />

so $v_c \propto r^{-1/2}$ once all the mass is inside the orbit. A flat curve instead needs the enclosed mass to keep growing:

<Formula label="Mass needed for a flat rotation curve" tex="v_c^2 \propto \frac1r\int_0^r 4\pi r'^2\rho(r')\,dr' \;\Rightarrow\; \rho(r) \propto r^{-2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (23), p. 10" />

[The notes write $v_c \propto$ rather than $v_c^2 \propto$. The conclusion is the same either way, since a constant $v_c$ means a constant $v_c^2$, but the relation itself needs the square.] This was one of the first hints of **dark matter**. What is it? The notes summarize what is known:

- it cannot be non-luminous gas, which would show absorption lines;
- dim stars or other compact objects (MACHOs, massive compact halo objects) are ruled out by microlensing surveys, which see too few of the brief brightenings such objects would cause by bending the light of background stars;
- neutrinos are ruled out as the main component: they move so fast (**hot dark matter**) that they cannot be bound in small potential wells, and would give the wrong structure formation;
- WIMPs (weakly interacting massive particles) remain possible, though none has been detected.

General theories include **cold dark matter** (slow, collisionless, interacting only by gravity), **self-interacting** dark matter (which can also scatter off itself), **warm** dark matter (faster than CDM, so fewer small halos form), very light **Bose–Einstein condensate** dark matter (whose de Broglie wavelength is galaxy-sized, giving interference patterns in halos), and **modified Newtonian dynamics** (MOND), which changes gravity instead of adding matter.

Elliptical galaxies, by contrast, show mostly random motions: velocity dispersions $\sigma_v \sim 200$–$300$ km/s with little rotation, $v_c \sim 50$–$100$ km/s.

<WorkedExample title="Weighing a spiral galaxy">
<Problem>
A spiral galaxy rotates at 200 km/s at a radius of 10 kpc (1 kpc = $3.086\times10^{19}$ m). (a) What mass lies inside that radius? (b) If the rotation curve stays flat out to 30 kpc, how much mass is inside 30 kpc?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Inside 10 kpc">
From $v_c^2 = GM/r$: $M = v_c^2r/G = (2\times10^5)^2 \times 3.086\times10^{20}/(6.674\times10^{-11}) = 1.8\times10^{41}$ kg, which is $9\times10^{10}$ solar masses.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Inside 30 kpc">
With $v_c$ constant, $M \propto r$: three times as much, about $2.8\times10^{11}$ solar masses, even though very little light comes from beyond 10 kpc.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Redshifts and distances

Spectra also give the redshift of a galaxy:

<Formula label="Redshift" tex="z = \frac{\lambda_\text{obs} - \lambda_0}{\lambda_0} \quad\text{or}\quad 1 + z = \frac{\lambda_\text{obs}}{\lambda_0}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (24), p. 11" />

For nearby galaxies ($z \ll 1$) the distance is proportional to the redshift, through the Hubble constant:

<Formula label="Hubble's law at small redshift" tex="d \approx \frac{cz}{H_0}, \qquad v = H_0d \approx cz,\quad z \ll 1" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (25)–(26), p. 11" />

where $H_0 = 100\,h$ km/s/Mpc defines the dimensionless $h$. A formal derivation, as the low-redshift limit of all cosmological distances, comes in the cosmology part of the course (the observational-cosmology page).

<WorkedExample title="Distance modulus of a nearby galaxy">
<Problem>
A galaxy has redshift $z = 0.005$. With $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc, how far away is it, and what is its distance modulus? If its absolute magnitude is $M = -21$, what is its apparent magnitude?
</Problem>
<Step title="Distance">
$v = cz = 3\times10^5 \times 0.005 = 1500$ km/s, so $d = 1500/70 = 21.4$ Mpc.
</Step>
<Step title="Distance modulus and magnitude">
$\mu = 5\log(2.14\times10^7) - 5 = 31.65$, so $m = M + \mu = -21 + 31.65 = 10.65$: easily visible in a small telescope.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Stellar population synthesis

Spectra contain much more than velocities. The **spectral energy distribution** (SED) of a galaxy is the sum of the light of its stars. Stellar spectra are roughly black bodies of different temperatures, classified from hot to cool as O, B, A, F, G, K, M, L and T, so the galaxy's flux is a weighted sum:

<Formula label="Population synthesis" tex="F_\nu = N_O F_{\nu,O} + N_B F_{\nu,B} + \ldots" cite="reading1" at="eq. (27), p. 12" />

Finding the numbers $N_O$, $N_B$, $N_A$, … that reproduce an observed SED is the basic idea of **stellar population synthesis**: it tells us which stars, and so roughly what ages, make up a galaxy.

## Summary

- Flux falls as $1/r^2$; specific intensity (flux per solid angle) does not change with distance.
- Magnitudes are logarithmic, $m_1 - m_2 = -2.5\log(F_1/F_2)$, in the Vega or AB system ($m_\text{AB} = -2.5\log F_\nu - 48.6$ in CGS); the distance modulus is $\mu = m - M = 5\log(D/\text{pc}) - 5$, and colours are magnitude differences.
- Spirals: rotating gas-rich discs, young and blue, exponential profiles ($n \approx 1$). Ellipticals: pressure-supported spheroids, old and red, de Vaucouleurs profiles ($n \approx 4$). Lenticulars lie between.
- The Schechter function describes galaxy numbers per luminosity, with $L_* \approx 10^{10}h^{-2}L_\odot$.
- Flat rotation curves at about 200 km/s require $\rho \propto r^{-2}$ far beyond the stars: an early hint of dark matter, whose nature (cold, warm, self-interacting, very light, or modified gravity instead) is still open.
- Redshifts give distances through $d \approx cz/H_0$ for $z \ll 1$; population synthesis decodes the stellar content from the spectrum.
