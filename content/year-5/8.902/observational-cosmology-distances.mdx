The density parameters of the previous pages describe the universe, but nobody can measure $\Omega_{m,0}$ with a ruler. What astronomers measure are redshifts, brightnesses, angles on the sky and counts of objects. This page follows section 1.C of the 8.902 notes (Lecture 11), which connects the two: how redshift arises, and how to define distance in an expanding universe. (The Lecture 11 notes then begin the section on inflation, which the next page covers.)

## Redshift

**Redshift** compares the wavelength we observe with the one emitted:

<Formula label="Definition of redshift" tex="\frac{\lambda_\text{obs}}{\lambda_\text{em}} = 1 + \frac{\lambda_\text{obs} - \lambda_\text{em}}{\lambda_\text{em}} \equiv 1 + z" cite="reading1" at="eq. (306), p. 70" />

In cosmology it comes from the expansion of space. Light travels from the source, at time $t_\text{em}$ and comoving position $\chi_\text{em}$, to us at $t_\text{obs}$ and $\chi_\text{obs}$. Light follows $ds = 0$, which in the Robertson–Walker metric (along a radial line) means

<Formula label="Light rays" tex="ds = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; c\,dt = a(t)\,d\chi" cite="reading1" at="eq. (307), p. 70" />

Follow two successive crests of a wave, A and B, emitted a short time $\delta t_\text{em} = \lambda_\text{em}/c$ apart and received $\delta t_\text{obs} = \lambda_\text{obs}/c$ apart. Both cover the same comoving distance:

<Formula label="Two crests, same comoving path" tex="\int_{\chi_\text{em}}^{\chi_\text{obs}} d\chi = \int_{t_\text{em}}^{t_\text{obs}}\frac{c\,dt}{a(t)} = \int_{t_\text{em} + \delta t_\text{em}}^{t_\text{obs} + \delta t_\text{obs}}\frac{c\,dt}{a(t)} \approx \int_{t_\text{em}}^{t_\text{obs}}\frac{c\,dt}{a(t)} + \frac{c\,\delta t_\text{obs}}{a(t_\text{obs})} - \frac{c\,\delta t_\text{em}}{a(t_\text{em})}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (308), p. 70" />

The two extra pieces must cancel, which gives the key result:

<Formula label="Wavelengths stretch with the universe" tex="\frac{c\,\delta t_\text{obs}}{a(t_\text{obs})} = \frac{c\,\delta t_\text{em}}{a(t_\text{em})} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{\lambda_\text{obs}}{\lambda_\text{em}} = \frac{a_\text{obs}}{a_\text{em}} = 1 + z" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (309)–(310), p. 70" />

Observing today with $a_0 = 1$:

<Formula label="Scale factor and redshift" tex="\frac1a = 1 + z, \qquad a = \frac{1}{1 + z}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (311), p. 70" />

## Light arrives dimmer, twice over

The notes point out that luminosity, energy per time, is hit **twice** by the expansion. Each photon's energy $h\nu$ is reduced by $a_\text{em}/a_\text{obs}$, and the time between arriving photons is stretched by the same factor:

<Formula label="Luminosity and the expansion" tex="\frac{\lambda_\text{obs}}{\lambda_\text{em}} = \frac{\delta t_\text{obs}}{\delta t_\text{em}} = \frac{a_\text{obs}}{a_\text{em}} \;\Rightarrow\; L_\text{obs} = \frac{h\nu_\text{obs}}{\delta t_\text{obs}} = \frac{h\nu_\text{em}}{\delta t_\text{em}}\left(\frac{a_\text{em}}{a_\text{obs}}\right)^2 = L_\text{em}\left(\frac{a_\text{em}}{a_\text{obs}}\right)^2" cite="reading1" at="eq. (312), p. 71" />

For a source at redshift $z$ seen today:

<Formula label="Received luminosity" tex="L_\text{obs} = \frac{L_\text{em}}{(1 + z)^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (313), p. 71" />

## Which distance?

In ordinary, static Euclidean space, every way of measuring the distance to an object gives the same answer. For a source of luminosity $L$ and size $l$:

- the **luminosity distance** $D_L$ is defined by the flux, $F = L/4\pi D_L^2$;
- the **angular diameter distance** $D_A$ is defined by the angle it covers, $\varphi = l/D_A$.

In an expanding universe these are **not** the same. Both are built from a third, theoretical distance.

### Comoving distance

**Comoving coordinates** move with the expansion. The comoving distance light covers from emission to observation follows from $a\,d\chi = c\,dt$, and with $dt = da/\dot a$ and $\dot a = aH = aH_0E(a)$ it can be written with the Friedmann equation:

<Formula label="Comoving distance" tex="\chi(t_\text{em}, t_\text{obs}) = \int_{t_\text{em}}^{t_\text{obs}}\frac{c\,dt}{a(t)} = c\int_{a_\text{em}}^{a_\text{obs}}\frac{da}{a\,\dot a} = \frac{c}{H_0}\int_{a_\text{em}}^{a_\text{obs}}\frac{da}{a^2E(a)}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (314), p. 71" />

It cannot be measured directly, but every observable distance is built from it, via the curvature function

<Formula label="Curvature function" tex="f_k(\chi) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{\sqrt k}\sin\!\left(\chi\sqrt k\right), & k > 0 \\ \chi, & k = 0 \\ \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{|k|}}\sinh\!\left(\chi\sqrt{|k|}\right), & k < 0 \end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (315), p. 72" />

### Angular diameter distance

An object of size $l$ at comoving distance $\chi$ covers an angle $\varphi$. Its two ends are at the same $\chi$ and time, and the light left them when the scale factor was $a_\text{em} = 1/(1+z)$, so its physical size then was

<Formula label="Size and angle" tex="\varphi = \frac{l}{D_A}, \qquad l = a_\text{em}\,f_k(\chi)\,\varphi = \frac{1}{1 + z}\,f_k(\chi)\,\varphi" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (316)–(317), p. 72" />

<Formula label="Angular diameter distance" tex="D_A = \frac{1}{1 + z}\,f_k(\chi)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (318), p. 72" />

### Luminosity distance

Light emitted by the source spreads over a sphere around it. When it reaches us, at $a_\text{obs} = 1$, the sphere's area is $4\pi f_k^2(\chi)$, and the luminosity it carries has been reduced by $(1 + z)^2$:

<Formula label="Flux from a distant source" tex="F = \frac{L}{4\pi D_L^2}, \qquad F = \frac{L}{4\pi f_k^2(\chi)\,(1 + z)^2} = \frac{L}{4\pi\left[f_k(\chi)(1 + z)\right]^2}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (319)–(321), p. 72" />

[The notes' eqs. (320) and (321) drop or add a square: the area is $4\pi f_k^2(\chi)$, and the last denominator has $f_k(\chi)$, not $f_k^2(\chi)$, inside the square bracket.] So

<Formula label="Luminosity distance" tex="D_L = (1 + z)\,f_k(\chi)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (322), p. 73" />

<Formula label="Relation between the two" tex="D_A = \frac{D_L}{(1 + z)^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (323), p. 73" />

so $D_A \approx D_L$ only when $z \ll 1$.

## The Einstein–de Sitter example

For the simplest model, flat and matter-only ($\Omega_{m,0} = 1$, everything else 0), the integral can be done in closed form:

<Formula label="Distances in an Einstein–de Sitter universe" tex="D_A = \frac{2c}{H_0}\,\frac{1}{1 + z}\left(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + z}}\right), \qquad D_L = \frac{2c}{H_0}\,(1 + z)\left(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + z}}\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (324), p. 73" />

For $z \ll 1$, $1/\sqrt{1+z} \approx 1 - z/2$, and both reduce to Hubble's law:

<Formula label="Small redshifts" tex="D_A \approx D_L \approx \frac{2c}{H_0}\left(1 - \left(1 - \frac{z}{2}\right)\right) = \frac{c}{H_0}\,z" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (325)–(326), p. 73" />

The comoving distance gives the same at small $z$: with $E \approx 1$ and $a \approx 1 - z$, $\int_{a_\text{em}}^1 da/a^2 \approx 1 - a_\text{em} \approx z$, so $\chi \approx cz/H_0$ as well (eqs. 327–332, p. 73; the notes take a slightly different route. In fact $\int_{a_\text{em}}^1 da/a^2 = 1/a_\text{em} - 1 = z$ exactly, so only $E \approx 1$ is an approximation). All distance measures agree nearby, where Hubble measured.

## The flat universe in general

For any flat model, $k = 0$ and $f_k(\chi) = \chi$. Changing the variable from $a$ to $z$ ($a = 1/(1+z)$, $da = -dz/(1+z)^2$) turns the integral into one over redshift:

<Formula label="Angular diameter distance, flat case" tex="D_A = \frac{1}{1 + z}\,\chi = \frac{c}{H_0}\,\frac{1}{1 + z}\int_{a_\text{em}}^{1}\frac{da}{a^2E(a)} = \frac{c}{H_0}\,\frac{1}{1 + z}\int_0^z\left[\Omega_{m,0}(1 + z')^3 + \Omega_{r,0}(1 + z')^4 + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}\right]^{-1/2}dz'" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (333)–(335), pp. 73–74" />

<Formula label="Luminosity distance, flat case" tex="D_L = \frac{c}{H_0}\,(1 + z)\int_0^z\left[\Omega_{m,0}(1 + z')^3 + \Omega_{r,0}(1 + z')^4 + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}\right]^{-1/2}dz'" cite="reading1" at="eq. (336), p. 74" />

(The notes use $z$ for both the integration variable and the upper limit; $z'$ is the variable here.) These integrals are done numerically. The notes plot all three distances for the Planck values $\Omega_m = 0.315$, $H_0 = 67.4$ km/s/Mpc and point out that by $z \sim 1$ they differ significantly. [The notes' plot itself looks wrong at high redshift: its curves lie far above the values these formulas give, and its $D_A$ never turns over. For these parameters $D_A$ peaks near $z \approx 1.6$, at about 1800 Mpc, just as the Einstein–de Sitter $D_A$ peaks in the graph above.]

<WorkedExample title="A galaxy at z = 1">
<Problem>
For flat ΛCDM with the Planck values $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.315$, $\Omega_{\Lambda,0} = 0.6847$, $H_0 = 67.4$ km/s/Mpc, the integral in eq. (335) for $z = 1$ gives a comoving distance of about 3400 Mpc. Find $D_A$ and $D_L$, and the angle covered by a galaxy 10 kpc across.
</Problem>
<Step title="The two distances">
$D_A = 3400/2 = 1700$ Mpc and $D_L = 3400 \times 2 = 6800$ Mpc: a factor $(1 + z)^2 = 4$ apart. (For comparison, $c/H_0 = 4450$ Mpc.)
</Step>
<Step title="The angle">
$\varphi = l/D_A = 0.01/1700 = 5.9\times10^{-6}$ rad, about **1.2 arcseconds**. Using the luminosity distance instead would have given a size four times too large for the measured angle; using the comoving distance, twice too large.
</Step>

## Counting objects: the comoving volume

To turn the number of objects counted in a patch of sky into a number density, we need the **comoving volume** in a solid angle $d\Omega$ between redshifts $z$ and $z + dz$. A volume is an area times a depth. The proper area of the patch is $D_A^2\,d\Omega$; comoving areas are larger by $1/a^2 = (1 + z)^2$; the comoving depth is $d\chi$:

<Formula label="Comoving volume element, built up" tex="dV_\chi = \left(D_A^2\,d\Omega\right)(1 + z)^2\,d\chi, \qquad A_\text{comoving} = \frac{1}{a^2}A_\text{proper} = (1 + z)^2A_\text{proper}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (337)–(340), pp. 74–75" />

With $d\chi = (c/H_0)\,dz/E(z)$:

<Formula label="Comoving volume element" tex="dV_\chi = \frac{c}{H_0}\,\frac{(1 + z)^2D_A^2}{E(z)}\,d\Omega\,dz = \frac{c}{H_0}\,\frac{f_k^2(\chi)}{E(z)}\,dz\,d\Omega" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (341)–(343), p. 75" />

The last form uses $D_A = f_k(\chi)/(1 + z)$: it is just the comoving area $f_k^2\,d\Omega$ times the comoving depth $d\chi$. [The notes' final eq. (344) contains a stray factor r; it should read $f_k^2(\chi)\,d\Omega\,(d\chi/dz)\,dz$.]

## Summary

- Redshift measures how much the universe has expanded since the light left: $1 + z = a_\text{obs}/a_\text{em}$, and $a = 1/(1+z)$ for light received today.
- A source's luminosity reaches us reduced by $(1 + z)^2$: one factor from photon energy, one from arrival rate.
- The comoving distance $\chi = (c/H_0)\int da/(a^2E)$ is the theoretical building block. Observable distances: $D_A = f_k(\chi)/(1 + z)$ from angles and $D_L = (1 + z)f_k(\chi)$ from fluxes, with $D_L = (1 + z)^2D_A$.
- All agree with Hubble's law, $cz/H_0$, for $z \ll 1$, and differ strongly by $z \sim 1$. In an Einstein–de Sitter universe $D_A$ even has a maximum at $z = 1.25$.
- The comoving volume element $dV = (c/H_0)\,f_k^2(\chi)\,dz\,d\Omega/E(z)$ turns counts of objects into densities.
