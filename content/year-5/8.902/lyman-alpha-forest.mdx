The last lecture of 8.902 opens Part IV, "Selected topics", with a tool that ties several earlier pages together. The CMB page mentioned that the first stars and quasars reionized the universe; the nucleosynthesis page said that deuterium is measured in "the Lyman-α forest". This page follows the 8.902 Lecture 21 notes, which explain what the forest is, what it tells us about the gas between the galaxies, and how it pins down the end of reionization.

## Quasars as backlights

**Quasars** are galaxies whose central supermassive black hole is swallowing gas so fast that the hot accretion disk outshines the whole galaxy. They are so bright that they can be seen out to very high redshift, which makes them ideal **backlights**: everything between the quasar and us leaves its fingerprint as absorption lines in the quasar's spectrum.

The quasar's own hydrogen emits strongly in the **Lyman-α** line, the transition from the $n = 2$ to the $n = 1$ level of hydrogen, at 1216 Å (ultraviolet). On the way to us, the light passes clouds of **neutral hydrogen** (HI) at lower redshifts, $z_\text{abs} < z_\text{em}$. Each cloud absorbs at 1216 Å **in its own rest frame**. After that, the expansion stretches both the emission line and every absorption line, by different amounts. We receive:

- the quasar's emission line at $1216\,\text{Å}\,(1 + z_\text{em})$;
- one absorption line for each cloud, at $1216\,\text{Å}\,(1 + z_\text{abs})$, always **short-ward** (blue-ward) of the emission line.

With many clouds along the line of sight, these lines form the **Lyman-α forest**. Since each line's wavelength gives the cloud's redshift, the spectrum is a one-dimensional map of the gas between the galaxies, the **intergalactic medium** (IGM): its density, ionization, temperature, chemistry and structure.

The notes compare two real spectra (shown in the PDF, not reproduced here): a nearby quasar at $z = 0.158$, which shows only a handful of absorption lines, and one at $z = 3.62$, which shows a dense forest on the short-wavelength side of its Lyman-α peak. The farther the quasar, the more hydrogen the light crosses.

Some practical points from the notes:

- Other elements are recognized by pairs of lines with a fixed spacing (doublets), such as the magnesium doublet at 2796 and 2803 Å. [The notes write this as "μ0II (λ = 2795 Å, 2802 Å)"; the lines are those of ionized magnesium, Mg II.]
- The forest only appears between an observed wavelength of 1216 Å and the observed emission line at $1216\,\text{Å}\,(1 + z_\text{em})$: light emitted at a shorter wavelength than Lyman-α is stretched to 1216 Å at some point on its way, where any neutral hydrogen can absorb it.
- Absorbers come in three kinds, by their **column density** $N_\text{HI}$ (hydrogen atoms per cm² along the line of sight):
  - $N_\text{HI} \lesssim 10^{17}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$: narrow lines, the forest itself;
  - $N_\text{HI} \gtrsim 10^{17}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$: **Lyman-limit systems**, thick enough to absorb completely all photons that can ionize hydrogen, those with rest wavelength below 912 Å (13.6 eV);
  - the thickest: **damped Lyman-α systems**, whose lines become very broad. [The notes put their threshold at $10^{32}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$; the usual definition is $N_\text{HI} \geq 2\times10^{20}\ \text{cm}^{-2}$.]

## The Gunn–Peterson test

Quasar spectra also tell us in what **state** the baryons are. If the IGM contained a substantial amount of neutral hydrogen spread smoothly through space, every part of the spectrum short-ward of Lyman-α would be absorbed at some redshift: there would be no forest of separate lines, just a complete **Gunn–Peterson trough**. [The notes say the "high-wavelength region" is absorbed; it is the region at wavelengths shorter than the quasar's Lyman-α line.]

What is observed:

- for quasars at $z \gtrsim 6$ the light short-ward of Lyman-α is almost completely suppressed: the notes show a quasar at $z = 6.13$ with a Gunn–Peterson trough short-ward of its Lyman-α line (at $1216 \times 7.13 \approx 8670$ Å), beyond a small "near-zone" right next to the line where the quasar's own light has ionized its surroundings;
- for $z \lesssim 6$ there is only a forest of individual lines.

So the hydrogen was **mostly neutral above $z \approx 6$ and mostly ionized below it**. Combined with the earlier pages: the universe became neutral at recombination ($z \approx 1100$), stayed neutral through the dark ages until the first stars and galaxies formed, and was reionized by them, a process complete by about $z \approx 6$, roughly a billion years after the Big Bang.

## How much neutral hydrogen? The optical depth

To make this quantitative the notes compute the **optical depth** $\tau$: the light transmitted at each observed wavelength is reduced by a factor $e^{-\tau}$,

<Formula label="Absorption along the line of sight" tex="F(\lambda_\text{obs}) = F\big(\lambda_\text{em}(1 + z)\big)\,e^{-\tau}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (494), p. 117" />

A photon crossing a hydrogen cloud can be absorbed by an atom in the ground state, which jumps to an excited level; the atom then falls back and re-emits a photon in a random direction, so the photon is lost from the line of sight. Each transition has a frequency-dependent **cross-section**, given by atomic physics as

<Formula label="Absorption cross-section" tex="\sigma(\nu) = \frac{\pi e^2}{m_e c}\,f\,\phi(\nu - \nu_0)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (495), p. 117" />

(in Gaussian units), where $f$ is the **oscillator strength** of the transition and $\phi$ the line profile (a Voigt profile, normalized so that $\int\phi\,d\nu = 1$). For Lyman-α the prefactor is $\sigma_0 \approx 10^{-2}\ \text{cm}^2\,\text{Hz}$. The optical depth adds up $d\tau = n_\text{HI}\,\sigma(\nu)\,dl$ along the proper path length $dl$, which we convert to redshift:

<Formula label="Proper length and redshift" tex="dl = c\,dt = c\,\frac{da}{\dot a} = \frac{c}{a}\,\frac{da}{H} = (1 + z)\,c\,\frac{da}{H}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (496), p. 117" />

With $a = 1/(1 + z)$, $|da/dz| = 1/(1 + z)^2$, so

<Formula label="Path length per unit redshift" tex="dl = \frac{c\,dz}{(1 + z)H(z)} = c\left(H_0(1 + z)\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}(1 + z)^3 + \Omega_\Lambda}\right)^{-1} dz" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (497)–(498), p. 117" />

(the notes write a capital M in the subscript). In the matter-dominated regime, $z \gtrsim 1$, the vacuum term can be dropped:

<Formula label="Matter-dominated path length" tex="dl = \frac{c\,dz}{H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}}\,(1 + z)^{5/2}}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (499), p. 118" />

Light observed at frequency $\nu$ had frequency $\nu(1 + z')$ when it passed redshift $z'$. Summing the absorption along the way,

<Formula label="Optical depth as an integral" tex="\tau_\nu = \sigma_0\,\frac{c}{H_0\Omega_{m,0}^{1/2}}\int_0^z dz'\,\frac{n_\text{HI}(z')}{(1 + z')^{5/2}}\,\phi\big(\nu(1 + z') - \nu_0\big)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (500), p. 118" />

The line profile is very narrow compared with the range of redshifts, so it acts like a delta function: only gas at the one redshift where the light has been stretched to exactly $\nu_0$ contributes. Doing the integral and writing $\lambda_0 = c/\nu_0$ gives the **Gunn–Peterson optical depth**:

<Formula label="Gunn–Peterson optical depth" tex="\tau_\nu(z) = \sigma_0\,\frac{n_\text{HI}(z)\,\lambda_0}{H_0\,\Omega_{m,0}^{1/2}\,(1 + z)^{3/2}}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (501)–(502), p. 118" />

where $z$ is the redshift at which the absorption happens, $\lambda(1 + z) = \lambda_0$. Evaluating it at every frequency gives the optical depth across the whole spectrum.

## Neutral gas would absorb everything

The notes evaluate this at $z = 3$, assuming all the hydrogen is **neutral and uniformly spread**:

<Formula label="Values for the estimate" tex="H_0 = 70\ \text{km/s/Mpc} = 2.3\times10^{-18}\ \text{s}^{-1}, \quad n_\text{HI} = \frac{\rho_\text{crit}\Omega_{b,0}}{m_\text{H}}(1 + z)^3 \sim 10^{-5}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\left(\frac{1 + z}{4}\right)^3, \quad \lambda_0 = 1216\ \text{Å}, \quad \sigma_0 = 10^{-2}\ \text{cm}^2\,\text{Hz}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (503), p. 118" />

[The notes give the units of $\sigma_0$ as cm² here and as cm² Hz⁻¹ on p. 117. Since the profile $\phi$ has units Hz⁻¹ and $\sigma(\nu)$ is an area, $\sigma_0$ is in cm² Hz; the number is right.]

The result is a Lyman-α optical depth $\tau \sim 10^5$: the quasar's light short-ward of Lyman-α would be extinguished completely. But at $z = 3$ the observed optical depth is only about 1. Either the gas is not in intergalactic space (but we see it there), or it is **not neutral**. To bring $\tau$ down to about 1, the **neutral fraction** must be $X_\text{HI} \equiv n_\text{HI}/n_\text{H} \sim 10^{-5}$.

</Problem>
<Step title="The pieces">
In CGS units: numerator $\sigma_0 n_\text{HI}\lambda_0 = 10^{-2} \times 10^{-5} \times 1.216\times10^{-5} = 1.2\times10^{-12}\ \text{s}^{-1}$. Denominator $H_0\sqrt{0.3}\,(4)^{3/2} = 2.27\times10^{-18} \times 0.548 \times 8 = 9.9\times10^{-18}\ \text{s}^{-1}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The optical depth">
$\tau \approx 1.2\times10^{-12}/9.9\times10^{-18} = 1.2\times10^5$, as in the notes. A neutral fraction of $1/(1.2\times10^5) \approx 8\times10^{-6}$, about $10^{-5}$, brings it down to 1.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## What keeps the gas ionized?

**Collisions?** The widths of the absorption lines give the gas temperature. Lines about 20 km/s wide mean thermal speeds of that size:

<Formula label="Temperature from line widths" tex="\Delta v \sim 20\ \text{km/s}, \qquad \tfrac12 m v^2 \sim kT \;\Rightarrow\; 20\ \text{km/s} \sim \sqrt{\frac{2kT}{m}} \;\Rightarrow\; T \sim 30{,}000\ \text{K} \sim 3\ \text{eV}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (504), p. 119" />

[With the proton mass, $T = mv^2/2k \approx 2.4\times10^4$ K, about 2 eV; the notes round up.] The notes conclude that this is not enough to ionize hydrogen, since the typical energy is far below 13.6 eV. [That is too strong: the fast tail of the thermal distribution does ionize most of the hydrogen at these temperatures; in collisional equilibrium a few percent stays neutral at about 2.4 × 10⁴ K. What collisions cannot do is bring the neutral fraction down to the $10^{-5}$ the forest requires.] Collisions alone are not enough.

**Photoionization.** The combined ultraviolet light of all galaxies and quasars (the ultraviolet background) photoionizes each hydrogen atom at a rate $\Gamma \sim 10^{-12}\ \text{s}^{-1}$. [The notes call $\Gamma$ "ionizing photons per second"; it is the number of ionizations per second per atom.] The ionization rate per volume is $\Gamma n_\text{HI}$, so each atom is ionized after about

<Formula label="Ionization timescale" tex="\frac{n_\text{HI}}{n_\text{HI}\,\Gamma} \sim 10^{12}\ \text{s} \sim 30{,}000\ \text{yr}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (505), p. 119" />

Ions and electrons recombine at the rate $R\,n_\text{HII}n_e$ per volume, with recombination coefficient $R = 4.3\times10^{-13}\,(T/10^4\ \text{K})^{-0.7}$ cm³/s. Each ion waits about

<Formula label="Recombination timescale" tex="\frac{n_\text{HII}}{R\,n_e\,n_\text{HII}} \sim 2\times10^{17}\ \text{s}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (506), p. 119" />

for an electron. [The notes convert this to $3\times10^6$ years; $2\times10^{17}$ s is about $6\times10^9$ years, so recombination is even slower than they say.] Recombination is far slower than ionization, so photoionization can easily keep the gas ionized.

**The equilibrium neutral fraction.** Setting the two rates equal,

<Formula label="Ionization equilibrium" tex="R\,n_\text{HII}\,n_e = \Gamma\,n_\text{HI}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (507), p. 119" />

and using $n_\text{HII} \approx n_e \approx n_\text{H}$ (almost everything is ionized, $n_\text{HI} \ll n_\text{HII}$) with $n_\text{HI} = X_\text{HI}n_\text{H}$,

<Formula label="Equilibrium condition" tex="R\,n_\text{H}^2 = X_\text{HI}\,n_\text{H}\,\Gamma" cite="reading1" at="eq. (508), p. 119" />

<Formula label="Neutral fraction" tex="X_\text{HI} \approx \frac{R\,n_\text{H}}{\Gamma} \sim 5\times10^{-6}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (509), p. 119" />

exactly the tiny neutral fraction the Lyman-α forest demands. The forest's lines come from the slightly denser clumps and filaments where a little more neutral hydrogen survives.

## The history of reionization

Putting it together, the neutral fraction $X_\text{HI}$ has a simple history, which the notes sketch:

- before recombination the hot universe was ionized ($X_\text{HI} \approx 0$);
- at recombination, $z \approx 1100$, it became neutral ($X_\text{HI} \approx 1$), and stayed so while there was no strong source of ionizing light;
- during the **epoch of reionization**, roughly $z \approx 15$ down to 6 in the notes' sketch, the first stars, galaxies and quasars ionized it again;
- since then $X_\text{HI}$ has been tiny, $\sim 10^{-5}$, held there by the ultraviolet background.

Exactly how and when reionization happened is an active research field. The notes mention radio telescopes such as HERA, which look for the 21 cm line of the neutral hydrogen during that era, and large simulations of reionization such as THESAN (whose image, used with permission, opens Part IV of the notes and is not reproduced here).

## Summary

- A quasar's light is absorbed by every neutral hydrogen cloud on the way: each absorbs at 1216 Å in its own frame, seen at $1216\,\text{Å}\,(1 + z_\text{abs})$, short-ward of the quasar's emission line. The many lines are the **Lyman-α forest**, a map of the intergalactic medium.
- Absorbers range from thin forest clouds ($N_\text{HI} \lesssim 10^{17}$ cm⁻²) through Lyman-limit systems to damped Lyman-α systems.
- The **Gunn–Peterson optical depth** $\tau = \sigma_0 n_\text{HI}\lambda_0/\big(H_0\Omega_{m,0}^{1/2}(1+z)^{3/2}\big)$ would be about $10^5$ for neutral gas at $z = 3$; the observed $\tau \sim 1$ requires a neutral fraction $X_\text{HI} \sim 10^{-5}$.
- Collisions in gas at $T \sim 3\times10^4$ K cannot make the neutral fraction that small, but photoionization by the ultraviolet background ($\Gamma \sim 10^{-12}$ s⁻¹) is much faster than recombination, and the equilibrium $X_\text{HI} \approx Rn_\text{H}/\Gamma \sim 5\times10^{-6}$ matches.
- Quasars beyond $z \approx 6$ show a complete **Gunn–Peterson trough**: reionization ended around $z \approx 6$.
