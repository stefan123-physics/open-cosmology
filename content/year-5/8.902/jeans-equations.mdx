Single orbits, the subject of the previous page, are not enough to describe a galaxy of $10^{11}$ stars. What is needed is how **all** the stars are distributed over positions and velocities. Lecture 6 of 8.902 (section 3.C of the notes) introduces that distribution, derives the equation it obeys and extracts from it fluid-like equations that can be compared with observations. (The notes open with the luminosity–velocity relations, which the previous page covers, and close by starting section 3.D on stability, which the next page covers.)

## The phase-space distribution function

Each star has a position $\vec x$ and a velocity $\vec v$: a point in six-dimensional **phase space**. The **phase-space distribution function** $f$ is defined so that

<Formula label="Phase-space distribution function" tex="f(\vec x, \vec v, t)\,d^3\vec x\,d^3\vec v" cite="reading1" at="eq. (164), p. 37" />

is the probability that, at time $t$, a randomly chosen star lies within $d^3\vec x$ of $\vec x$ and $d^3\vec v$ of $\vec v$. As a probability it is normalized at all times:

<Formula label="Normalization" tex="\int f(\vec x, \vec v, t)\,d^3\vec x\,d^3\vec v = 1" cite="reading1" at="eq. (165), p. 38" />

## The collisionless Boltzmann equation

Stars are neither created nor destroyed, and without collisions they move smoothly through phase space. Probability is therefore conserved as it flows, so $f$ obeys a continuity equation in six dimensions. With the phase-space vector $\vec w = (\vec x, \vec v)$,

<Formula label="Six-dimensional continuity equation" tex="\vec w = (\vec x, \vec v), \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial\vec w}\cdot\left(f\dot{\vec w}\right) = 0" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (166)–(167), p. 38" />

the same form as the continuity equation of a 3D fluid. Splitting $\vec w$ into its parts, with $\dot{\vec x} = \vec v$ and $\dot{\vec v} = -\nabla\phi$ (which does not depend on $\vec v$, while $\vec v$ does not depend on $\vec x$, so both can be taken out of the derivatives):

<Formula label="Expanding the continuity equation" tex="0 = \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial\vec x}\cdot(f\vec v) + \frac{\partial}{\partial\vec v}\cdot\left(f(-\nabla\phi)\right) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \vec v\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial\vec x} - \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\vec x}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial\vec v}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (168), p. 38" />

This is the **collisionless Boltzmann equation** (CBE), also called the Vlasov equation:

<Formula label="Collisionless Boltzmann equation" tex="\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \vec v\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial\vec x} - \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\vec x}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial\vec v} = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (169), p. 38" />

Equivalently, $df/dt = 0$ along the path of any star: the phase-space density around a star never changes as it moves. (The equation describes stars in the smooth potential, which the relaxation-time page showed is exactly the situation in galaxies.)

## Moments of the distribution

Solving the CBE in full is hard. Instead one takes averages over velocity, **moments**, which are the quantities observations actually measure. The number density, mean velocity and velocity dispersion are

<Formula label="Density" tex="n = \int f\,d^3\vec v" cite="reading1" at="eq. (170), p. 38" />

<Formula label="Mean velocity" tex="\bar v_i = \frac1n\int v_if\,d^3\vec v" cite="reading1" at="eq. (171), p. 39" />

<Formula label="Velocity dispersion" tex="\overline{v_iv_j} = \frac1n\int v_iv_jf\,d^3\vec v, \qquad \sigma_{ij}^2 = \overline{v_iv_j} - \bar v_i\bar v_j = \overline{(v_i - \bar v_i)(v_j - \bar v_j)}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (172), p. 39" />

(Here $f$ is taken to count stars rather than probability, so that $n$ is a number density. The notes call these the first, second and third moments; in the usual counting they are the zeroth, first and second.)

## The continuity equation

Integrate the CBE over all velocities, term by term:

<Formula label="Integrating the CBE over velocity" tex="\int d^3\vec v\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \int d^3\vec v\,\vec v\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial\vec x} - \int d^3\vec v\,\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\vec x}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial\vec v} = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (173), p. 39" />

The first term is $\partial n/\partial t$; the second, with the $\vec x$-derivative taken out of the integral, is $\nabla\cdot(n\bar{\vec v})$; the third is a total derivative in $\vec v$, which vanishes because $f \to 0$ for very large speeds (eq. 174). What remains is the ordinary **continuity equation**:

<Formula label="Continuity equation" tex="\frac{\partial n}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial\vec x}\cdot\left(n\bar{\vec v}\right) = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (175), p. 39" />

## The Jeans equations

Multiply the CBE by a velocity component $v_j$ and integrate again (eq. 176). The three terms become (eq. 177):

- the time derivative: $\partial(n\bar v_j)/\partial t = n\,\partial\bar v_j/\partial t - \bar v_j\sum_i\partial(n\bar v_i)/\partial x_i$, using the continuity equation;
- the streaming term: $\sum_i\partial\left(n\overline{v_iv_j}\right)/\partial x_i = \sum_i\partial\left[n(\sigma_{ij}^2 + \bar v_i\bar v_j)\right]/\partial x_i$;
- the force term, integrated by parts in velocity: the integral equals $-n\,\partial\phi/\partial x_j$, and since it enters the CBE with a minus sign it contributes $+n\,\partial\phi/\partial x_j$.

Putting them together,

<Formula label="Collecting the terms" tex="n\frac{\partial\bar v_j}{\partial t} - \bar v_j\sum_i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}(n\bar v_i) + \sum_i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\left[n\left(\sigma_{ij}^2 + \bar v_i\bar v_j\right)\right] + n\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_j} = 0" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (178)–(179), pp. 40–41" />

[The notes' eq. (178) has $x_i$ in the last derivative; it should be $x_j$, as in their next line.] Expanding the derivative of $n\bar v_i\bar v_j$, one piece cancels the second term, leaving

<Formula label="The Jeans equation, multiplied by n" tex="n\frac{\partial\bar v_j}{\partial t} + \sum_i(n\bar v_i)\frac{\partial\bar v_j}{\partial x_i} + \sum_i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\left(n\sigma_{ij}^2\right) + n\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_j} = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (180), p. 41" />

and dividing by $n$, the **Jeans equation**:

<Formula label="The Jeans equation" tex="\frac{\partial\bar v_j}{\partial t} + \sum_i\bar v_i\frac{\partial\bar v_j}{\partial x_i} = -\frac1n\sum_i\frac{\partial\left(n\sigma_{ij}^2\right)}{\partial x_i} - \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_j}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (181), p. 41" />

It looks just like the Euler equation of fluid dynamics. Term by term (eq. 182): the acceleration of the stellar "fluid"; the change of velocity carried along by the flow (the notes call it "kinematic viscosity/shear"; it is the advective term of the fluid equations); a **pressure** term, in which the random motions $n\sigma_{ij}^2$ push like gas pressure, but can be different in different directions; and **gravity**.

## Spherical systems

In spherical coordinates the Jeans equations become complicated. For a **steady**, **spherically symmetric** system, though, most terms vanish:

- $\partial/\partial t = 0$ (steady state);
- $\bar v_r = 0$ (no net inflow or outflow);
- $\bar v_\theta = \bar v_\phi = 0$ (no rotation, which would break the symmetry);
- $\sigma_{r\phi}^2 = \sigma_{r\theta}^2 = 0$ and $\sigma_{\phi\phi}^2 = \sigma_{\theta\theta}^2 \equiv \sigma_t^2$ (symmetry).

Only the radial equation is left:

<Formula label="Radial Jeans equation" tex="\frac1n\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(n\sigma_{rr}^2\right) + \frac{2\left(\sigma_{rr}^2 - \sigma_t^2\right)}{r} = -\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r} = -\frac{GM(<r)}{r^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (183), p. 42" />

The orbits can be nearly circular ($\sigma_{rr}^2 \ll \sigma_t^2$), nearly radial ($\sigma_{rr}^2 \gg \sigma_t^2$) or isotropic ($\sigma_{rr}^2 = \sigma_t^2$), which the **anisotropy parameter** measures:

<Formula label="Anisotropy parameter" tex="\beta = 1 - \frac{\sigma_t^2}{\sigma_{rr}^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (184), p. 42" />

<Formula label="Radial Jeans equation with anisotropy" tex="\frac1n\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(n\sigma_{rr}^2\right) + \frac{2\beta\sigma_{rr}^2}{r} = -\frac{GM(<r)}{r^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (185), p. 42" />

## Weighing a galaxy with the Jeans equation

Solving for the enclosed mass and writing the derivatives logarithmically:

<Formula label="Mass from the Jeans equation" tex="M(<r) = -\frac{r^2}{G}\left(\frac1n\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(n\sigma_{rr}^2\right) + \frac{2\beta\sigma_{rr}^2}{r}\right) = -\frac{r\sigma_{rr}^2}{G}\left(\frac{d\ln n}{d\ln r} + \frac{d\ln\sigma_{rr}^2}{d\ln r} + 2\beta\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (186), p. 42" />

The notes call the right side measurable: the density profile of a tracer population (stars, globular clusters, satellite galaxies) and its radial velocity dispersion $\sigma_{rr}$. In practice telescopes measure the dispersion along the line of sight, a mixture of $\sigma_{rr}$ and $\sigma_t$, and turning it into $\sigma_{rr}$ itself depends on $\beta$. So $\beta$ is hard to pin down, and it is the main uncertainty of such mass estimates (the **mass–anisotropy degeneracy**). The method assumes spherical symmetry and a steady state.

<WorkedExample title="An isothermal sphere and an elliptical galaxy">
<Problem>
(a) A tracer population has isotropic orbits ($\beta = 0$), a constant velocity dispersion $\sigma = 150$ km/s and density $n \propto r^{-2}$. What is $M(<10\ \text{kpc})$? Use $G = 0.0043$ pc (km/s)² $M_\odot^{-1}$. (b) If instead $n \propto r^{-3}$, how does the answer change?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The isothermal sphere">
The bracket is $-2 + 0 + 0$, so $M = 2\sigma^2r/G = 2 \times 150^2 \times 10^4/0.0043 = 1.05\times10^{11}M_\odot$. The corresponding circular speed is $v_c = \sqrt{GM/r} = \sqrt2\,\sigma = 212$ km/s: a flat rotation curve.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A steeper tracer">
The bracket is now $-3$, so $M = 3\sigma^2r/G = 1.6\times10^{11}M_\odot$, 50% more. The same velocities imply different masses depending on how the tracers are distributed, which is why both $n(r)$ and $\beta$ must be known.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The phase-space distribution function $f(\vec x, \vec v, t)$ gives the probability of finding a star at each position and velocity.
- Stars move without collisions, so $f$ obeys a 6D continuity equation, the **collisionless Boltzmann equation** $\partial f/\partial t + \vec v\cdot\partial f/\partial\vec x - \nabla\phi\cdot\partial f/\partial\vec v = 0$.
- Velocity moments give the density $n$, mean velocity $\bar v_i$ and dispersion $\sigma_{ij}^2$. Integrating the CBE gives the continuity equation; multiplying by $v_j$ first gives the **Jeans equation**, an Euler equation in which $n\sigma_{ij}^2$ acts as an anisotropic pressure.
- For a steady spherical system, the radial Jeans equation with anisotropy $\beta = 1 - \sigma_t^2/\sigma_{rr}^2$ gives $M(<r) = -\frac{r\sigma_{rr}^2}{G}\left(\frac{d\ln n}{d\ln r} + \frac{d\ln\sigma_{rr}^2}{d\ln r} + 2\beta\right)$: a way to weigh galaxies from their stars' motions, limited by the uncertainty in $\beta$.
