8.902 opens its second part, "Cosmology and structure formation", by treating the entire universe as a single dynamical system. The 8.286 pages built this up slowly over many lectures; the 8.902 Lecture 10 notes cover the essentials in one go, and this page follows their first half: the cosmological principle, Hubble's law, a Newtonian derivation of the Friedmann equation and the four simple ways a universe can expand. The second half, the derivation from general relativity and the density parameters, is the next page.

## The cosmological principle

Everything rests on two assumptions about the universe on large scales:

- it is **homogeneous**: uniform, the same at every place;
- it is **isotropic**: the same in every direction, for every observer.

These symmetries are what make cosmology possible. They imply that the space-time metric has the same form everywhere, which simplifies the equations of general relativity enormously.

## Hubble's law from the cosmological principle

Observations show that galaxies recede with a speed proportional to their distance:

<Formula label="Hubble's law" tex="\vec v = H_0\,\vec r" cite="reading1" at="eq. (267), p. 62" />

with the **Hubble constant** today $H_0 \sim 70$ km/s/Mpc. The notes show that this is the only form an expansion can take if the cosmological principle holds.

**It must be linear.** Suppose galaxies move with $\vec v = \vec f(\vec r)$ as seen by observer A. Two galaxies at $\vec r_1$ and $\vec r_2$ have velocities $\vec f(\vec r_1)$ and $\vec f(\vec r_2)$, so their velocity difference is $\vec f(\vec r_1) - \vec f(\vec r_2)$. Now let observer B sit on galaxy 2. B sees galaxy 1 at $\vec r_1 - \vec r_2$ with velocity $\vec v_1 - \vec v_2$, and since B must see the **same** law as A,

$\vec f(\vec r_1 - \vec r_2) = \vec f(\vec r_1) - \vec f(\vec r_2)$.

A (continuous) function with this property is linear:

<Formula label="Linear expansion law" tex="\vec f(\vec r) = \mathbf{H}\,\vec r" cite="reading1" at="eq. (268), p. 62" />

with $\mathbf{H}$ a $3\times3$ matrix.

**It must be the same in every direction.** A general matrix would stretch some directions more than others, picking out special axes. Isotropy forbids that, so $\mathbf{H}$ must be $H_0$ times the identity matrix, and

<Formula label="Hubble's law derived" tex="\vec f(\vec r) = H_0\,\vec r" cite="reading1" at="eq. (269), p. 62" />

[Two small slips in the notes: they say $\mathbf{H}$ is assumed "non-diagonal", where they mean that it can have no off-diagonal parts and no unequal diagonal entries, i.e. it must be proportional to the identity. And they credit linearity to isotropy and uniqueness to homogeneity. The step "every observer sees the same law" is homogeneity; "no preferred direction" is isotropy.]

<WorkedExample title="Using Hubble's law">
<Problem>
With the notes' $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc, how fast does a galaxy 100 Mpc away recede? Seen from that galaxy, how fast do we recede?
</Problem>
<Step title="The speed">
$v = 70 \times 100 = 7000$ km/s.
</Step>
<Step title="The other way round">
By the same argument as above, the observer on that galaxy sees the same law, so we recede from them at 7000 km/s too. Nobody is at the centre.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The Friedmann equation from a Newtonian sphere

Hubble's law means the universe expands. General relativity gives the full dynamics, but the notes first take a shortcut through **Birkhoff's theorem**: inside a spherically symmetric distribution of matter, the matter outside a sphere exerts no net force on it. So a uniform sphere cut out of the universe expands exactly like the universe as a whole, and Newton's law is enough.

Take a sphere of radius $a$ (which grows with the universe) and density $\rho$. A particle on its surface feels only the mass inside:

<Formula label="Acceleration of the sphere's surface" tex="M(<a) = \frac43\pi a^3\rho \;\Rightarrow\; \ddot a = -\frac{GM(<a)}{a^2} = -\frac{4\pi G\rho}{a^2}\cdot\frac13 a^3" cite="reading1" at="eq. (270), p. 63" />

Multiply by $\dot a$ and use that the mass inside is constant, $\rho a^3 = \rho_0 a_0^3$:

<Formula label="Multiplying by the velocity" tex="\ddot a\,\dot a = -\frac{4\pi G\rho}{3}\,a\,\dot a = -\frac{4\pi G\rho_0}{3}\,a_0^3\,a^{-2}\,\dot a" cite="reading1" at="eq. (271), p. 63" />

Both sides are time derivatives: $\ddot a\dot a = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12\dot a^2\right)$ and $a^{-2}\dot a = \frac{d}{dt}\left(-\frac1a\right)$. So

<Formula label="An exact derivative" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12\dot a^2\right) = -\frac{4\pi G\rho_0 a_0^3}{3}\,\frac{d}{dt}\left(-\frac1a\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (272), p. 63" />

and integrating over time gives an energy equation, with an integration constant $\tilde\kappa$:

<Formula label="Energy equation" tex="\frac12\dot a^2 = \frac{4\pi G\rho_0 a_0^3}{3}\,\frac1a + \tilde\kappa = \frac{4\pi G}{3}\rho a^2 + \tilde\kappa" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (273)–(274), p. 63" />

The left side is the kinetic energy per unit mass of the surface, the first term on the right minus the potential energy, and $\tilde\kappa$ is the total energy per unit mass. Dividing by $a^2/2$:

<Formula label="Friedmann equation without Λ" tex="\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho + \frac{\tilde k}{a^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (275), p. 63" />

with $\tilde k = 2\tilde\kappa$ (the notes keep the tilde). This is the **Friedmann equation** without a cosmological constant. [The derivation used a fixed mass inside the sphere, so strictly it covers matter; that the same equation holds for radiation, with $\rho \propto a^{-4}$, follows from the general-relativity derivation on the next page.] Adding one gives

<Formula label="Friedmann equation with a cosmological constant" tex="\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho + \frac{\tilde k}{a^2} + \frac{\Lambda}{3}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (276), p. 63" />

Here $a$ is the **scale factor**: every length grows as $l = l_0\,a/a_0$, and the expansion rate is the **Hubble parameter** $H(t) \equiv \dot a/a = \dot r/r = v/r$. [The notes write $(\dot a/a)^2 = \dot r/r$; the square is a slip.] The three terms are:

- $\frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho$, from the density of matter or radiation;
- $\tilde k/a^2$, the **curvature** term (positive $\tilde\kappa$ means positive energy and an open universe in this sign convention; the general-relativity version on the next page writes it as $-kc^2/a^2$);
- $\Lambda/3$, the **cosmological constant**.

## How each ingredient dilutes

The terms change differently as the universe grows:

- **Matter**: the mass in a comoving volume is fixed and the volume grows as $a^3$, so $\rho_m \propto a^{-3}$.
- **Radiation**: the number of photons is diluted the same way, and in addition each photon's wavelength is stretched by $a$, so its energy falls as $1/a$: $\rho_r \propto a^{-4}$.
- **Dark energy** in the form of $\Lambda$ does not dilute at all.

The notes compare several model universes: the **ΛCDM** model, with roughly 30% matter, 70% dark energy, a tiny amount of radiation and no curvature, which describes our universe; the **de Sitter** model (flat, dark energy only); the **Einstein–de Sitter** model (flat, matter only); the **empty** universe (only curvature, open); and closed models that recollapse. ## Which term dominates when

In the Friedmann equation $H^2$ is a sum of terms scaling as $a^{-4}$, $a^{-3}$, $a^{-2}$ and $a^0$. As $a$ grows from zero, each in turn dominates:

- radiation, $\propto a^{-4}$: at very early times;
- matter, $\propto a^{-3}$: at early times;
- curvature, $\propto a^{-2}$: at intermediate times (if present);
- $\Lambda$, constant: at late times.

When one term dominates, the Friedmann equation is easy to solve:

<Formula label="Radiation regime" tex="\dot a^2 \propto a^{-2} \;\Rightarrow\; a\,da \propto dt \;\Rightarrow\; a \propto t^{1/2}, \quad H = \frac{1}{2t}, \quad t_0 = \frac12\,\frac{1}{H_0}" cite="reading1" at="p. 64" />

<Formula label="Matter regime" tex="\dot a^2 \propto a^{-1} \;\Rightarrow\; \sqrt a\,da \propto dt \;\Rightarrow\; a \propto t^{2/3}, \quad H = \frac{2}{3t}, \quad t_0 = \frac23\,\frac{1}{H_0}" cite="reading1" at="p. 65" />

<Formula label="Curvature regime" tex="\dot a^2 \propto \text{constant} \;\Rightarrow\; da \propto dt \;\Rightarrow\; a \propto t, \quad H = \frac1t, \quad t_0 = \frac{1}{H_0}" cite="reading1" at="p. 65" />

<Formula label="Λ regime" tex="\dot a^2 = \frac{\Lambda}{3}\,a^2 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{da}{a} = \sqrt{\frac{\Lambda}{3}}\,dt \;\Rightarrow\; a \propto e^{\sqrt{\Lambda/3}\,t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 65" />

The ages $t_0$ follow from $H = \dot a/a$ at the present time: for $a \propto t^n$, $H = n/t$ and so $t_0 = n/H_0$. In the $\Lambda$ regime the growth is exponential and there is no beginning. Our universe passed through these stages in turn: $a \propto t^{1/2}$, then $t^{2/3}$, now turning exponential.

<WorkedExample title="How old is each universe?">
<Problem>
Take $H_0 = 67.4$ km/s/Mpc, the Planck value quoted in the notes. In billions of years, $1/H_0 = 977.8/67.4$ (977.8 billion years is the Hubble time for a Hubble constant of one kilometre per second per megaparsec). Find the age of a universe that has always been dominated by radiation, by matter, or by curvature.
</Problem>
<Step title="The Hubble time">
$1/H_0 = 977.8/67.4 = 14.5$ billion years.
</Step>
<Step title="The three ages">
Radiation: $\frac12 \times 14.5 = 7.3$ billion years. Matter: $\frac23 \times 14.5 = 9.7$ billion years. Curvature: $14.5$ billion years.
</Step>
<Step title="Compared with our universe">
The real universe (radiation, then matter, then $\Lambda$) is about 13.8 billion years old. A matter-only universe would be younger than the oldest stars; the recent acceleration by $\Lambda$ is what makes the true age close to $1/H_0$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The **cosmological principle**: on large scales the universe is homogeneous and isotropic.
- Homogeneity makes the expansion law linear, $\vec v = \mathbf{H}\vec r$; isotropy makes $\mathbf{H}$ a multiple of the identity: **Hubble's law** $\vec v = H_0\vec r$.
- By **Birkhoff's theorem** a uniform sphere expands like the universe. Its energy equation is the **Friedmann equation**, $(\dot a/a)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho + \tilde k/a^2$, to which a cosmological constant adds $\Lambda/3$.
- Matter dilutes as $a^{-3}$, radiation as $a^{-4}$, curvature as $a^{-2}$, and $\Lambda$ not at all, so radiation, matter, curvature and $\Lambda$ dominate in turn.
- Single-term solutions: $a \propto t^{1/2}$ (radiation), $t^{2/3}$ (matter), $t$ (curvature), $e^{\sqrt{\Lambda/3}\,t}$ ($\Lambda$), with ages $t_0 = \frac12, \frac23, 1$ times $1/H_0$.
