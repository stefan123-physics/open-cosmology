The structure-formation pages took cold dark matter as given. This page, following the Lecture 19 notes of 8.902, asks where it came from. The answer uses the same idea as 8.286's account of the early universe: since $T \propto 1 + z$, the universe was once hot enough to **make** particles of almost any mass. For a particle of mass $m_x$, when $kT \gtrsim m_xc^2$ it is continually created and annihilated in collisions. As the temperature falls the reactions stop (the particles **freeze out**) and their abundance is fixed from then on. (A handy conversion from the notes: $1\ \text{eV}/k_B = 1.16 \times 10^4$ K, so 1 eV corresponds to about $10^4$ K; p. 105.)

The notes first treat the freeze-out of dark matter, which for the leading candidates happens at temperatures of roughly 10–100 GeV, and then sketch the rest of the thermal history down to today.

## Equilibrium: as many made as destroyed

At early enough times (the notes give $kT \approx 100$ GeV, which is about $10^{15}$ K), $kT \ge m_xc^2$ for the leading dark matter candidates. [The notes print "$T > 10^{12}$ K $\cong$ 100 GeV"; $10^{12}$ K is only about 86 MeV, and 100 GeV corresponds to $1.2\times10^{15}$ K.] Once the particles are non-relativistic but still in **thermal equilibrium**, their number density is set by the temperature alone:

<Formula label="Equilibrium number density (non-relativistic)" tex="n_\text{eq} = g\left(\frac{mkT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}e^{-mc^2/kT}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (466), p. 106" />

where $g$ counts the internal states (spin). Equilibrium means creation and annihilation balance, for example through

<Formula label="Creation and annihilation" tex="x + \bar x \;\rightleftharpoons\; 2\gamma" cite="reading1" at="eq. (467), p. 106" />

The annihilation rate per unit volume is $n^2\langle\sigma v\rangle$, with $\langle\sigma v\rangle$ the annihilation cross section times relative speed, averaged over the thermal velocities. In equilibrium the creation rate $\psi$ matches it:

<Formula label="Balance of creation and annihilation" tex="\psi = n_\text{eq}^2\,\langle\sigma v\rangle" cite="reading1" at="eq. (468), p. 106" />

[The notes print only the right-hand side, "$= n_\text{eq}^2\langle\sigma v\rangle$".]

If equilibrium lasted for ever, the Boltzmann factor $e^{-mc^2/kT}$ would drive the number to zero once $kT \ll mc^2$: no particles left, **no relic abundance**. What saves them is that the annihilation rate $n^2\langle\sigma v\rangle$ also falls as the universe expands and dilutes them.

## The race between annihilation and expansion

Without any reactions, the particles are simply diluted, $n \propto a^{-3}$, so the **comoving** density $n_c$ stays constant:

<Formula label="Dilution without reactions" tex="\frac{dn_c}{dt} = 0 \quad \left(n_c = n\left(\frac{a}{a_0}\right)^{3}\right), \qquad \frac{dn}{dt} = -3\frac{\dot a}{a}\,n \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{dn}{dt} + 3Hn = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (469), p. 106" />

[The notes print the exponent as $-3$; with $n \propto a^{-3}$, the comoving density is $n(a/a_0)^{+3}$.] With reactions, annihilations remove particles and creation replaces them, pushing $n_c$ towards its equilibrium value:

<Formula label="Abundance equation with reactions" tex="\frac{dn_c}{dt} = -\langle\sigma v\rangle\left(n_c^2 - n_{c,\text{eq}}^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (470), p. 106" />

[As written, this suppresses a factor $(a_0/a)^3$ on the right, which turns comoving densities back into physical ones in the collision rate. It is this factor that makes the $n_{c,\text{eq}}$ in the prefactor of eq. (471) the physical $n_\text{eq}$ used in $\tau_\text{coll}$ below, so read it as included.] Changing the variable from $t$ to $a$ with $\dot a = aH$:

<Formula label="Rewritten in terms of the scale factor" tex="\frac{a}{n_{c,\text{eq}}}\frac{dn_c}{da} = -\frac{\langle\sigma v\rangle\,n_{c,\text{eq}}}{H}\left[\left(\frac{n_c}{n_{c,\text{eq}}}\right)^2 - 1\right]" cite="reading1" at="eq. (471), p. 106" />

The prefactor is a ratio of two time scales: the expansion time $\tau_H = 1/H$ and the mean time between annihilations, $\tau_\text{coll} = 1/(n_\text{eq}\langle\sigma v\rangle)$:

<Formula label="Freeze-out equation" tex="\frac{a}{n_{c,\text{eq}}}\frac{dn_c}{da} = -\frac{\tau_H}{\tau_\text{coll}}\left[\left(\frac{n_c}{n_{c,\text{eq}}}\right)^2 - 1\right]" cite="reading1" at="eq. (472), p. 107" />

This gives two regimes:

- **Early times**, $\tau_\text{coll} \ll \tau_H$: the huge prefactor forces the bracket to zero, so $n_c \approx n_{c,\text{eq}}$. Reactions are fast; the particles track equilibrium.
- **Late times**, $\tau_\text{coll} \gg \tau_H$: the right-hand side vanishes, so $n_c \approx$ constant $\approx n_{c,\text{eq}}(z_\text{freeze})$. Particles no longer find each other.

The switch happens at the redshift $z_\text{freeze}$ where $\tau_\text{coll} \sim \tau_H$. From then on the comoving number is frozen: that is the relic abundance.

## The WIMP miracle

Running the argument backwards: the **observed** amount of dark matter fixes $\langle\sigma v\rangle$ (the relic abundance depends on the mass only weakly, through $x_f$). The notes point out that the mass and cross section this suggests land at the **electroweak scale** (a particle of order 100 GeV with a weak-interaction-sized cross section, freezing out at a temperature some 20–30 times below its mass), exactly what particle physicists had proposed for other reasons as **WIMPs** (weakly interacting massive particles). This coincidence is called the **WIMP miracle**. The notes add a sober caveat: so far, nothing has been detected.

<Callout kind="note" title="Beyond the notes">
Direct-detection experiments deep underground (such as LZ and XENONnT), searches at the Large Hadron Collider and searches for annihilation products from space have kept tightening the limits on WIMPs without a detection, and other candidates (axions, sterile neutrinos, and more) are actively searched for as well. Cold dark matter's gravitational effects remain firmly established; its particle identity is still unknown.
</Callout>

## Hot and cold dark matter

Were the particles still moving at nearly the speed of light when they froze out? A particle becomes **non-relativistic** roughly when

<Formula label="Becoming non-relativistic" tex="3kT(t_\text{nr}) \approx mc^2" cite="reading1" at="eq. (473), p. 107" />

which splits relics into two kinds:

- $t_\text{nr} > t_\text{freeze}$ (still relativistic at freeze-out): a **hot relic**, **hot dark matter** (light neutrinos are the classic example).
- $t_\text{nr} < t_\text{freeze}$ (already slow at freeze-out): a **cold relic**, **cold dark matter** (WIMPs, which freeze out at $x_f \approx 20$–$30$, are cold).

### Hot dark matter: free-streaming

Fast particles don't stay inside small overdensities: they stream straight out of them. By analogy with the Jeans length, with the particle speed $v$ in place of the sound speed, the scale below which this smooths out perturbations is the **free-streaming length**

<Formula label="Free-streaming length" tex="\lambda = v\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{G\rho}}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (474), p. 107" />

While $v \approx c$, every scale smaller than the horizon is wiped out. The particles do slow down, because their peculiar momentum redshifts with the expansion:

<Formula label="Peculiar velocity decays" tex="v_\text{pec} = v - Hd \;\Longrightarrow\; v \propto a^{-1}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (475), p. 108" />

($d$ the distance), and free-streaming effectively ends once they become non-relativistic, $mc^2 \sim 3kT$. The scale erased is roughly the (comoving) horizon at that time:

<Formula label="Time and scale of the non-relativistic transition" tex="t \sim 2\times10^{12}\,\text{s}\left(\frac{mc^2}{2\ \text{eV}}\right)^{-2}, \qquad l_h \sim c\,t_h\,(1 + z_h) \sim 60\ \text{Mpc}\left(\frac{mc^2}{3\ \text{eV}}\right)^{-1}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (476), p. 108" />

[The notes print the normalising masses as 2 keV and 3 keV and write $l_h \sim ct_h$. Recomputing in the radiation era ($t \propto T^{-2} \propto m^{-2}$ and comoving horizon $\propto t(1 + z) \propto m^{-1}$), the quoted numbers fit masses in **eV** and the comoving length $ct_h(1 + z_h)$: a 2 eV particle turns non-relativistic at $t \approx 3\times10^{12}$ s, and for 3 eV, $ct(1 + z) \approx 55$ Mpc. For keV masses the times are a million times shorter and the lengths a thousand times smaller. The worked example below uses the full comoving horizon $2ct(1 + z)$, twice the notes' $ct(1+z)$; at this level of estimate the factor 2 doesn't matter.]

So hot dark matter **erases all structure below $l_h$**. For eV-mass particles that is around a hundred megaparsecs, the scale of superclusters.

### Cold dark matter: bottom-up

Cold dark matter is already slow at freeze-out, so its free-streaming scale is very small and perturbations survive on almost all scales. In the power spectrum, hot dark matter cuts off $P(k)$ sharply beyond the free-streaming wavenumber, while cold dark matter keeps the gentle $k^{-3}$ fall-off of the transfer function.

The two give opposite orders of events:

- With **hot** dark matter only very large structures can collapse first; they would then have to **fragment** into galaxies: **top-down** formation.
- With **cold** dark matter small halos form first and **merge** into larger ones, which keep the smaller ones inside as subhalos: **bottom-up** (hierarchical) formation, as on the Press–Schechter page.

Observations not discussed in these notes, such as the clustering of galaxies, the Lyman-α forest (8.902 Lecture 21) and the ages of galaxies, favour bottom-up, which is why the standard model uses **cold** dark matter.

## A timeline of the hot universe

The notes close the section with a brief history in temperature and time; here it is in summary, from the earliest moments to today:

- **$10^{19}$ GeV, $10^{-43}$ s**, the Planck era, where quantum gravity would be needed. [$10^{-43}$ s is the Planck time itself; the radiation-era relation, counting $g_* \approx 100$ particle species at such temperatures, would put $10^{19}$ GeV at about $10^{-45}$ s. At this point both are only indicative.]
- **$10^{16}$ GeV, $10^{-38}$ s**, a possible grand-unified (GUT) phase transition; before it, strong and electroweak forces would be indistinguishable (8.286's monopole and inflation lectures).
- **$10^{12}$ GeV, $10^{-30}$ s**, a Peccei–Quinn transition, if that mechanism solves the strong CP problem (it would also produce axions, another dark matter candidate).
- **10s–100s GeV, $10^{-8}$ s**, WIMPs freeze out. [With $g_* \approx 100$ species at these temperatures (the worked example's 3.36 applies only after electron–positron annihilation, below about 0.1 MeV), 10–100 GeV correspond to about $10^{-9}$–$10^{-11}$ s; $10^{-8}$ s fits $kT \approx 4$ GeV, where a 100 GeV WIMP actually freezes out.]
- **100–300 MeV, $10^{-5}$ s**, quark–hadron transition: quarks and gluons are bound into protons and neutrons.
- **10 MeV–0.1 MeV, seconds to minutes**, Big Bang nucleosynthesis: deuterium, helium-3, helium-4 and lithium-7 form (the next lecture).
- **~keV, ~1 day**, photon-number-changing processes stop; from then on the number of photons is conserved, preserving the blackbody spectrum. [By the radiation-era relation of the worked example, 1 keV is reached after about two weeks; one day corresponds to $kT \approx 4$ keV.]
- **~3 eV, $10^4$–$10^5$ yr**, matter–radiation equality; before it radiation dominates the energy density. [With today's parameters equality is at $1 + z \approx 3400$, i.e. $T \approx 9000$ K, $kT \approx 0.8$ eV, some $5$–$7\times10^4$ years; 8.286's matter–radiation equality page estimates 68 000–75 000 years and quotes 47 000 years from a fuller calculation.]
- **~eV, ~400 000 yr**, electrons and protons combine into hydrogen; the CMB is released. [More precisely $kT \approx 0.26$ eV ($T \approx 3000$ K) at about 380 000 years; recombination happens well below 1 eV because photons vastly outnumber baryons.]
- **$10^{-3}$ eV, $10^9$ yr**, first stars and galaxies.
- **$10^{-4}$ eV, $10^{10}$ yr**, today ($T = 2.7$ K, $kT = 2.3\times10^{-4}$ eV).

## Summary

- In the hot early universe particles with $mc^2 \lesssim kT$ are created and annihilated in equilibrium, with $n_\text{eq} = g(mkT/2\pi\hbar^2)^{3/2}e^{-mc^2/kT}$ once non-relativistic.
- Annihilation competes with expansion: the ratio $\tau_H/\tau_\text{coll}$ decides. When $\tau_\text{coll} \sim \tau_H$ the particles **freeze out** and their comoving density is fixed: the relic abundance, lower for larger $\langle\sigma v\rangle$.
- The observed dark matter density points to weak-scale masses and cross sections: the **WIMP miracle**. No WIMP has been detected.
- **Hot** relics (still relativistic at freeze-out) free-stream and erase structure below $l_h \propto m^{-1}$ (~100 Mpc for eV masses), giving top-down formation; **cold** relics allow bottom-up, hierarchical growth, which observations support.
