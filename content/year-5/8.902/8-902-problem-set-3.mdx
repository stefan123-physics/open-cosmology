## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Oort constants" points={70} mit="Problem 1">

Take the Milky Way to be infinitely thin, and every star in it to move on a circular orbit about the centre, with the speed $v_c(R)$ of the rotation curve at its radius $R$. The Sun moves on such an orbit at radius $R_0$. A nearby star lies at distance $d$ from the Sun, with $d \ll R_0$, in the direction of galactic longitude $l$: the angle in the plane of the disc from the direction of the Galactic centre ($l = 0$) towards the direction in which the Sun moves ($l = 90°$).

<Part label="a" points={20}>

Show that the velocity of the star relative to the Sun has a component along the line of sight (positive away from the Sun) and a component across it (positive towards larger $l$) given by

<Formula label="Velocities of nearby stars" tex="v_r = A\,d\sin 2l, \qquad v_T = d\left(A\cos 2l + B\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(A), p. 1" />

with the Oort constants

<Formula label="Oort constants" tex="A = \frac12\left(\frac{v_c}{R} - \frac{dv_c}{dR}\right)_{R_0}, \qquad B = -\frac12\left(\frac{v_c}{R} + \frac{dv_c}{dR}\right)_{R_0}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(A), p. 1" />

The derivation has no box: mark it yourself after opening the solution. Then, as a first use of the result (our addition), find $A$ for a flat rotation curve, $v_c(R) = v_0$ at every radius, in terms of $v_0$ and $R_0$.

<Hint>

Put the Sun at the origin, with one axis towards the Galactic centre and the other along the Sun's motion. Write down the position of the star, then the velocity of a point on a circular orbit through that position, and subtract the Sun's velocity.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The exact answers are $v_r = (\Omega - \Omega_0)R_0\sin l$ and $v_T = (\Omega - \Omega_0)R_0\cos l - \Omega d$, with $\Omega = \Omega(R)$ at the star and $\Omega_0$ at the Sun. Now expand $\Omega(R)$ to first order about $R_0$: how does $R - R_0$ depend on $d$ and $l$ when $d$ is small?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1a-flat" label="For a flat rotation curve, $A =$" expr="v0/(2*R0)"
  vars={{ v0: [150, 300], R0: [5, 12] }}
  names={{ v0: "v_0", R0: "R_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "v0/R0", hint: "That is the angular speed of the Sun, v₀/R₀. For a flat curve the slope term is zero, but the factor in front of the bracket still counts." },
    { expr: "-v0/(2*R0)", hint: "That is B for a flat curve. Look again at the sign in front of A's bracket." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In a frame fixed to the Sun, the star's velocity minus the Sun's velocity is a small difference of two large circular velocities. Work it out exactly first, then keep only the terms of first order in $d/R_0$.

<Step title="A frame on the Sun (our choice)">

Put the Sun at the origin. The $x$ axis points to the Galactic centre, which sits at $(R_0, 0)$; the $y$ axis points along the Sun's motion, towards $l = 90°$. The star is at $(d\cos l,\ d\sin l)$, and the unit vectors along and across the line of sight are $\hat n = (\cos l, \sin l)$ and $\hat t = (-\sin l, \cos l)$, the second pointing towards larger $l$.

</Step>

<Step title="Circular velocities">

Seen from the centre, a point at $(x, y)$ has position $(x - R_0,\ y)$. Its circular velocity is perpendicular to that, with size $\Omega R$, and turned in the sense that gives the Sun the velocity $(0,\ \Omega_0R_0)$:

<Formula label="Velocity of a point on a circular orbit" tex="\vec V(x, y) = \Omega(R)\,\big(y,\ R_0 - x\big), \qquad R^2 = (x - R_0)^2 + y^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(A), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

At the star this is $\Omega\,(d\sin l,\ R_0 - d\cos l)$, and the Sun has $\Omega_0\,(0,\ R_0)$.

</Step>

<Step title="The exact components">

Take the difference and project it on $\hat n$ and on $\hat t$. The terms $\Omega d\sin l\cos l$ cancel in the first, and $\sin^2 l + \cos^2 l = 1$ in the second:

<Formula label="Relative velocity, exact" tex="v_r = \left(\Omega - \Omega_0\right)R_0\sin l, \qquad v_T = \left(\Omega - \Omega_0\right)R_0\cos l - \Omega\,d" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(A), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Nothing has been approximated yet.

</Step>

<Step title="First order in d">

From $R^2 = R_0^2 - 2R_0d\cos l + d^2$, to first order $R - R_0 \approx -d\cos l$, so $\Omega - \Omega_0 \approx -(d\Omega/dR)_{R_0}\,d\cos l$. In the last term of $v_T$, $\Omega d \approx \Omega_0 d$. With $2\sin l\cos l = \sin 2l$ and $2\cos^2 l = 1 + \cos 2l$:

<Formula label="Relative velocity, first order" tex="v_r = -\frac12R_0\frac{d\Omega}{dR}\,d\sin 2l, \qquad v_T = d\left[-\frac12R_0\frac{d\Omega}{dR}\left(1 + \cos 2l\right) - \Omega_0\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(A), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

So $v_r = A\,d\sin 2l$ and $v_T = d(A\cos 2l + B)$ with

<Formula label="Oort constants in terms of the angular speed" tex="A = -\frac12R\frac{d\Omega}{dR}, \qquad B = -\left(\Omega + \frac12R\frac{d\Omega}{dR}\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (156), p. 36" />

taken at $R_0$, so that $B = A - \Omega_0$. (As printed, the notes' $A$ lacks the factor $R$ that their $B$ has; the form here is the one the sheet's expressions need.) The notes call $d(A\cos 2l + B)$ the proper motion; it is the velocity across the line of sight, and the proper motion, an angle per unit time, is that divided by $d$.

</Step>

<Step title="In terms of the rotation curve">

With $\Omega = v_c/R$, $d\Omega/dR = (1/R)\,dv_c/dR - v_c/R^2$. Then $A = \frac12(v_c/R - dv_c/dR)$ at $R_0$, and $B = A - v_c/R = -\frac12(v_c/R + dv_c/dR)$ at $R_0$: the sheet's two expressions.

</Step>

<Step title="A flat rotation curve">

If $v_c = v_0$ everywhere, $dv_c/dR = 0$, so $A = v_0/(2R_0)$ and $B = -v_0/(2R_0)$: then $A = -B$, and $A - B = v_0/R_0$ is the Sun's angular speed.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Units: $A$ and $B$ are speeds per length, as $v_r/d$ must be. In a disc that turns as a solid body, $v_c \propto R$, every star keeps its distance from the Sun and the pair simply turns with the disc. Indeed $dv_c/dR = v_c/R$ gives $A = 0$ and $B = -\Omega_0$, so $v_r = 0$ and $v_T = -\Omega_0d$: no motion along the line of sight, and across it only the turning of the disc as a whole, seen against axes that do not turn. We also checked the exact components against the first-order ones numerically for a rotation curve $v_c \propto R^{0.3}$ at $d = 0.01$ kpc (our check).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

The sheet gives the values of the Oort constants that it says were found with the Hipparcos satellite:

<Formula label="Measured Oort constants (the sheet's values)" tex="A = 14.82 \pm 0.84\ \text{km/s/kpc}, \qquad B = -12.37 \pm 0.64\ \text{km/s/kpc}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

and asks you to take $R_0 = 8.5$ kpc. For each part below, find an expression in terms of $A$, $B$ and $R_0$, then its value at the Sun with the best-fit values above (the errors need not be carried along). For times, use 1 kpc/(km/s) = 977.8 million years (with 1 kpc = 3.0857 × 10¹⁶ km and a year of 365.25 days; our choice).

<Part label="b" points={15}>

Find the Sun's circular speed $v_0$ and its orbital period $P$ around the Galactic centre.

<Hint>

The two expressions of part (a) contain $v_c/R$ and $dv_c/dR$. Which combination of $A$ and $B$ removes the slope?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1b-v0" label="$v_0 =$" expr="R0*(A - B)"
  vars={{ A: [10, 20], B: [-20, -5], R0: [5, 12] }}
  names={{ A: "A", B: "B", R0: "R_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "R0*(A + B)", hint: "In A + B the circular-speed terms cancel and the slope is left. Which combination removes the slope instead?" },
    { expr: "(A - B)/R0", hint: "A − B is a speed per length. To get a speed, multiply by the radius rather than divide." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1b-v0-num" label="$v_0$ at the Sun (km/s):" value="231.1" unit="km/s" tolerance="2" calc="p1b-v0-num" />

<Answer id="p1b-period" label="$P =$" expr="2*pi/(A - B)"
  vars={{ A: [10, 20], B: [-20, -5], R0: [5, 12] }}
  names={{ A: "A", B: "B", R0: "R_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*pi*R0/(A - B)", hint: "Check the units: a length divided by a speed per length is a length squared over a speed, not a time." },
    { expr: "2*pi/sqrt(-4*B*(A - B))", hint: "That is the period of the radial oscillation, 2π/κ. The orbit is set by the angular speed instead." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1b-period-num" label="$P$ at the Sun (millions of years):" value="226" unit="Myr" tolerance="2" calc="p1b-period-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $A - B$ keeps the circular-speed terms and cancels the slope.

<Step title="The angular speed and the speed">

From part (a), $A - B = \frac12(v_c/R - dv_c/dR) + \frac12(v_c/R + dv_c/dR) = v_0/R_0 = \Omega_0$. So

<Formula label="Speed and period of the Sun" tex="v_0 = R_0\left(A - B\right), \qquad P = \frac{2\pi R_0}{v_0} = \frac{2\pi}{A - B}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(B), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

$A - B = 14.82 + 12.37 = 27.19$ km/s/kpc, so $v_0 = 8.5 \times 27.19 = 231.1$ km/s. The period is $2\pi/27.19 = 0.2311$ kpc/(km/s), which is $0.2311 \times 977.8 = 226$ million years.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Units: km/s/kpc times kpc is km/s. The Sun covers $2\pi \times 8.5 = 53.4$ kpc in one orbit; at 231 km/s, which is 0.236 kpc per million years, that takes $53.4/0.236 = 226$ million years.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={10}>

Find the slope of the rotation curve at the Sun, $(dv_c/dR)_{R_0}$. Then say, in the last box, what it tells about the rotation curve near the Sun (our addition).

<Hint>

Now you want the combination of $A$ and $B$ in which the circular-speed terms cancel.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1c-slope" label="$(dv_c/dR)_{R_0} =$" expr="-(A + B)"
  vars={{ A: [10, 20], B: [-20, -5] }}
  names={{ A: "A", B: "B" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "A + B", hint: "Check the sign: in both A and B the slope enters with a minus sign." },
    { expr: "-(A - B)", hint: "That is minus the angular speed, −v₀/R₀. The slope is in the other combination." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1c-slope-num" label="$(dv_c/dR)_{R_0}$ (km/s/kpc):" value="-2.45" unit="km/s/kpc" tolerance="0.03" calc="p1c-slope-num" />

<Answer id="p1c-means" label="Near the Sun, the rotation curve is:" choices={["Rising like a solid body, with the speed growing in step with the radius", "Close to flat, falling only a little with radius compared with v₀/R₀", "Falling as round a point mass, so most of the mass lies inside the Sun's orbit", "Falling so steeply that stars just outside the Sun's orbit move backwards"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $A + B$ cancels the circular-speed terms and leaves the slope.

<Step title="The slope">

<Formula label="Slope of the rotation curve" tex="A + B = -\left(\frac{dv_c}{dR}\right)_{R_0} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \left(\frac{dv_c}{dR}\right)_{R_0} = -\left(A + B\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(C), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

With the sheet's values, $-(14.82 - 12.37) = -2.45$ km/s/kpc.

</Step>

<Step title="What it means">

Compare it with $v_0/R_0 = 27.19$ km/s/kpc: the slope is less than a tenth of that, and negative. Over 1 kpc the speed drops by only 2.45 km/s out of 231. A solid body would have a slope of $+27.19$ km/s/kpc, and a point mass, with $v_c \propto R^{-1/2}$, one of $-v_0/(2R_0) = -13.6$ km/s/kpc. The measured curve near the Sun is close to flat, falling gently.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Within one standard error of the sheet's values, $A + B$ lies between about $2.45 - 1.06$ and $2.45 + 1.06$ km/s/kpc (the two errors added in quadrature, $\sqrt{0.84^2 + 0.64^2} = 1.06$, if they are independent). So the curve could be a little flatter or a little steeper, but nowhere near the point mass.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={15}>

Find the epicycle frequency $\kappa$ at the Sun (the sheet writes it $k$).

<Hint>

Start from the notes' $\kappa^2 = R\,d\Omega^2/dR + 4\Omega^2$, and use the forms of $A$ and $B$ in terms of $\Omega$ and $d\Omega/dR$ from the solution of part (a).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1d-kappa" label="$\kappa =$" expr="sqrt(-4*B*(A - B))"
  vars={{ A: [10, 20], B: [-20, -5] }}
  names={{ A: "A", B: "B" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(2)*(A - B)", hint: "That is κ = √2 Ω, true only for an exactly flat rotation curve. Use A and B as measured." },
    { expr: "A - B", hint: "That is the angular speed Ω₀ itself, which equals κ only round a point mass." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1d-kappa-num" label="$\kappa$ at the Sun (km/s/kpc):" value="36.68" unit="km/s/kpc" tolerance="0.4" calc="p1d-kappa-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write $\kappa^2$ with $R\,d\Omega/dR$ and $\Omega$, and replace both by Oort constants.

<Step title="From the rotation curve to A and B">

$R\,d\Omega^2/dR = 2\Omega R\,d\Omega/dR$, and from part (a) $R_0\,d\Omega/dR = -2A$ and $\Omega_0 = A - B$. So $\kappa^2 = -4A\Omega_0 + 4\Omega_0^2 = 4\Omega_0(\Omega_0 - A)$, and $\Omega_0 - A = -B$:

<Formula label="Epicycle frequency from the Oort constants" tex="\kappa^2 = -4B\left(A - B\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (157), p. 36" />

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

$-4 \times (-12.37) \times 27.19 = 1345.4$, so $\kappa = \sqrt{1345.4} = 36.68$ km/s/kpc. One radial oscillation takes $2\pi/\kappa = 0.1713$ kpc/(km/s), or 167.5 million years.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

For a flat curve $A = -B$ and $\kappa^2 = 8A^2 = 2\Omega_0^2$, the notes' $\kappa = \sqrt2\,\Omega$; round a point mass $\kappa = \Omega$. Our $\kappa/\Omega_0$ should therefore lie between 1 and 1.41, a little below 1.41 since the curve falls gently: part (e) finds it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={10}>

How many radial (epicyclic) oscillations does the Sun make during one orbit of its guiding centre about the Galactic centre? Give the expression and its value. Then say what this means for the shape of the Sun's path (our addition).

<Hint>

Count oscillations per orbit as the ratio of two frequencies, or of two periods the other way round.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1e-ratio" label="Oscillations per orbit $=$" expr="sqrt(-4*B/(A - B))"
  vars={{ A: [10, 20], B: [-20, -5] }}
  names={{ A: "A", B: "B" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt((A - B)/(-4*B))", hint: "That is the number of orbits per oscillation: the ratio is upside down." },
    { expr: "-4*B/(A - B)", hint: "That is κ²/Ω₀². Take the square root." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1e-ratio-num" label="Number at the Sun:" value="1.349" tolerance="0.015" calc="p1e-ratio-num" />

<Answer id="p1e-shape" label="Seen from the Galactic centre, the Sun's path:" choices={["Closes on itself after every orbit, as an ellipse round a point mass would", "Closes after exactly four orbits, since 1.35 is close to the fraction 4/3", "Does not close: its innermost point moves on from orbit to orbit, a rosette", "Is an exact circle, since the epicycles are only a small effect of order d/R₀"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In one orbital period $2\pi/\Omega_0$ the radial oscillation, with period $2\pi/\kappa$, runs $\kappa/\Omega_0$ times.

<Step title="The ratio">

<Formula label="Epicycles per orbit" tex="\frac{\kappa}{\Omega_0} = \frac{\sqrt{-4B\left(A - B\right)}}{A - B} = \sqrt{\frac{-4B}{A - B}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(E), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Numerically $36.68/27.19 = 1.349$: also 226/167.5 from the two periods.

</Step>

<Step title="What it means">

The ratio is not a whole number, so after one orbit the Sun is at a different phase of its radial oscillation: the point of smallest radius advances each time and the path is a rosette that does not close. (A ratio of exactly 4/3 would close after three orbits, not four; 1.349 is not that ratio.) Round a point mass the ratio would be exactly 1 and the ellipse would close.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

1.349 lies between 1 (point mass) and 1.41 (flat curve), and close to 1.41, as part (c) led us to expect. The [lesson's worked example](/courses/8-902/orbits-and-epicycles), with later Gaia values of $A$ and $B$ (2017), finds 1.32: the same picture.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Free-fall time" points={30} mit="Problem 2">

A uniform sphere of fluid with density $\rho$ and no pressure is released from rest. Nothing holds it up, so it falls in on itself under its own gravity. The notes estimate the time this takes as $1/\sqrt{G\rho}$ (Lecture 7, in the meaning of the Jeans length):

<Formula label="Free-fall time, estimate" tex="t_\text{ff} \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{G\rho}}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (204), p. 45" />

Here you find the exact time.

<Part label="a" points={15}>

Follow one thin shell of the sphere that starts at radius $r_0$, with mass $M$ inside it. How long does the shell take to reach the centre? Give the time in terms of $r_0$, $G$ and $M$.

<Hint>

Before the shell reaches the centre, no shell from inside overtakes it (part (b) shows why), so the mass inside it stays $M$. Its motion is that of a particle falling from rest towards a point mass $M$. Use energy conservation to find $dr/dt$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The time is $\int_0^{r_0} dr\,/\,|\dot r|$. Substitute $r = r_0\cos^2\theta$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2a-time" label="Time to reach the centre $=$" expr="(pi/2)*sqrt(r0^3/(2*G*M))"
  vars={{ r0: [0.5, 5], G: [0.5, 2], M: [0.5, 5] }}
  names={{ r0: "r_0", G: "G", M: "M" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(r0^3/(G*M))", hint: "That is the estimate from dimensions alone. The question asks for the exact factor in front." },
    { expr: "pi*sqrt(r0^3/(2*G*M))", hint: "That is twice the answer: the time to fall in and climb back out to r₀. Stop at the centre." },
    { expr: "(pi/2)*sqrt(r0^3/(G*M))", hint: "Look again at the energy: ṙ² = 2GM(1/r − 1/r₀) has a factor 2 that ends up inside the root." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The shell falls like a single particle towards a point mass $M$, from rest. Energy conservation gives its speed at each radius; adding up $dr/|\dot r|$ gives the time.

<Step title="The equation of motion">

By Newton's shell theorem only the mass inside pulls on the shell, and it pulls as if it sat at the centre:

<Formula label="Motion of the shell" tex="\ddot r = -\frac{GM}{r^2}, \qquad \frac12\dot r^2 - \frac{GM}{r} = -\frac{GM}{r_0}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The second equation is the first multiplied by $\dot r$ and integrated, with $\dot r = 0$ at $r = r_0$. So $\dot r^2 = 2GM(1/r - 1/r_0)$.

</Step>

<Step title="The substitution">

Put $r = r_0\cos^2\theta$, so $\theta$ runs from 0 at the start to $\pi/2$ at the centre. Then $1/r - 1/r_0 = \tan^2\theta/r_0$ and $dr = -2r_0\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta$, and

<Formula label="Collapse in parametric form" tex="r = r_0\cos^2\theta, \qquad t = \sqrt{\frac{r_0^3}{2GM}}\left(\theta + \frac12\sin 2\theta\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The time">

At $\theta = \pi/2$ the shell is at the centre, and $\sin\pi = 0$:

<Formula label="Time for a shell to reach the centre" tex="t = \frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{\frac{r_0^3}{2GM}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Another route: a particle falling from rest is on a Kepler ellipse squeezed flat, with semi-major axis $r_0/2$. Its full period is $2\pi\sqrt{(r_0/2)^3/(GM)}$, and the fall is half of it: $\pi\sqrt{r_0^3/(8GM)}$, the same result. We also checked the integral numerically (our check).

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="b" points={10}>

Prove that the whole sphere collapses to a point at the time

<Formula label="Free-fall time" tex="t_f = \sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{32\,G\rho}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1" />

called the free-fall time of a system of density $\rho$. The proof has no box: mark it yourself after opening the solution. Then (our addition) give the number $t_f\sqrt{G\rho}$, and say in the last box why every shell arrives at the centre at the same moment.

<Hint>

Write $M$ in terms of $\rho$ and $r_0$, and put it into part (a). What is left of $r_0$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2b-factor" label="$t_f\sqrt{G\rho} =$" value="0.5427" tolerance="0.005" calc="p2b-factor" />

<Answer id="p2b-why" label="All shells reach the centre together because:" choices={["The time for each shell depends only on the density inside it, which is the same for every shell", "The outer shells start further out but feel a stronger pull, which grows as their radius squared", "Pressure inside the sphere pushes the inner shells back until the outer ones have caught up", "Gravity inside a uniform sphere is zero, so every shell moves at the same constant speed"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The time of part (a) depends on $r_0$ and $M$ only through $M/r_0^3$, which for a uniform sphere is fixed by its density.

<Step title="The free-fall time">

The mass inside radius $r_0$ is $M = \frac43\pi r_0^3\rho$. Then $r_0^3/(2GM) = 3/(8\pi G\rho)$, and part (a) gives

<Formula label="Free-fall time from the shell's motion" tex="t_f = \frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{\frac{3}{8\pi G\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{\pi^2}{4}\cdot\frac{3}{8\pi G\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{32\,G\rho}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The radius $r_0$ has dropped out.

</Step>

<Step title="Why the sphere stays uniform">

Every shell takes the same time, whatever its starting radius. More than that: by the parametric solution of part (a), at any moment every shell has shrunk by the same factor $\cos^2\theta$, since $\theta$ depends on $t$ and $M/r_0^3$ only. So the shells never cross, the mass inside each one stays its own $M$ (the assumption of part (a)), and the sphere stays uniform while its density grows. All of it arrives at the centre at $t_f$: the sphere collapses to a point.

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

$t_f\sqrt{G\rho} = \sqrt{3\pi/32} = 0.5427$: the exact time is about half of the notes' estimate $1/\sqrt{G\rho}$, which is fine for an estimate "of order".

</Step>

  curves={[
    { x: "(s + sin(2*s)/2)/(pi/2)", y: "cos(s)^2", range: [0, 1.5708], label: "exact" },

<Step title="Check">

Units: $G\rho$ has units of 1/s², so $t_f$ is a time. Denser things collapse faster, as $\rho^{-1/2}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

(Our addition.) The cold molecular cloud of the [Lecture 7 lesson](/courses/8-902/stability-jeans-toomre) has about $10^3$ molecules per cubic centimetre, a density of $\rho = 3.9\times10^{-18}$ kg/m³ (our rounding). How long would it take to collapse if nothing held it up? Use $G = 6.674\times10^{-11}$ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻² and a year of $3.156\times10^7$ s.

<Hint>

Put the numbers into $t_f$ in SI units, then turn seconds into millions of years.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2c-cloud" label="$t_f$ for the cloud (millions of years):" value="1.066" unit="Myr" tolerance="0.015" calc="p2c-cloud" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A direct use of the formula of part (b).

<Step title="The number">

$32G\rho = 32 \times 6.674\times10^{-11} \times 3.9\times10^{-18} = 8.33\times10^{-27}$ s⁻², so $t_f = \sqrt{3\pi/(8.33\times10^{-27})} = 3.36\times10^{13}$ s. Divided by $3.156\times10^{13}$ s per million years, that is 1.066 million years.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The notes' estimate $1/\sqrt{G\rho} = 6.20\times10^{13}$ s, about 1.96 million years, is longer by the factor $1/0.5427$, as part (b) says. On the scale of the Galaxy this is short: the Sun takes 226 million years for one orbit (Problem 1(b)). Real clouds live longer than their free-fall time, held up by the motions of their gas, as the Jeans criterion of Lecture 7 describes.

</Step>
