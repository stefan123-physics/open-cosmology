The 8.286 pages showed that the cosmic microwave background has an almost perfect black-body spectrum and is the same in every direction to high accuracy. "Almost" is the interesting part. This page follows the 8.902 Lecture 17 notes, which open Part III of the course (the CMB, nucleosynthesis and the thermal history of the universe) with the tiny temperature differences across the sky: where they come from and how they are measured.

## The basic picture

Until a redshift of about 1000 the photons were tied to matter by **Thomson scattering** off free electrons. When the electrons and nuclei combined into atoms, the photons **decoupled** and have travelled freely ever since. The CMB is therefore a snapshot of the universe at $z \sim 1000$.

By then the dark matter, which feels no radiation pressure, had already started to collect in **potential wells**. The baryons, coupled to the photons, were pulled towards these wells, and the photons carry the imprint as small temperature differences:

- observed: $\delta T/T \sim 10^{-5}$;
- the notes' comparison: without dark matter we would expect $\delta T/T \sim 10^{-3}$.

The comparison connects to the earlier page on why cold dark matter is needed: if the baryons had to grow all of today's structure on their own after decoupling, they would have needed ripples about a hundred times larger than the ones we see. The differences across the sky are called **anisotropies**, and the notes sort them into two groups.

## Primary anisotropies

Primary anisotropies were set at decoupling, by conditions at the last scattering surface itself. From large scales to small:

- **The Sachs–Wolfe effect.** A photon that leaves a dark matter potential well loses energy climbing out (**gravitational redshift**), so it looks colder. But the well also slows the local clock (**gravitational time delay**): the region is seen at a slightly earlier time, when the universe was hotter. The notes stress that the two effects always come together; their combination is the Sachs–Wolfe effect.
- **The Doppler effect**, from the peculiar motion of the electrons that scattered the photons last.
- **On scales larger than the horizon** at recombination, nothing but gravity has had time to act. The baryons simply follow the dark matter, and the notes describe higher temperatures in the dark matter wells. (What we see from here is the combination with the Sachs–Wolfe effect: in the standard calculation, not in the notes, the climb out of the well wins, so on these large scales the wells appear as slightly cold spots, with δT/T = Φ/3c² for a well of depth Φ.)
- **On scales smaller than the horizon**, the baryons feel the photons' **radiation pressure**. Gravity pulls the fluid into a well, pressure pushes it back out, and the fluid oscillates: **baryon acoustic oscillations** (BAO).
- **On the very smallest scales** the coupling between photons and electrons is not perfect. Photons random-walk out of small hot and cold patches and mix them: fluctuations smaller than about 5 arcminutes are smeared out. This is **Silk damping**.

## Secondary anisotropies

Secondary anisotropies are picked up on the long way from the last scattering surface to us:

- **Thomson scattering after reionization.** The first stars, galaxies and quasars reionized the gas, and the freed electrons scatter some CMB photons again. The scattering is the same in all directions, so it blurs the pattern and lowers the anisotropies overall. [The notes say the universe was reionized "between z ∼ 1000 and z ∼ 6"; reionization by the first sources happened much later than z ∼ 1000. In the standard picture it was under way by z ∼ 10 and complete by z ∼ 6, and the scattering applies to the photons' journey after that.]
- **The integrated Sachs–Wolfe effect.** On the way, photons cross wells and hills of structures that are still forming. If a well changes while the photon is inside, the energy gained falling in and lost climbing out no longer cancel.
- **Gravitational lensing** by structures along the line of sight bends the photons' paths and distorts the pattern slightly.
- **The Sunyaev–Zel'dovich (SZ) effect.** CMB photons that pass through the hot gas of a galaxy cluster are Thomson-scattered by fast electrons, which give them energy on average. The spectrum shifts towards higher frequencies: less intensity at low frequencies, more at high ones. (The notes say the "hot intergalactic medium of galaxies"; the effect is strongest for the hot gas in clusters.)

## Measuring the ripples: the angular power spectrum

From here the notes concentrate on the primary anisotropies, where three effects dominate:

- large scales: the Sachs–Wolfe and Doppler effects roughly compensate each other, and the photons show an imprint of the dark matter distribution;
- smaller scales: the acoustic oscillations of the photon–baryon plasma;
- the smallest scales: Silk damping.

To quantify a pattern on a sphere, the notes expand it in **spherical harmonics** $Y_\ell^m$, the sphere's analogue of the plane waves used for the 3D density field on the power-spectrum page:

<Formula label="Spherical harmonic expansion of the sky" tex="T(\vec\theta) = \sum_{\ell, m} a_{\ell m}\,Y_\ell^m(\vec\theta), \qquad \vec\theta = (\theta, \varphi)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (459), p. 101" />

(In practice the expansion is applied to the fluctuation $\delta T/T$ about the mean.) Because the $Y_\ell^m$ are orthonormal on the sphere,

<Formula label="Orthonormality of spherical harmonics" tex="\int_0^{2\pi} d\varphi \int_0^{\pi} d\theta\,\sin\theta\,Y_{\ell_1}^{m_1 *}(\theta, \varphi)\,Y_{\ell_2}^{m_2}(\theta, \varphi) = \delta_{\ell_1\ell_2}\,\delta_{m_1 m_2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (461), p. 101" />

each coefficient can be picked out by multiplying by $Y_\ell^{m*}$ and integrating over the sky:

<Formula label="Coefficients of the expansion" tex="a_{\ell m} = \int_0^{2\pi} d\varphi \int_0^{\pi} d\theta\,\sin\theta\,T(\theta, \varphi)\,Y_\ell^{m *}(\theta, \varphi)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (460), p. 101" />

[In the notes' eq. (460) the harmonic carries a stray subscript 1 on its degree and a different letter for the second angle; both are slips.] The notes write the degree as a plain italic l; this page uses $\ell$, as most CMB papers do.

For a statistically isotropic sky no direction is special, so all $2\ell + 1$ values of $m$ carry the same power on average. The **angular power spectrum** is that average:

<Formula label="Angular power spectrum" tex="C_\ell = \left\langle |a_{\ell m}|^2 \right\rangle" cite="reading1" at="eq. (462), p. 101" />

Large $\ell$ means small angles. A harmonic of degree $\ell$ has hot and cold patches about $\pi/\ell$ radians across:

<Formula label="Angular scale of a multipole" tex="\theta \sim \frac{\pi}{\ell} = \frac{180^\circ}{\ell}" cite="reading1" at="p. 101" />

One usually plots $\ell(\ell + 1)C_\ell$, which the notes call the amplitude of the fluctuations on that angular scale. Two multipoles have their own names:

- $\ell = 1$, the **dipole**: the sky is slightly hotter in the direction we are moving and cooler behind, a Doppler effect of our own motion relative to the CMB (the notes say the Earth's; most of it is the Sun's motion through the Galaxy and the Galaxy's own motion);
- $\ell = 2$, the **quadrupole**.

## Large scales: the plateau

The horizon at recombination sets a characteristic angle on the sky. The notes give

<Formula label="Angle of the horizon at recombination" tex="\varphi_{\text{horizon,rec}} \approx 1.7^\circ\,\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (463), p. 101" />

so that for a flat universe made of matter only ($\Omega_{m,0} = 1$) the angle is about $1.7^\circ$. [The notes just say "flat universe"; the formula refers to matter-dominated models in which $\Omega_{m,0}$ alone sets the geometry.] On angles much larger than this no region had yet been in causal contact, so no pressure could act. The Sachs–Wolfe and Doppler effects dominate, there are no acoustic oscillations, and $C_\ell$ reflects the primordial matter power spectrum directly. For the Harrison–Zel'dovich spectrum $P(k) \propto k$ the result is that $\ell(\ell + 1)C_\ell$ is roughly **constant** for $\ell \ll 180^\circ/1.7^\circ \approx 100$. That flat part of the spectrum is the **Sachs–Wolfe plateau**.

## Small scales: the acoustic peaks

On angles much smaller than $1.7^\circ$, physical processes have had time to act. The photon–baryon fluid behaves like a gas whose pressure is mostly that of radiation, with sound speed about $c/\sqrt3$. The largest wave that could fall into a dark matter well and complete **half an oscillation**, a single compression, before recombination has a wavelength of about the distance sound travelled by then:

<Formula label="Sound speed and the largest compressed wavelength" tex="c_s \approx \frac{c}{\sqrt3}, \qquad \lambda_\text{max} = t_\text{rec}\,c_s = t_\text{rec}\,\frac{c}{\sqrt3}" cite="reading1" at="p. 102 (c_s in the sentence before eq. 464; λ_max is eq. 464)" />

This **sound horizon** is $1/\sqrt3$ of the horizon, so its angle on the sky is

<Formula label="Angle and multipole of the first peak" tex="\theta_1 \approx \frac{1.7^\circ}{\sqrt3} \sim 1^\circ, \qquad \ell_1 \approx 200" cite="reading1" at="eq. (465), p. 102" />

At that scale the baryons are maximally compressed in the wells at the moment the photons escape, and the adiabatic compression together with the Doppler effect leaves a strong temperature signal: the **first acoustic peak** of $\ell(\ell + 1)C_\ell$.

Smaller waves oscillate faster. A wave that completes **one full oscillation** by recombination (compression, then rarefaction) is caught at maximum rarefaction: the second peak. One and a half oscillations give the third, and so on. The peaks sit where the oscillation is at a **turning point** (maximum compression or maximum rarefaction) at recombination, since that is where the temperature difference is largest. In this simple picture the $n$-th peak is at roughly $n$ times the first peak's $\ell$.

Beyond a few arcminutes Silk damping wins and the peaks die away. The notes' sketch shows the whole shape: the plateau, the first peak at about $1^\circ$ ($\ell \approx 200$), smaller peaks continuing to about $0.2^\circ$ ($\ell \approx 1000$), and damping beyond.

<WorkedExample title="Checking the angles">
<Problem>
In a flat, matter-only universe the comoving distance to redshift $z$ is $(2c/H_0)(1 - 1/\sqrt{1+z})$, and the comoving horizon at $z$ is $(2c/H_0)/\sqrt{1+z}$. (a) What angle does the horizon at $z = 1000$ subtend today? (b) What angle and multipole does the sound horizon give? (c) Which multipole corresponds to the Silk damping scale of 5 arcminutes?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The horizon">
The angle is the ratio of the two comoving lengths: $\theta = 1/(\sqrt{1+z} - 1) = 1/(31.64 - 1) = 0.0326$ rad, which is $1.87^\circ$. This is close to the notes' $1.7^\circ$; the simple model ignores the radiation era, which makes the real horizon at recombination somewhat smaller.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The sound horizon">
Dividing by $\sqrt3$: $1.87^\circ/\sqrt3 = 1.08^\circ$, and $\ell \approx 180^\circ/1.08^\circ \approx 170$. That is the same rough size as the notes' estimate $\ell_1 \approx 200$. (Planck measures the first peak at $\ell \approx 220$; getting it right needs the full calculation with radiation, dark energy and the exact sound speed.)
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Silk damping">
5 arcminutes is $5/60 = 0.083^\circ$, so $\ell \approx 180^\circ/0.083^\circ \approx 2160$. Damping already starts to bite at lower $\ell$, which is why the notes' sketch shows the peaks shrinking from about $\ell \approx 1000$ onwards.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="note" title="The measured spectrum">
The 8.902 Lecture 18 notes reproduce the Planck satellite's 2018 temperature power spectrum (Planck 2018 V, Fig. 57). It shows exactly these features: a low plateau at small $\ell$, a tall first peak near $\ell \approx 220$, a series of smaller peaks, and the decline at large $\ell$, measured out to $\ell \approx 2500$.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The CMB shows the universe at decoupling, $z \sim 1000$. Its temperature ripples, $\delta T/T \sim 10^{-5}$, are smaller than the $\sim 10^{-3}$ that baryons alone would need, because dark matter wells had already started to grow.
- **Primary anisotropies** come from the last scattering surface: Sachs–Wolfe and Doppler effects on large scales, baryon acoustic oscillations inside the horizon, Silk damping on the smallest scales. **Secondary anisotropies** arise on the way to us: scattering after reionization, the integrated Sachs–Wolfe effect, lensing and the SZ effect.
- The sky is expanded in spherical harmonics, $T = \sum a_{\ell m}Y_\ell^m$, and the **angular power spectrum** is $C_\ell = \langle|a_{\ell m}|^2\rangle$, at angular scale $\theta \sim 180^\circ/\ell$.
- Above the horizon at recombination ($\approx 1.7^\circ$, $\ell \ll 100$), $\ell(\ell+1)C_\ell$ is flat for a Harrison–Zel'dovich spectrum.
- Sound at $c_s \approx c/\sqrt3$ sets the **first acoustic peak** at about $1^\circ$, $\ell \approx 200$; further peaks follow at turning points of the oscillation, until Silk damping takes over.
