So far 8.902 has treated stars as point masses moving in a potential. But stars are born, shine and die, and they change the gas they form from. Lecture 8 (sections 3.E and 3.F of the notes) covers both sides: **stellar population synthesis**, which explains a galaxy's light, and **chemical evolution**, which follows how its heavy elements build up. (The notes end with the G-dwarf problem, which the next page covers together with Lecture 9.)

## Star formation rate and initial mass function

A galaxy's spectrum is the sum of its stars' spectra. To add them up we need three ingredients:

- the **star formation rate** (SFR) $\psi(t)$: how much stellar mass forms per unit time;
- the **initial mass function** (IMF) $\phi(m)$: the mass spectrum of newly born stars;
- the light $F_\lambda$ each star emits at each wavelength.

Comparing the summed spectrum with observations constrains both the IMF and the star formation history. The star formation rate is usually given in solar masses per year; the Milky Way forms about $3\,M_\odot$/yr.

<Formula label="Star formation rate" tex="\psi(t) = \frac{dm}{dt}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (218), p. 48" />

[The notes print the left side without the $\psi$, and drop it in the same way in eq. (222) and in their list of variables on p. 52; eq. (253) also writes Z(s) for Z(t).] It is measured from radiation that only young, massive stars produce, with empirical calibrations:

<Formula label="Star formation rate indicators" tex="\frac{\text{SFR}_\text{FIR}}{M_\odot/\text{yr}} \sim \frac{L_\text{FIR}}{5.8\times10^9\,L_\odot}, \qquad \frac{\text{SFR}_{\text{H}\alpha}}{M_\odot/\text{yr}} \sim \frac{L_{\text{H}\alpha}}{1.3\times10^{41}}, \qquad \frac{\text{SFR}_\text{UV}}{M_\odot/\text{yr}} \sim \frac{L_\text{UV}}{7.2\times10^{27}}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (219)–(221), p. 48" />

from **far-infrared** emission of dust heated by young stars, from **Hα** emission of the ionized (HII) regions around them (with the Hα luminosity in erg/s), and from their **ultraviolet** light (the notes write erg/s; the UV calibration is in fact per unit frequency, erg/s/Hz). A simple model history is an exponential decline,

<Formula label="Exponential star formation history" tex="\psi(t) \propto e^{-t/\tau}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (222), p. 48" />

Physically, stars form where gas is cold and dense enough to collapse (the previous page), and the rate is roughly the gas mass divided by the free-fall time. The notes show, from simulations and observations (figures not reproduced here), that only a small fraction of the gas is in this cold, condensed phase; that star formation follows mainly the **molecular** gas, whose fraction depends on density, metallicity and clumping; and that the cosmic star formation rate, summed over all galaxies, rose from early times, **peaked around $z \approx 2$** and has declined since.

The **initial mass function** $\phi(m)\,dm$ is the relative number of stars born with masses between $m$ and $m + dm$, normalized per solar mass of stars formed:

<Formula label="IMF normalization" tex="\int_{m_l}^{m_h}m\,\phi(m)\,dm = 1\,M_\odot" cite="reading1" at="eq. (223), p. 50" />

with $m_l \approx 0.08\,M_\odot$ (below which hydrogen cannot fuse) and $m_h \approx 100\,M_\odot$ (above which radiation pressure, the Eddington limit, prevents stars from forming). If $M_*$ of stars forms, the number and mass born in $(m, m + dm)$ are

<Formula label="Numbers and masses of newborn stars" tex="dN(m) = \frac{M_*}{M_\odot}\,\phi(m)\,dm, \qquad dM(m) = \frac{M_*}{M_\odot}\,m\,\phi(m)\,dm" cite="reading1" at="eq. (224), p. 50" />

The IMF is often taken to be **Salpeter's** power law:

<Formula label="Salpeter IMF" tex="\phi(m) \propto m^{-(1+x)}, \qquad x = 1.35" cite="reading1" at="eq. (225), p. 50" />

Other forms (Chabrier, Kroupa) flatten out below about one solar mass. The IMF is uncertain, because it has to be inferred from luminosities and from the **present-day** mass function, from which the massive stars have already disappeared.

## Stellar lifetimes

A star's spectrum depends on its luminosity, effective temperature and composition, and its path through the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram depends only on its initial mass and metallicity. The Stefan–Boltzmann law $L = 4\pi R^2\sigma T_\text{eff}^4$ makes lines of constant radius straight lines in that diagram:

<Formula label="Stefan–Boltzmann law in logarithms" tex="\log L = 2\log R + 4\log T + \text{constant}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (226), p. 50" />

[The notes add that the main sequence lies along the line $R = 1$; in fact main-sequence stars have different radii, from about 0.1 to 10 solar radii, so the main sequence is not a line of constant radius.] As stars age they leave the main sequence and become giants and then remnants. The time they spend on it is

<Formula label="Main-sequence lifetime" tex="\tau_\text{MS} \propto M^{-3}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (227), p. 51" />

because the lifetime is the fuel supply over the burning rate, and the luminosity rises steeply with mass:

<Formula label="Lifetime from fuel and burning rate" tex="\tau_\text{MS} \propto \frac{M}{L}, \qquad L \propto M^4 \;\Rightarrow\; \tau_\text{MS} \propto \frac{M}{M^4} = M^{-3}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (228)–(229), p. 51" />

For low-mass stars ($M < 0.7\,M_\odot$), $L \propto M^3$ and $\tau_\text{MS} \propto M^{-2}$.

<WorkedExample title="Who is born, and who survives?">
<Problem>
(a) With $\tau_\text{MS} \approx 10^{10}$ years for the Sun and $\tau \propto M^{-3}$, how long does a 10 $M_\odot$ star live? (b) For a Salpeter IMF from 0.1 to 100 $M_\odot$, what fraction of newborn stars, and what fraction of the mass, is in stars above 8 $M_\odot$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Lifetimes">
$10^{10} \times 10^{-3} = 10^7$ years. Massive stars are gone within ten million years; stars below about a solar mass outlive the present universe.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Numbers and masses">
Integrating $m^{-2.35}$ for numbers and $m^{-1.35}$ for mass: about 0.3% of the stars, but 14% of the mass, are above 8 $M_\odot$. (A page-own calculation.) These few massive stars produce most of the ultraviolet light and all the core-collapse supernovae.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Population synthesis

A galaxy's spectrum at time $t$ adds up all the stars formed at earlier times $t'$, weighted by the star formation rate then:

<Formula label="Galaxy spectrum" tex="L_\lambda = \int_0^t\mathcal{L}_{\lambda,\text{cp}}\big(t - t', Z(t')\big)\,\psi(t')\,dt'" cite="reading1" at="eq. (230), p. 51" />

Here $\mathcal{L}_{\lambda,\text{cp}}$ is the luminosity per unit stellar mass of a **coeval population** (stars all born together), of age $\tau = t - t'$ and initial metallicity $Z$, itself a sum over the IMF:

<Formula label="Spectrum of a coeval population" tex="\mathcal{L}_{\lambda,\text{cp}}\big(\tau, Z(\tau_0)\big) = \int\mathcal{L}_\lambda\big(m, Z(\tau_0), \tau\big)\,\frac{\phi(m)}{M_\odot}\,dm" cite="reading1" at="eq. (231), p. 51" />

where $\mathcal{L}_\lambda$ is the spectrum of one star of initial mass $m$ at age $\tau$. The galaxy spectrum is thus a convolution of the IMF, the star formation history and stellar spectra. Since $\phi$ and $\psi$ are not known precisely, population-synthesis codes iterate: assume an IMF, impose a star formation history, compute the spectrum, compare with data (spectral features break some degeneracies), adjust and repeat.

## Metallicity and abundances

Heavy elements act as a **clock** for a galaxy's history. At first there are none; stars make them by nucleosynthesis; supernovae eject them into the interstellar medium; new stars incorporate them. The **metallicity** is their mass fraction:

<Formula label="Metallicity" tex="Z = \frac{\text{mass of heavy elements}}{\text{total mass}}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (232), p. 52" />

often quoted relative to the Sun's, $Z_\odot \approx 0.02$. The **abundance** of one element relative to another is given logarithmically, by number, relative to the Sun:

<Formula label="Abundance ratio" tex="\left[\frac XY\right] = \log\left(\frac{n_X/n_Y}{(n_X/n_Y)_\odot}\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (233), p. 52" />

so $[\text{Fe/H}] = 0$ is solar, $-1$ is a tenth of the solar iron abundance, $-2$ a hundredth.

## Modelling chemical evolution

Consider a galaxy (or a region of one) with total mass $M = M_s + M_g$ in stars and gas, gas inflow rate $f$ (with metallicity $Z_f$), outflow rate $e$, star formation rate $\psi$, and rates $E$ and $E_Z$ at which dying stars return gas and metals. Bookkeeping gives

<Formula label="Equations of chemical evolution" tex="M = M_s + M_g, \quad \frac{dM}{dt} = f - e, \quad \frac{dM_s}{dt} = \psi - E, \quad \frac{dM_g}{dt} = -\psi + E + f - e, \quad \frac{d(ZM_g)}{dt} = -Z\psi + E_Z + Z_ff - Ze" cite="reading1" at="eq. (234), p. 52" />

[The notes write the third equation for $dM_g/dt$; it is the stellar mass $M_s$ that grows by $\psi - E$.] The return rates sum over the stars dying at time $t$, those born at $t - \tau_\text{MS}(m)$ with masses above the current main-sequence turnoff mass $m_t$:

<Formula label="Return of gas and metals" tex="E(t) = \int_{m_t}^\infty(m - w_m)\,\psi\big(t - \tau_\text{MS}(m)\big)\,\phi(m)\,dm, \qquad E_Z(t) = \int_{m_t}^\infty\Big[(m - w_m)\,Z\big(t - \tau_\text{MS}(m)\big) + m\rho_{Zm}\Big]\psi\big(t - \tau_\text{MS}(m)\big)\,\phi(m)\,dm" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (235)–(236), p. 53" />

Each star of mass $m$ leaves a remnant $w_m$ and ejects $m - w_m$, carrying both the metals it was born with and the new metals $m\rho_{Zm}$ it made (negative for elements it destroys, such as lithium).

## Instantaneous recycling

To solve this analytically, assume **instantaneous recycling**: stars above some mass $m_\text{lim}$ return their ejecta at once, fully mixed, and lighter stars never return anything. This works because massive stars live so briefly. Then $\psi(t - \tau_\text{MS}) \approx \psi(t)$ (eq. 237) and

<Formula label="Returned mass under instantaneous recycling" tex="E(t) \approx \psi(t)\int_{m_\text{lim}}^\infty(m - w_m)\,\phi(m)\,dm = \psi(t)\,R, \qquad R = \int_{m_\text{lim}}^\infty(m - w_m)\,\phi(m)\,dm" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (238)–(239), p. 53" />

with $R$ the fraction of mass returned per mass of stars formed. Similarly

<Formula label="Returned metals under instantaneous recycling" tex="E_Z(t) \approx \psi(t)Z(t)R + (1 - R)\,y\,\psi(t), \qquad y = \frac{1}{1 - R}\int_{m_\text{lim}}^\infty m\rho_{Zm}\,\phi(m)\,dm" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (240)–(241), pp. 53–54" />

where the **yield** $y$ is the mass of new metals produced per mass locked up in long-lived stars and remnants (the notes say "per remnant mass"). The equations become

<Formula label="Chemical evolution with instantaneous recycling" tex="\frac{dM_s}{dt} = (1 - R)\psi, \qquad \frac{dM_g}{dt} = -(1 - R)\psi + f - e, \qquad \frac{d(ZM_g)}{dt} = (1 - R)(y - Z)\psi + Z_ff - Ze" cite="reading1" at="eq. (242), p. 54" />

and combining the last two,

<Formula label="Metallicity of the gas" tex="M_g\frac{dZ}{dt} = (1 - R)\,y\,\psi + (Z_f - Z)f" cite="reading1" at="eq. (243), p. 54" />

[The notes have an extra term $+Ze$; the outflow carries gas at the current metallicity and so does not change $Z$, and the term cancels.] Metallicity rises through new metals and falls through dilution by metal-poor inflow.

## The closed box

The simplest case is a **closed box**: no inflow or outflow ($f = e = 0$), starting as pure, metal-free gas ($M_g(0) = M$, $Z(0) = 0$). Then

<Formula label="Closed-box equations" tex="\frac{dM_g}{dt} = -(1 - R)\psi, \qquad M_g\frac{dZ}{dt} = (1 - R)\,y\,\psi" cite="reading1" at="eq. (244), p. 54" />

Dividing one by the other eliminates time:

<Formula label="Integrating the closed box" tex="\frac{1}{M_g}\frac{dM_g}{dZ} = -\frac1y \;\Rightarrow\; \ln\left(\frac{M_g(t)}{M}\right) = -\frac{Z(t)}{y} \;\Rightarrow\; Z(t) = y\ln\left(\frac{M}{M_g(t)}\right)" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (245)–(247), pp. 54–55" />

The gas metallicity depends only on how much gas is left. In a closed box, stars and gas together hold all the metals ever made. With instantaneous recycling the metals made so far are $(1 - R)\,y\int\psi\,dt'$, and the mass in stars is $M_s = (1 - R)\int\psi\,dt'$, so (with $Z_s$ the stars' average metallicity)

<Formula label="All metals ever made" tex="Z_sM_s + ZM_g = \int_0^t(1 - R)\,y\,\psi(t')\,dt' = y\,M_s" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (248)–(251), p. 55" />

and dividing by $M_s = M - M_g$ gives $Z_s = y - ZM_g/(M - M_g)$. With the gas fraction $u = M_g/M$:

<Formula label="Closed-box metallicities of gas and stars" tex="\text{gas:}\ Z(t) = y\ln\left(\frac{1}{u(t)}\right), \qquad \text{stars:}\ Z_s(t) = y - Z(t)\,\frac{u(t)}{1 - u(t)}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (252)–(253), p. 55" />

As the gas runs out, $u \to 0$, the mean stellar metallicity approaches the yield: $Z_s \to y$.

<WorkedExample title="A closed-box galaxy">
<Problem>
A galaxy has turned 90% of its gas into stars ($u = 0.1$) with a yield $y = 0.02 \approx Z_\odot$. What are the metallicities of its gas and, on average, of its stars?
</Problem>
<Step title="Gas">
$Z = y\ln(1/u) = 0.02 \times 2.303 = 0.046$, about 2.3 times solar.
</Step>
<Step title="Stars">
$Z_s = y - Z\,u/(1 - u) = 0.02 - 0.046 \times 0.111 = 0.0149$, about 0.74 times solar. Old stars formed early from metal-poor gas pull the average below the yield.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A galaxy's spectrum is the convolution of its star formation history $\psi(t)$, the IMF $\phi(m)$ and stellar spectra. The SFR is measured from far-infrared, Hα and ultraviolet light; the cosmic SFR peaked around $z \approx 2$.
- The IMF, normalized to one solar mass of stars, is roughly Salpeter, $\phi \propto m^{-2.35}$, between about 0.08 and 100 $M_\odot$: few massive stars, but they dominate the ultraviolet and the supernovae.
- Main-sequence lifetimes scale as $\tau_\text{MS} \propto M/L \propto M^{-3}$.
- Metallicity $Z$ (mass fraction of heavy elements, $Z_\odot \approx 0.02$) and abundances $[X/Y]$ trace a galaxy's history. Mass and metal bookkeeping with inflow, outflow, star formation and stellar return gives the equations of chemical evolution.
- With instantaneous recycling (return fraction $R$, yield $y$) and a closed box, the gas reaches $Z = y\ln(1/u)$ and the stars on average $Z_s = y - Zu/(1 - u) \to y$.
